**TL;DR:** Sell one measured result on one process to the person who owns it: the head of operations or the finance lead at a company of 20 to 500 people. Find the first clients through people who already know your work, then add referral partners who see the manual work, outreach to companies whose job posts or public posts show it piling up, and the partner directories of the tools you build on. Close with a paid pilot that has a baseline and a target, because a skeptical buyer can approve a small bet with a countable result.

This playbook covers how to get clients for an AI automation agency that builds workflows, AI steps and integrations for small and mid-size companies. It skips the hype on purpose: the buyers who pay are operations people, and what they buy is fewer hours of copy-paste.

---

## Who signs for automation work

The budget sits with the person who feels the manual work: a COO or head of operations, a controller or finance director, a RevOps or customer operations lead. At a company of 20 to 500 people, that person can sign a pilot without a committee, and the work they want gone is easy to count.

Most of these companies are early. In the Census Bureau's [Business Trends and Outlook Survey](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html) period ending May 3, 2026, 19.8% of US businesses said they had used AI in the previous two weeks. Among firms with 100 to 249 employees the figure was 32%, and between December 2025 and May 2026, AI use grew only among firms with at least 20 employees. So the typical buyer has probably seen a few demos, hasn't put AI into a live process yet, and needs a first step that can't embarrass them in front of the owner.

Two groups fit less well. Local businesses such as dental practices and contractors buy automation too, but they rarely post jobs or write about their processes in public, so reach them through local referrals, the software vendors they use and their trade associations. And if you sell AI strategy more than builds, the [consulting client playbook](/blog/how-to-get-consulting-clients) fits your offer better than this one.

## Sell a process, not a model

An offer an operations leader can approve names three things: the process, the number it moves, and the price of finding out.

- **The process.** "Invoice intake from email into the accounting system." "New-client onboarding documents." "Quote requests into the CRM." Never "AI agents for your business."
- **The number.** Hours per week, error rate, days to close the month, size of the backlog. Pick one the buyer already tracks or can count in two weeks.
- **The price shape.** A fixed-price pilot on one workflow, then a monthly fee for monitoring, fixes and the next workflow. Hourly billing for undefined "AI work" is hard for a buyer to approve.

Say a 90-person freight brokerage has two coordinators keying rate confirmations from PDFs into its transport management system. The pilot: extract the fields, have a coordinator approve each record, and measure minutes per document before and after. The scope is small, the risk is visible, and the result is a number the COO can repeat to the owner.

## Warm channels: your old industry and the partners who see the mess

**Former colleagues and your old industry.** Your first clients usually come from people who already trust you, and your edge is the industry you came from. A former insurance operations analyst knows where brokerages lose hours. A former accountant knows what month-end close looks like from the inside. Write to twenty people from that world with one named process you automate and a fixed pilot price, and ask each one who else has the problem.

**Partners who see the manual work first.** Some firms see a client's processes up close and don't build automations:

- **Accountants and fractional CFOs** see the spreadsheets behind every close.
- **Managed IT providers** get the "can we connect these two apps?" requests.
- **CRM and ERP consultants** finish an implementation and leave a list of manual workarounds behind.
- **Software development shops** turn down workflow jobs too small for a build team; [how software development companies get clients](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-software-development-company) shows where their pipeline and yours meet.

Offer a referral fee or a share of the first year, and make the partner look good: they introduce you, you fix the problem they couldn't, and the client thanks them for it.

## Reading the signs of manual work

Manual work leaves public traces, and three of them point at a company that would pay to stop doing it:

- **Hiring for admin and operations roles.** A post for a data entry clerk, an accounts payable clerk, an administrative assistant or an operations coordinator means the volume outgrew the team. Don't pitch the automation as a replacement for the hire. Ask about the volume and offer to take the repetitive part off the new person's desk. An automation specialist or operations analyst opening is the other end of the same signal: the company has decided to automate and is short of builders, so offer to ship the first workflows while they recruit.
- **Zapier or n8n named in job posts.** The company already automates and has someone maintaining it, which often means brittle workflows and a queue of requests. Offer a rebuild, monitoring, or the AI steps their current setup can't do. Make is harder to search for in posts, because the word is so common; look for "Make.com" or "Integromat" instead.
- **Posts about manual processes.** Operations and finance leaders write about month-end close, onboarding checklists and spreadsheet sprawl. One post names the process and the person who owns it.

The first message asks about the process and offers nothing yet: "Saw the AP clerk opening. Is the team still keying invoices by hand, or is part of that automated already?" The [guide to job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) covers reading posts at volume. You can run the same searches by role with [companies hiring for a role](/plays/companies-hiring) and by tool with [companies whose job posts name a tool](/plays/tech-stack); they are the core of [Pancake for AI automation agencies](/for/ai-automation-agencies).

## The Zapier and n8n partner programs

If you build on one tool, its partner directory puts you in front of buyers who already chose it. Both programs are selective, so treat them as a second step. As of November 2026:

- **Zapier.** Its [partner programs](https://zapier.com/l/partners) are free to join. The Solution Partner Program is for consulting, implementation and managed services firms, and partners move up tiers starting at Certified Zapier Expert. Zapier says it answers applications within 21 business days, and customers find partners through its "Find a Solutions Partner" directory.
- **n8n.** The [Expert Partner Program](https://n8n.io/expert-partners/) runs as a closed pilot. n8n looks for agencies that already deliver n8n work, offer automation and AI as core services, have at least three active n8n customers, work in the UK, Ireland, Northern Europe or North America, and take part in the n8n community. It reviews applications and admits partners as spots open. Its [service partner directory](https://experts.n8n.io/) listed 48 partners in early November 2026, tiered Select, Premium and Enterprise, and buyers filter it by services, budget, region and language.

A small directory means less competition and fewer buyers. Treat a listing as an extra source of qualified conversations, not a replacement for channels 1 to 3.

## Teardowns, communities and marketplaces

These three build a name more than a pipeline:

- **Public teardowns.** Write up each finished project as the process, the before and after numbers, and what broke along the way. An operations leader comparing agencies trusts a write-up with an error rate more than a demo video.
- **Communities.** The n8n and Zapier community forums are full of builders. Answer the hard questions well, and other builders send you overflow and the clients they can't take. Buyers are rarer there.
- **Marketplaces.** Upwork carries plenty of automation jobs, most of them small and price-shopped. Use it for a first review or a new skill, not for the pilot with a measured target.

## The pilot that turns a skeptic into a client

Most automation deals are won or lost here. Before signing a contract, an operations leader needs to see seven things, in this order:

1. **A baseline.** Two weeks of counts on the current process: volume, minutes per item, error rate. Without it, nobody can prove the pilot worked.
2. **One workflow, written down.** Inputs, outputs, the systems touched, and what is out of scope.
3. **A target agreed in writing.** For example, "cut entry time from 12 minutes per document to 3, with fewer than 1 error in 100 records." The buyer owns the number.
4. **A human check where the AI decides.** Extraction and classification steps get a review queue until the error rate earns trust.
5. **Failure handling.** Logs, alerts, and a named person who fixes it when an upstream app changes its fields.
6. **Data handling.** Which accounts you need, where data goes, which model provider processes it, and answers to their security questionnaire before they ask.
7. **A readout.** At 30 days: the numbers against the baseline, the monthly cost to run, and the next workflow.

Price the pilot as a fixed fee and credit part of it toward the retainer if they continue. Don't promise a percentage of time saved before the baseline exists. And keep "replace your team" out of every conversation: the buyer usually manages that team.
