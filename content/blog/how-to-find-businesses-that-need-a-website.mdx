## How to find businesses that need a website: the two halves

The method depends on the half. Local businesses with no site, or a broken one, are found by hand, one listing at a time. B2B companies due for a redesign show it in public, in job posts, posts and news.

The two halves differ in more than method. Local prospects are plentiful, the owner decides alone, and budgets come out of the owner's pocket. B2B prospects are fewer, the head of marketing owns the site, and the budget sits in a marketing plan. If you sell to both, write two offers and two first messages.

## How to find local businesses that don't have a website

Search Google Maps for one trade in one town, open every listing, and log the ones with no Website button or a link that goes nowhere useful.

1. **Pick one trade and one town.** Search Maps for "[trade] in [town]", for example "physical therapy Boise". One trade per session keeps your notes comparable.
2. **Open each listing.** No Website button means the owner never added a site to their Business Profile.
3. **Click the links that exist.** A social page, a directory page, a parked domain or an error page all count as no site.
4. **Note the review count.** An owner with 150 reviews and no site has customers and cash flow. An owner with three reviews from 2021 may have neither.
5. **Widen with directories.** Chamber of commerce member lists, trade association directories and Yelp categories surface businesses that Maps ranks low.
6. **Catch new businesses.** Many cities publish business registrations as open data. San Francisco's [Registered Business Locations dataset](https://data.sf.gov/Economy-and-Community/Registered-Business-Locations-San-Francisco/g8m3-pdis) lists each registered business with a Business Start Date column, so you can filter for businesses that started this month.

Log the name, the phone number, what is wrong and a screenshot. Then call or walk in. A local owner is easier to reach at the counter than in an inbox.

## How to check a website in five minutes

Five checks tell you whether a site works against its owner, and each one hands you a line for your first message.

1. **The address bar.** Chrome shows "Not secure" when a site doesn't use a private connection, and [Chrome Help](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95617) says the owner fixes it with HTTPS.
2. **Your phone.** Open the site on a phone. Tap the phone number and the booking button.
3. **Speed.** Run the homepage through [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/), which reports on mobile and desktop.
4. **Links.** Click the main menu, the contact page and the footer links.
5. **Freshness.** Read the copyright year in the footer, the date of the last blog post, and the team page.

Write down what you saw, with a screenshot. "Your booking button opens a blank page on iPhone" starts a conversation. "Your site looks dated" ends one.

## Signs a company needs a website redesign

A B2B company needs a redesign when its site falls behind the company: new people, new money, new products, a new name. Each of those changes shows up in public before the project goes out to studios.

- **Hiring for the site.** Search job boards and careers pages for "web designer", "Webflow developer", "website manager" and "web developer". A company [hiring for a web role](/plays/companies-hiring) has a budget and a deadline. Some will build in-house; pitch the rebuild while they hire, or support for the new hire.
- **A CMS named in job posts.** A line such as "experience migrating from WordPress to Webflow" reveals the move before anyone posts about it. Job posts are where you see the [tools a company plans to use](/plays/tech-stack).
- **Redesign and rebrand posts.** Search public posts for "website redesign", "new website", "rebrand" and "site migration" on the platforms your buyers use. Founders and marketers post updates such as "we're rebuilding the site", and some ask peers which studio to hire.
- **A new marketing leader.** Watch press releases and announcement posts. A new head of marketing tends to review the site and the messaging in the first months.
- **Funding.** Press releases and funding news cover the rounds companies announce. For rounds raised in the US under Regulation D, [Form D filings](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/capital-raising-building-blocks/what-form-d) are public on EDGAR and due within 15 days after the first sale of securities. More on [selling to recently funded companies](/plays/recently-funded-startups).
- **Launches and new markets.** A new product line needs pages; a new country needs a language. Read product announcements and job posts for regional roles.
- **Mergers and name changes.** These come with a date, and the site has to be ready by it.

## What doesn't count as a signal

Four things look like signals and aren't: the CMS alone, your taste, a site rebuilt last year, and a business too small to pay.

- **The CMS alone.** Running WordPress doesn't mean a company wants to leave it. A planned move shows up in job posts and posts, not in the page source.
- **Your taste.** "It looks dated" is your opinion. Find a problem the owner would agree with: a form that fails, a page that takes eight seconds on mobile, a product the site doesn't mention.
- **A recent rebuild.** Check the footer, the press page and old job posts. A company that relaunched in the spring won't rebuild in the fall.
- **No budget.** A side business with three reviews and no staff is a poor first project, however bad its site.

Once a business passes, write to it about the signal you saw. The [web design cold outreach templates](/blog/web-design-cold-outreach) include first messages for a job post, a redesign post, a funding round and a broken page.
