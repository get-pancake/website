**TL;DR:** Sell on a schedule, not in leftover time. That is how to get clients as a solopreneur when client work fills the week: two fixed 90-minute sessions, kept even in busy months. The first goes to people who already trust you and to people showing a need right now. The second goes to follow-ups and one piece of public proof. When you are booked, the sessions change job instead of disappearing, so the next project is already in conversation when this one ends.

---

## Why selling stalls when you work alone

Client work always feels more urgent than selling, so a one-person business sells in bursts: hard between projects, not at all during them. The pipeline empties while you deliver, and the gap shows up the week the project ends. The fix is unglamorous. Put selling on the calendar as two recurring appointments with yourself, and treat them like client meetings.

Working alone is not the handicap it can feel like. Nearly four in five US businesses have no paid employees, according to [Census Bureau data for 2023](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2025/07/nonemployer-business-growth.html). And a one-person firm sells something an agency can't: the person who scopes the work is the person who does it.

## Pick one buyer and one first offer

Three hours a week can't feed five audiences. Choose one buyer, meaning a role at a type of company, and one first offer with a fixed scope, a fixed price and a short timeline. A small, defined first project is easier to approve than an open-ended engagement, and it gives you a concrete reason to write to people.

A useful template: a result, for a role, at a type of company, in a set time, for a set price. Three hypothetical examples:

- A solo RevOps consultant: a two-week CRM cleanup for B2B software teams of 20 to 80 people, at a fixed price.
- A freelance copywriter: a homepage and pricing-page rewrite for seed-stage SaaS companies, delivered in ten days.
- A fractional controller: a month-end close process set up in 30 days for agencies with 15 to 60 staff.

Each of these tells a buyer what they get and tells you who to look for. That second part is what makes the weekly sessions work.

## How to get clients as a solopreneur: the twice-weekly system

Two sessions, two halves each. Pick the days that suit your delivery rhythm; what matters is that they repeat.

| Session | Time | What you do | What you count |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tuesday, first half | 45 minutes | Five personal notes to past clients, former colleagues and partners, each with one specific ask | Replies, names referred |
| Tuesday, second half | 45 minutes | Ten short messages to people showing a need, one question each | Conversations started |
| Thursday, first half | 45 minutes | Answer every open thread, send what you promised, turn good calls into proposals | Calls booked, proposals sent |
| Thursday, second half | 45 minutes | One piece of proof, then ten minutes on the scorecard | One piece shipped |

The scorecard is three numbers a week: conversations started, calls booked, proposals sent. After eight weeks, look at which half of Tuesday produced your calls, and give that half more of the time.

---

## Tuesday, first half: people who already trust you

Warm contacts answer first because the trust is already there. Write down everyone who has paid you, hired you or worked next to you in the last five years, plus people who serve the same buyer you do. That list is your first market. Keep it in a spreadsheet, not in your head.

Five notes a session, each with something true and one specific ask:

- To a past client: "It's been six months since we rebuilt your onboarding emails. Are the numbers holding?" A good answer can open the next project.
- To a former colleague: "Do you know a head of sales at a 30-person software company who's fighting with their CRM?" That gets a name. "Let me know if you hear of anything" gets a polite yes and nothing else.

Then build a small referral circle. Three partners who serve your buyer with a different skill are worth more than a long list of loose contacts: a brand designer and a copywriter, a fractional CFO and a bookkeeper, a RevOps consultant and a sales trainer. Each sees the other's work before the client asks for it. Agree on what each of you takes and refuses, and send work both ways. [How to get consulting clients](/blog/how-to-get-consulting-clients) goes further into partners who vouch for you.

Your current client counts too. Propose the next phase two weeks before this one ends, while the results are fresh.

## Tuesday, second half: strangers with a reason to talk

When the warm list thins, write to people you don't know, but only with a reason. The reasons are public:

- a company hiring the role you could cover part-time, such as a 15-person startup posting for a marketing manager, which might take a fractional marketer instead;
- a founder or team lead posting about the problem you fix;
- people commenting under the experts or bigger firms your buyers follow;
- a startup that announced a round and is now hiring.

[The full list of buying signals](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) shows where to see each one, and [lead generation for consultants](/blog/lead-generation-for-consultants) goes deeper on the triggers behind consulting work.

Send ten messages, one question each, no pitch. Say you are a freelance product designer, and a seed-stage founder posts that new users stall at the setup screen. "You mentioned new users stall at setup. Do you think it's the design, or the product asking for too much too early?" is easy to answer, and it shows you read the post.

If your messages go by email, CAN-SPAM covers them even when the buyer is a business. Under [the FTC's compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business), every commercial email has to carry your valid postal address and a clear opt-out, and you have 10 business days to act on an opt-out request.

## Thursday: follow-ups, and proof without a daily posting habit

Follow-ups are the first thing a busy solo skips. Give them the first half of Thursday: answer every open thread, send what you promised, and move each good call to a written proposal with a date on it. A follow-up should add something, such as a relevant example or a sharper question, never only a nudge.

The second half goes to proof, and you don't need a daily posting habit for it. You need one piece a week that shows how you think:

- a short case note: the problem, what you changed, what happened;
- a teardown of a public example in your niche;
- the answer to a question buyers keep asking you.

Publish it where your buyers read, then reuse it. The same note becomes a comment in a community thread and a link in next week's follow-ups. A guest spot on a niche newsletter or podcast puts you in front of an audience someone else spent years building, and one a quarter is a pace a solo can keep.

## When the calendar is full, or empty

The sessions change job with your capacity. They never drop to zero.

- **Fully booked.** Keep both sessions, but stop cold outreach. Spend the time on warm contacts, partners and proof, raise your price for new work, and keep a short waitlist with real start dates.
- **Running thin.** Add a third 90-minute session for a month, all of it outreach on fresh signals, and tell every partner you have room.

A booking page protects the hours in between. [Google Calendar's appointment schedules](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/10729749?hl=en) work from a personal Google account, and the settings let you add buffer time between appointments and cap the number of bookings per day. Offer calls on two afternoons only, capped at two a day, so a good week of replies doesn't swallow your delivery time.

## Answering the one-person objection

Larger buyers sometimes ask what happens if you get sick, go on vacation or take on too much. Answer it in the proposal before they ask:

- **Name a backup.** A partner you have worked with, who knows your methods and can step in for a week.
- **Write down how you work.** Response times, the weekly check-in, where files and passwords live, what happens at handover.
- **Keep the first scope short.** A fixed two- to six-week project carries little risk for the buyer, whatever your headcount.

Handled this way, the objection turns into the pitch: one accountable person, with a plan for the bad week.

## Channels that cost a solopreneur too much time

Some channels work, but they charge more hours than a one-person business can spare early on:

- **Marketplaces as the base.** Upwork takes a service fee of 0% to 15% on each contract, and you see the rate before you bid, [according to Upwork's help center](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/211062538-Learn-about-the-Freelancer-Service-Fee) as of November 2026. Every proposal takes time and sits next to cheaper bids. Use a marketplace to fill a gap or earn first reviews, not as the base.
- **Paid ads for a considered B2B service.** Hard to justify before you know what a client is worth to you.
- **Big conferences without a list.** Go with ten names you want to meet, or stay home and send ten messages.
- **A large website before your third client.** One page with the offer, one case note and a booking link is enough to start.
