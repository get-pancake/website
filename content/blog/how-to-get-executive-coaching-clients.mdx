**TL;DR:** Executive coaching has two buyers. A leader who chooses their own coach hires someone they were referred to or have watched think. A sponsor in HR, L&D or the CEO's office, buying coaching for others, hires the coach who is easiest to approve: credentialed, with a clear process and a way to show progress. Work your referrals first, then the people who sit next to new leaders (search firms, investors), then reach leaders directly at the moment their job gets bigger.

Below is how to get executive coaching clients from each buyer: nine channels ranked in one table, the moments that send leaders looking for a coach, and how to market a practice without a large audience.

---

## The leader and the sponsor buy differently

Most coaching sales go to one of two people, and the same pitch rarely works on both.

**The leader.** A founder-CEO, a newly promoted executive, a VP who wants to grow into the next job. They choose the coach, sometimes pay from their own budget, and decide on chemistry and credibility after one or two calls. They want to know you have sat with people like them, and that you won't waste their time.

**The sponsor.** A Chief People Officer, a head of learning and development, or a CEO buying coaching for their leadership team. They buy a process: how coaches are matched, what stays confidential, how progress gets reported, what it costs per leader. In larger companies, procurement joins the deal. [Selling coaching to companies](/blog/how-to-sell-coaching-to-companies) walks through that process, from the first brief to the renewal.

At a startup of 50 to 200 people, the founder-CEO is often both: the coachee and the person who signs. That shortens the sale, and it is why [our page for executive coaches](/for/executive-coaches) starts with founders.

The field is crowded. The [ICF's 2025 Global Coaching Study](https://coachingfederation.org/blog/coaching-industry-continues-global-growth-with-5-34-billion-usd-revenue-new-research-reveals/), run by PwC, counted 122,974 coach practitioners worldwide, up 15% since 2023, and 54% of coaches say they specialize in leadership and executive coaching. "Executive coach" alone no longer tells a buyer why you. A moment does: the first year as CEO, the first 90 days in a new executive role, the founder after a large round.

## Moments when leaders look for a coach

Coaching gets bought when a leader's job outgrows them, or when a company fears it will. These are the moments to watch, and where each one shows:

- **A bigger job.** A promotion, a first CEO role, a move from VP to C-level. Announcements, press releases and the leader's own posts about starting the role show it; watch them by hand.
- **A senior hire from outside.** A company hiring a VP of Sales or a Head of Product will need that person productive fast. The sponsor is the CEO or People leader who owns the hire.
- **A fast-growing team.** A company hiring many managers at once has new leaders who have never managed managers.
- **A funding round.** The CEO now answers to a board with new targets, and may need to build an executive team quickly.
- **An integration.** After an acquisition, leaders from two cultures share one org chart.
- **A development plan.** A 360 review, a succession plan, a high-potential program. The sponsor buys, and the brief usually comes from HR.

Each moment has a natural first question. For a new CEO: "What did you stop doing first when the job changed?" For a company hiring a senior leader: "How will you know at six months that the new VP is working out?"

## How to get executive coaching clients, channel by channel

The ranking orders channels by how fast each tends to produce a signed engagement for a coach with a few years of practice. Treat the time column as a rough guide drawn from how each channel works.

| # | Channel | Who pays | Time to first client | The trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Past coachees and their sponsors | Either buyer | 1 to 4 weeks | Limited by how many you have coached |
| 2 | People who have seen you lead | Mostly the leader | 2 to 6 weeks | A finite list |
| 3 | Executive search firms | The company | 1 to 3 months | They protect their placement, so they pick low-risk coaches |
| 4 | Investors' platform teams | The portfolio company | 1 to 3 months | You join a short list, not an exclusive |
| 5 | Leaders at a moment of change, reached directly | The leader or the company | 3 to 8 weeks | Needs a sharp first question |
| 6 | People leaders building a program | The sponsor | 2 to 6 months | Pilots, procurement, reporting |
| 7 | Coaching platforms such as BetterUp and CoachHub | The platform | 1 to 3 months after acceptance | Platform rates; the client is theirs |
| 8 | Associate work for leadership-development firms | The firm | 1 to 3 months | Their brand and their day rate |
| 9 | A point of view in writing and talks | Mostly the leader | 6 to 12 months | Slow to start, then lifts every other channel |

Most practices need three running at once: one referral channel, one direct channel, and one that pays the bills while the others build. A new coach might pick 1, 7 and 5. An established coach moving up to company programs might pick 1, 3 and 6.

## Referral channels: coachees, search firms and investors

### Past coachees and their sponsors (channel 1)

Your best source of executive coaching clients is the last engagement. A coachee who grew will hire you again at the next step up, and talk about you to peers facing the same step. The HR leader who sponsored them can bring the next three leaders.

Ask at the moment of visible change, such as the review where the coachee's manager noticed the difference. Make the ask specific: "Which other leaders in your company are about to take on a bigger team?" Then stay in touch with the sponsor between engagements: they move companies, and they take their list of trusted coaches with them.

### People who have seen you lead (channel 2)

If you led teams before you coached, the people who reported to you, the peers who watched you, and the bosses who promoted you are your early clients and referrers. They don't need convincing that you understand the job. Write to them one at a time with a question about their current role, not an announcement of your practice.

### Executive search firms (channel 3)

A search firm that places a new executive wants the placement to last. That makes support in the first months an easy thing for a search partner to recommend, and a partner who trusts you will send you the leaders they place. Approach boutique search partners in the industries you know, and offer to sit in on one placement's first 90 days.

### Investors' platform teams (channel 4)

A founder fresh from a round may first hear "you should get a coach" from a board member. If a venture or private equity firm has a platform or talent team, ask whether it keeps a list of coaches for portfolio CEOs and what it takes to join. Aim for two or three such lists in the stage and sector you know, and tell the platform lead what patterns you see across their companies, without breaking anyone's confidence.

## Going first: leaders at a moment of change, and People leaders

### Leaders at a moment of change (channel 5)

Some leaders announce their moment in public. They post about their first month as CEO, react to posts by leadership authors, or their company posts a job for a senior role they will own. Each gives you a reason to write without an introduction.

Keep the first message to one question about the moment and nothing about your services. "Your post about learning to delegate raised a question for me. What did you hand off first?" gives a busy CEO something to answer. A credentials paragraph gives them something to skip. When a company is hiring a senior leader, [searching companies by the roles they are hiring for](/plays/companies-hiring) finds them, and [recently funded startups](/plays/recently-funded-startups) finds the CEOs whose job changed with the round.

### People leaders building a program (channel 6)

A Chief People Officer or head of L&D who is building a leadership program buys coaching in volume, but slowly. Expect a brief, chemistry calls, a pilot with a few leaders and a review before anything larger. Your proposal has to show how coaches are matched, what the sponsor will see, and how progress will be measured. [Selling coaching to companies](/blog/how-to-sell-coaching-to-companies) covers the buying process, the contract and the pilot in detail.

## Platforms and associate work: paid hours, someone else's client

### Coaching platforms (channel 7)

Platforms sell coaching to companies at scale and staff it with experienced outside coaches. They can bring paid sessions with no selling on your part, at their rate, and the client relationship stays with them. Their entry bars, as published in November 2026:

- [CoachHub](https://www.coachhub.com/for-coaches) asks for certification from a leading coaching association, more than six years of managerial experience in a relevant industry, and at least 500 coaching hours, with most coaches above 1,000.
- [BetterUp](https://www.betterup.com/about-us/become-a-coach) asks applicants to have a resume and a coach credential certificate ready, and matches coaches with members across a range of industries and levels.

Those bars line up with the [ICF credentials](https://coachingfederation.org/credentialing/icf-credentials-overview/). As of November 2026, the ACC asks for 60 hours of coach-specific education and 100 hours of coaching experience, the PCC for 125 and 500, and the MCC for 200 and 2,500, each with 10 hours of mentor coaching. For corporate work, a PCC or higher settles the credential question early.

Use a platform for income and practice hours while you build your own clients. Cap the share of your week it takes, because the hours you give it are hours you aren't building a referral base.

### Associate work for leadership-development firms (channel 8)

Firms that sell leadership programs to large companies need associate coaches to deliver them. You coach under their brand and at their rate, with their client. In exchange you get corporate engagements you couldn't win alone, and references from inside companies you may later sell to directly, if your associate contract allows it. Read the non-solicit clause before you sign.

The same ICF study found that 60% of coaches also offer training and 57% offer consulting. If you run workshops as well, [how to get corporate training clients](/blog/how-to-get-corporate-training-clients) covers that buyer, and [the consulting playbook](/blog/how-to-get-consulting-clients) covers the advisory side.

## Executive coach marketing without a large audience

Executive coach marketing works when it makes one decision easy for one buyer. You don't need a following. You need four assets:

1. **A moment you own.** "I coach founder-CEOs in the year after their Series A" beats "I coach leaders to reach their potential." The sponsor knows when to call you; the founder recognizes themselves.
2. **A one-page bio for the sponsor.** Your background as a leader, who you coach, how you work (sessions, length, stakeholder input), your credential, and what the sponsor sees at the midpoint and the end.
3. **Three anonymous case summaries.** The situation, the goal agreed with the sponsor, what changed in the leader's behavior, how others noticed. No names, no confidential detail.
4. **One point of view in public.** A short piece every few weeks on the moment you own, in the places your buyers read. It is channel 9, and it works slowly: a leader who has read you for months comes to the chemistry call already leaning your way.

Then treat the chemistry call as the sale. Ask more than you tell, name what you heard back, and end with a clear proposal for the next step.
