**TL;DR:** In recruitment business development, the first message that gets a hiring manager to answer names the signal you saw, asks one question about their search, and offers nothing yet. The signal can be a job post, how long it has been open, or a team they said they are building. Eight templates follow, with two follow-ups and the email rules. Hold the CV until the manager asks for it, and send it only once the candidate has agreed to that company.

These messages assume you have already found the lead and qualified it. If not, [lead generation for recruitment agencies](/blog/lead-generation-for-recruitment-agencies) covers the signals and the lead sheet, and [how to get clients for a recruitment agency](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-recruitment-agency) ranks the channels these messages feed.

## What a hiring manager decides in the first line

A hiring manager decides in one line whether you looked at their situation or sent a blast. They run three tests, in this order:

1. **Is it about my search?** A message that names their role, their team or their post passes. "I specialize in finance recruitment" fails.
2. **Is it short?** Four lines on a phone screen, at most.
3. **Can I answer it in one line?** A specific question gets an answer. "Would you be open to a quick call?" gets ignored.

Build every first message from four parts: the signal with its date, one question, a clause on what you recruit, and your name. Leave out your fee, your terms, your candidates and the word "partnership".

## Recruitment business development messages for eight signals

Each template opens on a different signal. The first four come from job posts, which is also where [Pancake for recruiting agencies](/for/recruiting-agencies) begins. Replace the brackets with what you saw, and write it in your own voice: a message that reads like a template reads like everyone else's.

### 1. The role that has been open for weeks

Use it when a post is six weeks old or older.

> Hi [Name], the [role] post has been up since [month]. What's making it hard to fill?

### 2. The repost with a higher range

Use it when a role comes back with a new title or a bigger pay range.

> Hi [Name], saw the [role] went back up with a higher range. Did the first candidates fall short on experience, or on pay?

### 3. The hiring burst

Use it when one team opens several roles in a month.

> Hi [Name], counted [number] [role] openings on your careers page this month. Is that one team growing, or several?

### 4. The first hire in a function

Use it when a company posts its first role of a kind, such as its first data engineer.

> Hi [Name], looks like [Company] is hiring its first [role]. What does that person need to have done before?

### 5. The funding round

Use it in the weeks after a round is announced.

> Hi [Name], saw the [round] news. Which hire matters most to you over the next six months?

### 6. The post about building a team

Use it when the manager writes about growing their team.

> Hi [Name], read your post about building out the [team]. Are you hiring senior people first, or a lead to hire them?

### 7. The rival agency's post

Use it when the manager comments on a competitor's post about a hiring problem. Name the problem, never the rival.

> Hi [Name], saw you join the discussion on why [type of] searches take so long. What's the slowest step on your side?

### 8. The past client who moved

Use it when a manager you worked with starts a new job.

> Hi [Name], congrats on the move to [Company]. What's the first seat you're filling there?

Sign each one with your name and a line on your niche, such as "I recruit controllers for manufacturers in the Midwest." [How to find companies that are hiring](/blog/how-to-find-companies-that-are-hiring) shows where to spot each of these signals and how to date a post. Staffing desks selling shifts can use the same structure with volume-hiring signals, covered in [how to get clients for a staffing agency](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-staffing-agency).

## Following up without chasing

Send no more than two follow-ups, and make each one add something the first message didn't.

**Follow-up 1, four working days later.** Add one fact from your desk:

> Quick add: the last [three] [role] candidates I placed each had [two] other offers. Happy to share what they were offered, if useful.

**Follow-up 2, a week after that.** Close the loop:

> Should I check back when the [role] reopens, or is it covered for now?

Then stop. Wait for a new signal, such as a repost or a second opening, before you write again. The manager who said nothing this month may need you next quarter, and remembers who didn't pester them.

## The spec CV question

Send a candidate's CV only after the hiring manager asks to see it and the candidate has agreed to that company. A CV sent cold, on spec, can reach the candidate's current employer, start a dispute with another agency over who introduced them first, and cost you the candidate's trust.

In Great Britain, it can also break the rules agencies work under. Regulation 19 of [the Conduct of Employment Agencies and Employment Businesses Regulations 2003](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/3319/regulation/19) says an agency may not introduce a work-seeker to a hirer without confirming the work-seeker is willing to work in the position the hirer wants to fill. [Regulation 28](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/3319/regulation/28) bars disclosing a work-seeker's information to their current employer without their prior consent.

Removing the name doesn't solve it. In a small market, a job title, a sector, a city and a career history can identify someone, and [the UK GDPR's definition of personal data](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/4) covers any person who can be identified directly or indirectly.

What works better:

1. **Ask before you send.** Describe the profile in one line in your message: "a controller who led a carve-out at a 300-person manufacturer". Ask whether the manager wants to see more.
2. **Get the candidate's yes for that company,** in writing, and keep the record with the lead.
3. **Send the CV once both have agreed,** with your terms attached or already signed.

None of this is legal advice. If you place candidates in Great Britain, read the regulations themselves or ask your trade body.

## Email rules if you send these from your inbox

Each template works as a direct message or an email. Email brings legal rules, and they differ by country.

- **United States.** The [FTC's CAN-SPAM guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) says the law makes no exception for business-to-business email. Use a subject line that matches the message, include your postal address, and honor opt-outs within 10 business days.
- **United Kingdom.** [Regulation 22 of PECR](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/2426/regulation/22), the consent rule for marketing email, applies to individual subscribers, and the regulations count unincorporated bodies of individuals as individuals. A hiring manager at a limited company can receive your email without prior consent. A contractor working as a sole trader, or a partnership that isn't incorporated, needs to have agreed first, outside the narrow "soft opt-in" for existing customers. Every marketing email, to anyone, must say who it is from and give a working address for opting out.

Many recruiters mix the two channels: a direct message for the first question, an email once the manager asks for a job spec or terms.

## Habits that mark you as one more agency

- **"Do you have any vacancies?"** It asks the manager to do your research.
- **A list of named candidates.** It shares people's details without their say, and tells the manager you would do the same with their staff.
- **Your fee in the first message.** Price comes after they want the service.
- **The same message to five people at one company** in the same week. They compare notes.
- **Fake familiarity.** "As discussed" or "following up on our call" when there was no call ends the conversation.
