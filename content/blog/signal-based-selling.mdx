**TL;DR:** Signal-based selling means writing to a company when something public says the timing is right, and making that something the subject of your first message. The method has four steps: pick two or three signals tied to what you sell, reach out inside each signal's window, open with one question about the signal, and count results by signal so you keep the ones that work. [Buying signals in B2B](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) lists 18 signals to choose from. This page is the method for working them.

---

## What signal-based selling is, and what it replaces

Signal-based selling is prospecting driven by evidence of timing. You may also see it called signal-based sales, signal-led selling or signal-based outreach. The idea is the same: an event at one company decides who you write to, when, and what you say.

List-based outbound works the other way round. It starts from a filter, such as SaaS companies with 50 to 200 people, and sends the same sequence to everyone inside it. The differences show up at every step:

- **Who:** everyone who fits, versus the person the event concerns.
- **When:** whenever the sequence starts, versus inside the event's window.
- **What you say:** a pitch, versus a question about the event.
- **What you count:** sends and opens, versus conversations per signal.

The trade is volume for relevance. You reach fewer people in a week, and each of them has a reason to hear from you.

Signals matter even more once an agent does the prospecting. An agent fed only fit data writes confident messages to people with no reason to answer. [AI GTM](/blog/ai-gtm) covers what else an agent needs to run outbound well.

## Step 1: Pick signals that create the problem you solve

Pick a signal by asking whether the event creates the problem you solve, or makes it urgent. Start with two or three, each tied to a buyer you can name.

| If you sell | Watch for | Write to |
|---|---|---|
| Data consulting | A first analytics engineer role | The head of data |
| Onboarding software | Implementation specialist openings | The VP of customer success |
| A security compliance service | A trust page that says an audit is in progress | The CTO |
| Recruiting for engineering teams | Five or more engineering roles posted at once | The founder or head of engineering |
| A replacement for a legacy tool | Job posts that ask for that tool | The lead of the team that runs it |

Then run three checks on each candidate signal:

1. **Can you date it?** A signal without a date can't tell you whether you are early or late.
2. **Can you find it again next month?** A method needs a source that keeps producing, not one lucky thread.
3. **Does it lead to a person?** A company event is only useful once you know who owns the problem it describes.

If hiring is your first pick, [hiring signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) maps which roles point to which purchases, and [companies hiring for a role](/plays/companies-hiring) shows the signal written as a standing search.

## Step 2: Time outreach to each signal's window

Every signal has a window: the stretch of time in which mentioning it still makes sense. Date the signal itself, not the day you found it, and write while the event is still current. The windows below are our working estimates, not research findings.

| Signal | How to date it | Write within | Stale when |
|---|---|---|---|
| A public request for a recommendation | The post's timestamp | A day or two | They have picked someone |
| A post about the problem | The post's timestamp | About a week | The thread goes quiet |
| Comments under a competitor's post | The comment's date | About two weeks | The launch or debate is old news |
| A job post for the team you support | The original posting date | While the role is open | The post comes down |
| A recent funding round | The announcement or the filing date | One to three months | The hiring plan is done |
| A tool named in job posts | Dates across the company's posts | Several months | The tool drops out of new posts |

Two sources give you a reliable date.

**Job posts.** A board's "posted 2 days ago" can mean the day the board picked the post up or refreshed it. Careers pages that use Google's job posting markup carry a `datePosted` field, which [Google's documentation defines](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/job-posting) as the original date the employer posted the job. Open the page source and search for it.

**Funding.** A US company raising money under Regulation D [must file Form D within 15 days of the first sale of securities](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/capital-raising-building-blocks/what-form-d), and the filing then becomes public on EDGAR. The filing date tells you how fresh the money is. [How to find startups that raised money](/blog/how-to-find-recently-funded-startups) walks through reading one.

## Step 3: Write the first message from the signal

The signal is the subject of the first message. Name the event in a few words, ask one question the event raises, and stop. Leave out the pitch, the deck and the calendar link.

- **A job post:** "Saw the implementation specialist openings. Are they for new customers, or to fix onboarding for the ones you have?"
- **A funding round:** "Congrats on the seed round. Will the first hires be engineers, or someone to own sales?"
- **A post:** "You wrote that month-end close takes nine days. Which step eats most of it?"
- **A tool in job posts:** "Your warehouse postings mention a new WMS rollout. Who trains the floor team on it?"

Engagement signals need one more rule: open with the topic, never with the fact that you saw someone react to a rival. "Usage-based pricing keeps coming up this quarter. Has your team moved to it, or is it still a debate?" reads as a conversation. A message that cites their comment under a competitor's post reads as surveillance. [Finding people who engage with competitors](/blog/competitor-engagement-signals) covers that etiquette.

Follow up twice at most, a few days apart, and make each follow-up add something: a short example, a number, a relevant resource. A reply ends the sequence.

## Step 4: Measure by signal, not by send

Signal-based selling succeeds or fails one signal at a time, so measure it that way. A sheet with one row per person is enough:

- signal type and signal date;
- date of your first message;
- replied, replied positively, booked a meeting (yes or no);
- notes on why the conversation went nowhere.

Each month, read four numbers per signal: replies per 100 people, positive replies per 100 people, meetings, and the median days from signal to first message. Then apply three rules:

1. **Judge a signal on a fair sample.** Give each one at least 50 people before you call it.
2. **Drop signals that start talk but not deals.** Replies with no meetings mean curiosity, not need.
3. **Tighten the window when late messages lag.** If messages sent in week three of a job post do worse than week one, write earlier.

---

## Where signal-based selling breaks

The method fails in a few repeatable ways, and each has a fix.

- **Your buyers leave no trace.** Local businesses rarely post jobs or announce plans. Referrals and local search work better there.
- **Filters stacked until no one is left.** Hiring and funding and a named tool at the same company is rare. Run one signal plus your fit filters, and use a second signal to rank, not to exclude.
- **Fit mistaken for a signal.** "200-person fintech in Austin" says who could buy. Pair it with an event before you write.
- **No owner found.** A company event with no named person ends in a generic inbox. Find the person who owns the problem before you write a word.
- **Counting sends.** Say one week brings 300 messages and two conversations, and the next brings 40 messages and six. The second week won.
