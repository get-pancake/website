**TL;DR:** An outbound sales strategy comes down to five decisions: which accounts and people to target, which signals earn a message this week, which one or two channels to use, how many touches before you stop, and which numbers decide what you keep. Timing beats volume. A hundred messages to people with a reason to talk teach you more than five thousand sent to a list.

This is a B2B outbound strategy for founders and small teams without a sales department, and the outbound part of a wider [go-to-market strategy for startups](/blog/go-to-market-strategy-for-startups). Each decision comes with a worked example, followed by the outbound sales process step by step and the numbers to read after your first hundred messages.

---

## The five decisions behind an outbound sales strategy

Write each decision down before the first message goes out. Here they are, filled in for a hypothetical ten-person consultancy that cleans up CRMs, lead routing and sales reporting for US software companies.

| Decision | The question it answers | Example answer | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target | Which accounts, and which people in them? | US software companies with 50 to 300 staff on HubSpot or Salesforce; the VP of Sales, or the head of revenue operations if there is one | A list defined by industry alone |
| Signals | What earns a message this week? | A revenue operations or CRM admin job post; a Series A or B in the last quarter; a sales leader posting about forecast misses | Writing to everyone who fits |
| Channels | Where do the opening message and the follow-up go? | A direct message under the founder's name, then email to people who stay silent | Five channels at once |
| Cadence | How many touches, how far apart? | Three messages over ten working days, stopped by any reply | Follow-ups that repeat the opening message |
| Metrics | What decides keep or kill? | Meetings per 100 people contacted, per signal | Counting messages sent |

That table is the strategy. The sections below explain how to fill in each row for your own business.

## Targeting: name the accounts, then the people

Start from your three best customers, turn what they share into filters, and stop narrowing when the list is short enough to research by hand.

- **Filters you can check.** Industry, headcount, location, funding stage, business model and the tools named in job posts. Drop filters nobody can verify, like "innovative" or "growth-minded".
- **Two tiers.** Tier 1 is 30 to 50 accounts you research one by one and write to when anything changes. Tier 2 is a few hundred accounts that hear from you only when a signal fires.
- **Two people per account.** The problem owner feels the pain and replies. The budget owner signs. At 50 staff the CEO is often both; at 300 they tend to be a director and a VP. Write to the problem owner first.

[How to build a prospect list](/blog/how-to-build-a-prospect-list) turns the filters into named accounts, and [how to find decision makers](/blog/how-to-find-decision-makers) gets you from the account to the right name.

## Signals: what earns a message this week

A signal turns an account that fits into an account worth writing to now. Pick two or three that your offer answers, rank them, and write only when one fires. For the example firm, from strongest to weakest:

1. **A public request.** A sales leader asks peers to recommend a HubSpot or Salesforce consultant.
2. **A job post for the work.** A revenue operations manager or CRM administrator role: the work exists, and so does a budget.
3. **A recent round.** New sales hires are coming, and the CRM has to hold them.
4. **Two CRMs in the job posts.** HubSpot and Salesforce both named across the company's openings, which can mean a migration is on the table.
5. **A post about the pain.** A sales leader writing about forecast misses or a messy pipeline.

Two signals at one account move it to the top of the week's list. Date every signal from the event, not from the day you found it, because a stale signal reads as a form letter. The [list of 18 B2B buying signals](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) has more candidates for your own top three, and [signal-based selling](/blog/signal-based-selling) covers how long each kind stays usable.

## Channels: where the first message goes

Open on one channel and follow up on at most one more. Each channel has rules that decide whether your message arrives at all.

- **Direct messages.** On the platforms where your buyers post. Short, under your own name, written like a reply to something they did. No link in the opening message.
- **Email.** It needs a verified address and a domain in good standing. [Google's sender guidelines](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/81126?hl=en) require anyone sending to Gmail to set up SPF or DKIM and keep the spam rate shown in Postmaster Tools below 0.3%, and senders of more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail also need DMARC and one-click unsubscribe, as of November 2026. US commercial email follows CAN-SPAM too, and [warm outreach vs cold outreach](/blog/warm-outreach) sums up its rules.
- **Phone.** Business-to-business sales calls are exempt from most of the Telemarketing Sales Rule, as the [FTC's compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-telemarketing-sales-rule) explains, but since May 2024 the [amended rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/04/16/2024-07180/telemarketing-sales-rule) bans misrepresentations and misleading statements in those calls too. A call tends to land better as the second touch, once a message has given the person a reason to pick up.

Each extra channel adds setup work and splits your attention, so add the second only when the first runs without you checking it hourly.

## Cadence: three touches, then let it go

Three messages over about two working weeks is enough for a first attempt, and any reply stops everything. A cadence to start with:

1. **Working day 1.** The opening message: one question about the signal, no pitch, no link.
2. **Working day 4.** A follow-up that adds one new thing, such as a short example from similar work or a narrower question.
3. **Working day 10.** A short note that closes the loop and makes "no" an easy answer.

Send on weekdays, in the recipient's working hours. Stop at the first reply, "not now" included, and log the reason. Write to the account again only when a new signal gives you a new reason. Time passing isn't one.

## The outbound sales process, step by step

Every account moves through the same eight steps, and each step needs an owner, even when one founder owns them all.

1. Pull the accounts that match your filters.
2. Watch them, and move any account showing a signal into this week's list.
3. Find the person who owns the problem, and a way to reach them.
4. Write the opening message from the signal.
5. Send it, then follow up on the cadence.
6. Sort the replies into four piles: yes, not now, wrong person, no.
7. Book the call and send a one-line agenda.
8. Log the outcome in your CRM, with the signal that started it.

Small teams leak at step 6. A "not now" with a month attached is a follow-up task for that month, and a "wrong person" that names someone else is a new lead with a warm first line.

## Measure outbound by signal, not by volume

Read your numbers per signal and per message after every hundred people contacted, and keep what produces meetings. For each signal, track:

- people contacted;
- replies, and positive replies from people who want to talk;
- meetings held per 100 people contacted;
- opportunities opened and deals won;
- days from the opening message to the meeting.

Set the decision rules before you look at the data. A rule we'd start with: after 100 people on one signal with no meeting, rewrite the message once; after 200, drop the signal. Skip the reply-rate benchmarks that tool vendors publish, since they mix channels, markets and definitions of a reply. Your own last month is the benchmark that counts.
