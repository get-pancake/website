**TL;DR:** Corporate video gets booked around a date: a product launch, a trade show, a funding announcement, a rebrand. The studios that win B2B work hear about the date before the brief exists, through past clients whose calendars repeat, agencies that sell the launch or the event but hire out the camera, and outreach to marketing teams whose posts and job ads show the date coming. Directories, RFP portals and your reel help a buyer check you, and they rarely start the conversation.

This is how to get video production clients for a studio with a crew, producers and day rates that sells to businesses. Freelance editors will find parts of it useful, but their best channels differ, and the first section says how.

---

## Freelance editors and B2B studios need different channels

A freelance editor sells hours to creators, small businesses and agencies, and wins through marketplaces, creator communities and word of mouth. A B2B production company sells finished films to marketing teams at companies of roughly 50 to 1,000 people. Its projects come with a brief, several approvers, a purchase order and a deadline that can't move.

That buyer behaves differently from a creator:

- **They buy for a moment.** The launch, the conference, the kickoff. Miss the date and the budget goes to another studio or disappears.
- **They buy again.** A company that films one launch has another next year, plus events, customer stories and recruiting videos in between.
- **They check you before they call.** Reels, case studies and reviews from companies like theirs count for more than awards.

Studios that serve local corporate clients, such as the hospital, the law firm or the university across town, win them through a Google Business Profile, local business groups and public RFPs. Studios that film for tech and B2B brands anywhere win through the calendar, and the channels below are ranked for them.

## The dates B2B buyers book video around

Every corporate video starts with a date someone owns. Find the date, and you know who holds the budget and roughly when they decide.

| Moment | Who holds the budget | What they commission | Where it shows first |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product launch | Product marketing lead | Launch film, product demo, social cutdowns | Teaser posts, a product marketing hire |
| Trade show or user conference | Events or field marketing lead | Booth loop, sizzle reel, session recordings, recap film | Exhibitor directories, posts about the show |
| Funding round | Founder or CMO | Brand film, recruiting video | The funding announcement |
| Rebrand | Brand director or CMO | Brand film, website hero video | Posts about the rebrand |
| Hiring push | Talent or employer brand lead | Recruiting and culture videos | Many roles opening at once |
| Internal kickoff or all-hands | Sales enablement or internal comms | Opening film, customer clips | Rarely public: your clients tell you |

Work back from the date. A launch film needs a script, a shoot, an edit and review rounds before launch day, so the window to pitch opens when the date is set and closes when the brief goes out to three studios. The last row is invisible from outside, which is why the first channel below matters most.

## How to get video production clients: 9 channels, ranked

The order reflects two things for a studio selling to businesses: how fast each channel produces a booked shoot, and how often the client comes back. It is our reading of how each channel works, not a survey.

### 1. Your past clients' calendars

The client who booked a conference recap this year runs the same conference next year. Keep a calendar of every client's repeating moments (annual events, launch seasons, the year-end customer story) and write when the next date is set, before anyone drafts a brief.

Two more asks pay off with existing clients. Offer to recut footage you already shot into cutdowns, vertical versions or translated versions. And ask your contact who else in the company buys video: the employer brand team, sales enablement and internal comms all commission work, and an introduction from marketing makes you the vendor they already know.

**Monday task:** build a sheet of every client's dates for 2027 (conference, launch season, kickoff), and contact the owners of the three earliest this week.

### 2. Agencies that sell the moment but don't shoot it

Many agencies sell the launch or the event and hire out the camera. Each one is a referral partner or a white-label client:

- **Event agencies** need sizzle reels before the event and recap films after it. The [playbook for event management companies](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-event-management-company) shows how they win their own clients.
- **PR firms** need launch assets and executive interviews for the stories they pitch.
- **Branding agencies** need a brand film once the new identity ships, and [how branding agencies get clients](/blog/how-to-get-branding-clients) covers the rebrand signals they watch.
- **B2B marketing agencies** need ad creative and customer stories for the programs they run.

Agree on one model per agency. In white label, you invoice the agency and stay invisible to the client. In a referral deal, you pay a fee on the work they send. Either way, hand them a fixed-price package for the first job so they can resell it without asking you for a quote.

### 3. Outreach to marketers with a date coming up

Outreach works when it names the date. A marketer who posted a launch teaser, a company exhibiting at a show next quarter, a startup that announced a round: each has a video decision to make soon, and a message about that decision reads as useful rather than as a pitch.

The signals worth watching, in rough order of how close they sit to a budget:

- **A video producer, videographer or content lead job post.** The team needs more video than it makes. Pitch the productions one in-house hire can't cover (multi-camera shoots, animation, travel), or the months before the hire starts. Any job board shows these, and so does a [search for companies hiring a role](/plays/companies-hiring).
- **Posts about a launch or an event.** The date is public, and the vendor often isn't chosen yet.
- **A fresh funding round.** New money often pays for a brand refresh, a recruiting push and a launch in the same year.

The first message asks one question about the moment and attaches nothing: "Saw the teaser for the March launch. Is there a launch film planned, or is the team still deciding?" Send the reel after they answer. These are the signals behind [Pancake for video production companies](/for/video-production-companies), and the [guide to B2B buying signals](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) covers more of them.

### 4. Trade-show exhibitor lists

Large trade shows publish who is exhibiting months ahead. [CES 2027](https://exhibitors.ces.tech/8_0/#/) runs January 6 to 9 in Las Vegas, and its exhibitor directory is already public, searchable by category, state and country, with booth numbers. Each exhibitor is a candidate for a booth loop, product demos, clips from the floor and a recap.

Pick two or three shows where your buyers exhibit. List the exhibitors that match your client size, look at what they posted from last year's show, and write before the show with a question about their plans for it. If you attend, book three meetings before you travel. Then promise a recap turnaround in days: event teams want to post while the show is still news.

### 5. Other studios' overflow

Production companies pass work to each other. A studio in another city needs a local crew for its client's shoot in yours. An animation studio gets asked for live action, and the reverse. Publish a short note on what you take (crew days, live action, animation, post) and your day rates, and send work back when a project fits someone else better.

### 6. Clutch and other review directories

Buyers comparing studios use directories to build a shortlist, and the field is wide: Clutch's video production category lists [more than 28,000 companies as of November 2026](https://clutch.co/agencies/video-production). Buyers filter it by services, client budget, hourly rate and industry, and each listing shows a minimum project size, a rate range and verified reviews.

Compete on the filters you can win. Set a minimum project size that matches your real floor, pick the two industries you know best, and ask three happy clients from those industries for reviews this month. A profile with reviews from medical device companies wins the medical device buyer, even next to studios with bigger reels. Video platforms keep directories too: [Vidyard's partner directory](https://www.vidyard.com/partners-directory/) lists its certified agency partners, some of which produce video, with filters for country, language and services.

### 7. A reel cut for each buyer

One two-minute montage asks a product marketer to picture their launch inside someone else's. Cut short reels by moment instead: launch films, event recaps, customer stories, recruiting videos. Pair each with a one-page case study: the brief, the constraint (budget, days, locations), what you shot, and what the client did with it.

Put them on your site, sorted the way buyers search. A content lead looking for event recaps should reach the right reel in one click.

### 8. Public and institutional RFPs

Universities, hospitals, cities and federal agencies buy video through published requests for proposals. On [SAM.gov](https://sam.gov/content/opportunities), anyone can search federal contract opportunities without an account, and state, city and university procurement pages carry the rest. The work pays reliably, but it comes with paperwork, insurance requirements and long evaluation periods. It suits studios with a track record and a producer who doesn't mind forms.

### 9. Marketplaces

Upwork, Fiverr and similar marketplaces suit freelance editors building reviews. For a B2B studio with a crew, they mostly surface small, price-shopped jobs. Use them to fill gaps in the calendar, not to find the client who books a launch film.

## Turn one shoot into a year of work

The first project is the expensive one to win, so plan the second before the first wraps.

At wrap, send a one-page plan for the next twelve months built from what you learned on set: launch cutdowns, a customer story series, the next event recap. Price it as a retainer, with a fixed number of shoot days and edits per quarter booked ahead.

Say a five-person studio films a launch for a 200-person software company. At wrap, it proposes four customer stories over the year, shot two at a time to save travel, plus recaps of the client's two largest events. The client gets a predictable budget line. The studio gets four shoot dates on its calendar a year ahead.

Companies hiring a video producer are good retainer prospects as well. They want steady output, and a retainer delivers it before the hire lands, or next to the new hire on the large shoots.

## What to send first, and what to hold back

Send one question first, and the reel later. The rest of the first exchange follows the same restraint:

- **Don't attach the reel.** It asks for minutes of attention before the buyer has said they need anything. Send two relevant clips once they reply.
- **Do give a price range early.** Marketing teams need a number to get approval, and a range from similar past projects saves both sides a call.
- **Don't write a free treatment.** A one-page approach after a discovery call is fair. A full creative treatment is paid pre-production.
- **Do name the date.** "To deliver before your March launch, we would shoot in mid-February" tells the buyer you have done this before.
