**TL;DR:** Write about one thing the company did, ask one question about it, and keep the audit for the call. Each SEO cold email template below starts from a reason: an SEO hire, a migration, a traffic drop after an update, a new market, a funding round. Send two follow-ups, then stop. In the US every message must meet CAN-SPAM, which makes no exception for B2B email.

These templates are for agencies and consultants writing to a possible client. Link-building outreach to editors is a different job, with different rules. If you sell more than SEO, [marketing agency cold outreach](/blog/marketing-agency-cold-outreach) has ten openers written for marketing leaders. The signals behind each template are covered in [how to find businesses that need SEO](/blog/how-to-find-businesses-that-need-seo), a chapter of our [guide to getting SEO clients](/blog/how-to-get-seo-clients).

## Why the free audit email stopped working

The free audit was the classic SEO opener: crawl the prospect's site, export a long PDF of errors, attach it, ask for a call. It fails now for four reasons.

1. **Anyone can make one.** Audit tools produce a 40-page report in minutes, so the report no longer proves effort or skill.
2. **Google has primed buyers to distrust it.** Google's guide [for site owners hiring an SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo) tells them to be wary of SEO firms that email out of the blue, and to treat unsolicited email about search engines with skepticism. Your PDF starts in that pile.
3. **It can't say what matters.** The same guide says an audit should give realistic estimates of improvement and of the work involved. From outside, without Search Console data, you can't make those estimates, so the report becomes a list of tool flags, many of which don't matter.
4. **It's a list of mistakes from a stranger.** The person reading it is often the person who made them. They defend the work or ignore you.

So send the reason, not the report. The audit becomes your first paid project, or the agenda for the first call.

## The rules every first SEO email follows

1. **The reason goes in the first line:** the post, the job ad, the launch.
2. **Ask one question** the reader can answer in a line.
3. **Stay under 80 words**, with no attachment, no audit and no link in the first email.
4. **Write to the person who owns search:** the head of marketing, growth or content, not the CEO of a 300-person company.
5. **Plain text from a real person**, with your name, your agency and a reply address someone reads.
6. **End with your postal address and a one-line opt-out**, such as "Reply 'no thanks' and I won't write again." CAN-SPAM requires both, as the last section explains.

## 9 SEO cold email templates tied to a reason

Swap the braces for real details. If you can't fill a brace with something true, don't send the template. Four of the nine start from a job post, and [finding companies hiring for a role](/plays/companies-hiring) shows how to spot those.

### 1. They're hiring an SEO manager

**Subject:** The SEO manager role

"Hi {first name}, saw the SEO manager opening at {company}. While the seat is empty, who's keeping an eye on the technical fixes and the rankings you already have?"

### 2. They're hiring content writers

**Subject:** Your content hires

"Hi {first name}, noticed {company} is hiring two content writers. Is the plan to publish more, or to rework the pages that are already live?"

### 3. They announced a migration

**Subject:** The move to {platform}

"Hi {first name}, read that {company} is moving to {platform} in {month}. Who on your side owns the redirect map from the old URLs?"

### 4. Their redesign went live last month

**Subject:** The new {company} site

"Hi {first name}, saw the new site went live in {month}. Has organic traffic held since launch, or is it too early to tell?"

### 5. A marketer posted about a traffic drop

**Subject:** Your post about organic traffic

"Hi {first name}, read your post about organic traffic falling since {month}. Is the drop spread across the site, or concentrated in a few page types?"

### 6. A core update finished a week ago

Send this once the update's end date on Google's [Search Status Dashboard](https://status.search.google.com/products/rGHU1u87FJnkP6W2GwMi/history) is a week old and marketers in their industry are posting about it.

**Subject:** The {month} core update

"Hi {first name}, the {month} core update finished rolling out on {date}, and several {industry} marketers have posted about it since. Did it move {company}'s numbers?"

### 7. They're launching in a new country

**Subject:** The {country} launch

"Hi {first name}, saw {company} is hiring a country manager for {country}. Will the {language} site live on its own domain, in a subfolder or on a subdomain?"

### 8. They raised a round and opened marketing roles

**Subject:** After the {round}

"Hi {first name}, congrats on the {round}. With the new marketing roles open, is organic a channel you plan to build next year, or one you'll keep steady?"

### 9. They commented on an SEO voice's post

**Subject:** Your comment on {expert}'s post

"Hi {first name}, saw your comment on {expert}'s post about AI Overviews taking clicks. Are you seeing that in {company}'s own Search Console data, or is it still a worry?"

## The one audit finding worth sending

Sometimes you find a real break: the pricing page returns a 404, the new site carries a noindex tag, robots.txt blocks a whole folder. Send that one fact, in one sentence, with how you found it.

**Subject:** {company}'s pricing page

"Hi {first name}, your pricing page returns a 404 when I click through from Google today (I searched '{company} pricing'). Flagging it in case nobody has yet. I can share the two other things I noticed if that's useful."

No PDF and no call request. A real break, stated once, earns more trust than a report.

## Follow-up emails: what to add, and when to stop

**Follow-up 1, three or four business days later.** Add one thing they didn't have, such as a line from Google's guide to [moving a site](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes):

"Hi {first name}, one more note on the {platform} move. Google's migration guide says a medium-sized site can take a few weeks or more before the new URLs replace the old ones in results. Is that window in the launch plan?"

**Follow-up 2, about a week later.** Close the loop:

"Hi {first name}, I'll leave it here. If search moves up the list after the launch, you know where I am."

Skip "bumping this to the top of your inbox." It tells the reader you have nothing new to say.

## Lines that sink an SEO cold email

- **A ranking guarantee.** Google tells buyers that no one can guarantee a #1 ranking, and to find someone else if an SEO promises first place.
- **"I noticed you're not on page one for {keyword}."** It reads as automated, and the reader can't tell whether you checked.
- **A fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" subject.** The FTC requires subject lines that reflect the message.
- **Link-building offers inside a client pitch.** Guest-post swaps and link packages make you look like the spam you're trying not to be.
- **A calendar link in email one.** Ask the question first and book the call once they answer.

## CAN-SPAM and EU rules for SEO cold email

In the US, the CAN-SPAM Act covers commercial email, and [the FTC's compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) says the law makes no exception for business-to-business email. Its main requirements, as of November 2026:

1. Accurate header information: your From, To and Reply-To lines can't mislead.
2. A subject line that reflects the content.
3. A clear and conspicuous disclosure that the message is an ad. The law leaves leeway in how you word it.
4. A valid physical postal address: a street address, a registered PO box, or a private mailbox at a commercial mail receiving agency.
5. A clear way to opt out that keeps working for at least 30 days after you send.
6. Opt-outs honored within 10 business days, with no fee and nothing asked beyond an email address.
7. Responsibility for anyone who sends on your behalf. You can't contract it away.

The FTC guide puts the penalty at up to $53,088 for each separate email in violation.

In the EU, Article 13 of the [ePrivacy Directive](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:02002L0058-20091219) allows marketing email to individuals only with their prior consent, and leaves each member state to protect companies in its own way. The rules change from country to country, so check them before you write to anyone in the EU. None of this is legal advice.
