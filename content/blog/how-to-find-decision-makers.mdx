**TL;DR:** Name the problem you solve, then find the person at the target company who owns it: that owner is your first contact, and their boss usually signs. Their names sit on the leadership page, in job posts that say who a role reports to, in press releases, in public profiles and, for public companies, in the proxy statement. Check that the person still holds the job and owns the budget before you write. Above a small purchase, expect more voices: in a Gartner survey, B2B buying groups ran from five to 16 people.

That is how to find decision makers in a company without guessing from job titles. Below: the four roles behind a purchase, who owns and who signs by what you sell, how company size moves the signature, six sources for names, the checks, and the questions that settle it on a call.

---

## The four roles behind a B2B purchase

A decision maker is rarely one person. Four roles shape most purchases, and at a 15-person company the founder can hold all four.

- **The owner** feels the problem every week and will run the project: the head of marketing for a new site, the controller for month-end close software. Write to this person first. When the owner argues for you inside the company, sales teams call them the champion.
- **The signer** approves the money. It is often the owner's boss: a VP, the CFO or the founder. Sales methods call this role the economic buyer.
- **The users** live with what you sell. They rarely decide, but a team that dislikes a tool can stall the rollout and the renewal.
- **The blockers** can stop a deal they don't own: IT and security for software, legal for the contract, procurement for vendor terms.

Committees grow with the price. Gartner surveyed 632 B2B buyers in 2024 and found buying groups of five to 16 people across as many as four functions, and 74% of buyer teams showed what it calls unhealthy conflict during the decision ([Gartner, May 2025](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-07-gartner-sales-survey-finds-74-percent-of-b2b-buyer-teams-demonstrate-unhealthy-conflict-during-the-decision-process)). One name opens a conversation. A larger deal needs the owner, the signer and whoever can say no.

## Who decides, by what you sell

Titles follow the purchase. The table maps common B2B purchases at companies of roughly 50 to 500 people. Treat each row as a first guess to check against the company itself.

| What you sell | Owner: write here first | Usually signs | Can block |
|---|---|---|---|
| Website, brand or content | Head of marketing or brand lead | CMO, or the CEO at smaller firms | Product team, when the site carries the product |
| Paid ads or demand generation | Head of growth or demand gen lead | VP of marketing | Finance, on retainers and media budgets |
| Recruiting fees | Head of talent, or the hiring manager | VP of the hiring team; CFO on fee terms | A talent team that prefers to hire direct |
| Managed IT or security services | IT manager; the COO or office manager where nobody runs IT | COO or CFO | Compliance, the cyber insurer's questionnaire |
| Software for one team | The lead who will run it, such as RevOps for sales tools | That team's VP; the CFO above a set amount | IT and security review, legal |
| Developer tools | Engineering managers and staff engineers | VP of engineering or CTO | Security, the platform team |
| Fractional CFO or outsourced accounting | The CEO, or the controller | The CEO, sometimes with the board | The current accounting firm |
| Training or coaching | Head of learning, or VP of people | The leader whose team gets trained | The annual budget calendar |

Read the target's job posts before you trust a row. A post for a "marketing operations manager reporting to the CMO" names the owner's boss and shows the team is growing, in one line.

## How company size moves the signature

Company size changes who signs. As a rough guide:

- **Under 50 people.** The founder or CEO approves most spending and often owns the problem too. Write to the founder, or to the one person who runs the function.
- **50 to 500 people.** Function heads own their budgets. The CFO or CEO joins above a set amount, and software goes through an IT or security review.
- **Over 500 people.** A director or manager owns the project and a VP signs. Procurement, security and legal each run their own step, often with a vendor security questionnaire.

The pattern breaks often. A 40-person company with a strong COO may route every purchase through her, and a large company may let a team lead buy a small tool on a company card. Ask early how the last similar purchase was made.

## How to find decision makers in a company: six sources for names

Each source below is free. Use two or three per account, and write down the link and the date next to every name.

1. **The company's own site.** Leadership and team pages list the senior people. Press releases quote the executive behind each launch, which tells you who owns the topic. Blog bylines, webinar hosts and event speaker pages show who speaks for a function.
2. **Job posts.** Many posts say who the role reports to and how big the team is. That names the budget owner and shows the team is growing. [Job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) covers the rest of what a post gives away.
3. **Public profiles and company pages.** Search public profiles for the company name plus the title from the table, then read the current title and start date. A company's people page shows who else works in that function, which points to the boss and the peers.
4. **Filings.** For public companies, Item 10 of the 10-K covers directors and executive officers, and most companies put those details in the proxy statement, filed a month or two after the 10-K ([SEC: How to Read a 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/answers/reada10k.htm)). A private company that raised money may have filed a Form D, whose Item 3 lists executive officers and directors; [EDGAR full-text search](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/) finds those filings by company name.
5. **Org chart sites.** [The Org](https://theorg.com/) publishes public org charts for many companies. Coverage and freshness vary from one company to the next, so confirm each name in a second source.
6. **Google search.** Google's [search operators](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/2466433?hl=en) narrow the hunt. `site:example.com "head of marketing"` searches one company's site, quotes force an exact title, and a minus sign drops noise, as in `-jobs`.

## How to confirm you found the right person

A name is a guess until it passes four checks:

- **They still work there.** Look for the current title on the company's site or in recent public posts. A profile untouched for two years may belong to someone who has left.
- **They cover your scope.** A "VP of marketing, EMEA" is the wrong person for a US website project. Match the region, the product line and the business unit.
- **They own the problem now.** Good signs: they posted about it, a press release quotes them on it, or the open role reports to them.
- **They aren't the recruiter.** Talent teams and staffing firms publish job posts. The person you want is the one the role reports to.

When two people could own the problem, write to the one closer to the work. A director who runs the project has more reason to answer than a VP three levels away.

## How to identify the decision maker once you're talking

"Are you the decision maker?" irritates the people who are, and tempts the others to bluff. Ask about the process instead:

- "How did your team buy the last agency (or tool) like this?"
- "Who else will want a say before this moves forward?"
- "Whose budget would it come from?"
- "What will security, legal or procurement need to see?"
- "If you liked it, what happens between yes and a signature?"

Some answers mean you're one step away from the real owner. The person can't describe the last purchase, says "send me something" with no next step, or mentions a budget holder they never involve. Ask for an introduction to that person, and offer a one-page summary your contact can forward to the signer.

A concrete exercise for this week: pick 10 target accounts, such as [lookalikes of your best customers](/blog/how-to-find-similar-companies). For each, write the owner, the signer and one likely blocker from the table, then fill in names from the six sources with a link and a date. Ten accounts with three checked names each will teach you more than 300 rows of unchecked titles. Those names become the people column of your [prospect list](/blog/how-to-build-a-prospect-list). Then [find each person's work email](/blog/how-to-find-someones-work-email) and verify it before you send.
