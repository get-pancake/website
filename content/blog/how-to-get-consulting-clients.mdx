**TL;DR:** Consulting work goes to people a buyer already trusts, so your fastest clients sit in your own history: the employer you left, past clients, and former colleagues who now run teams elsewhere. Once those conversations are moving, add one channel where someone vouches for you (another firm's overflow, an accountant or investor who refers) and one that reaches strangers at the moment they name the problem you fix. That mix is how to get consulting clients without waiting a year for your writing to pay off.

Below: 10 channels ranked in one table by how fast each tends to produce signed work for a practice of one to twenty people, then grouped by the trust they run on: the trust you have, the trust you borrow, and the trust you earn from strangers. If you haven't signed anyone yet, [the step-by-step for your first consulting client](/blog/how-to-get-your-first-consulting-client) starts one step earlier.

## What has to be true before a consulting client signs

An executive hires a consultant when three things line up: a problem they can name, a budget they control, and a reason to believe you can fix it. The first two belong to the buyer. Every channel below exists to create the third.

That belief forms in one of three ways:

- **They have seen your work.** A former boss, a past client, the colleague who watched you run the turnaround.
- **Someone they trust vouches for you.** Their accountant, their investor, a firm they already pay.
- **You show up as they name the problem.** A COO posts about missed delivery dates, and the one note she gets asks the question only someone who fixes delivery would ask.

The field is crowded. The [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/business-and-financial/management-analysts.htm) counts about 1.1 million management analyst jobs in the US in 2025, and 14% of them are held by self-employed workers, paid by their clients by the hour or by the project. Expertise wins the engagement once you are in the room. The channel mix decides how often you get into one.

## Which channels fit your practice today

The right three depend on how long you have been independent and how many people you need to keep busy.

- **Newly independent, months 0 to 6:** channels 1, 2 and 4. Your history is the pipeline, and overflow from other firms pays the bills while it converts.
- **Solo, with a few years of clients:** channels 2, 3 and 5. Referrals carry the base. Outreach on signals and two or three advisor relationships fill the gaps between projects.
- **A firm of 5 to 20 people:** channels 2, 3 and 7, plus 6 or 10 if your work suits them. A firm must sell beyond the founder's contacts, so it needs at least one channel that works without the founder in the room.

Judge each channel after eight weeks on conversations started, not on contracts signed. Signed work trails conversations by weeks, and a channel that produced no conversations needs a sharper offer or a different audience before it needs more hours. Coaches face a different buyer split: [the executive coaching playbook](/blog/how-to-get-executive-coaching-clients) covers it.

## The trust you have: your own track record

### Your former employer and ex-colleagues (channel 1)

The company you left knows your work, already pays for the problem you solved there, and lost capacity when you walked out. That makes it a natural first client, often for the project you were in the middle of. Read your employment agreement before you pitch it: a non-solicit or confidentiality clause can limit which clients and colleagues you may approach.

Ex-colleagues matter more each year. The peer who moved to a competitor, the director who became a COO, the analyst who now runs a team: each has watched you work. Write to them one at a time about something you know they face. "You mentioned the forecast fights at your last company. Did they follow you?" gets an answer. "I've gone independent, let me know if I can help" gets a polite "congrats."

**First move:** list everyone you worked with closely in the last ten years and where they work now. Star the ones who own a budget today.

### Past clients and the people they know (channel 2)

A past client buys again when the next problem appears, and refers you when a peer runs into the one you already fixed. Both happen more often when you stay useful between projects: a short note when you see something relevant to their business beats a quarterly "checking in."

Ask for referrals when the result shows, such as the board meeting where the new forecast held or the quarter churn fell. Make the ask narrow: "Which other COOs in your peer group are fighting the same handoff problems?" And offer a second phase before the first one closes, such as a 60-day review or a handover workshop for the person who inherits your work.

## The trust you borrow: people who vouch for you

### Other firms' overflow (channel 4)

Generalist consultancies, boutiques and fellow independents win work they can't staff. A specialist who can run a workstream without supervision gets that call. You earn less per day, because the prime firm keeps a margin, but it did the selling, and its client may hire you directly for the next phase if your contract allows it.

Tell five firm owners in writing what you take, what you refuse and your day rate. Send work back when a project suits them better. Read every subcontract for a non-solicit clause before you sign.

### Advisors next to your buyer (channel 5)

Your buyer already relies on people who hear about problems early: an accountant, a lawyer, an investor's platform team, the vendor whose software you implement. Each gets asked "Do you know someone who can fix this?" and wants an answer that makes them look good.

Pick the two advisor types closest to your specialty. A pricing consultant pairs well with accountants and investors who work with SaaS companies. An operations consultant pairs with private equity operating partners, who answer for how portfolio companies perform. If your practice centers on one software tool, apply to that vendor's partner program, and read its current terms on the vendor's own site first.

### Talent networks (channel 6)

Talent networks staff independents on projects for large companies. [Catalant](https://catalant.com/for-independent-consultants/) presents its consultant community as working for large enterprises and private equity firms. [Business Talent Group](https://businesstalentgroup.com/become-an-independent-consultant/) places independent consultants and interim executives, and as of November 2026 it offers members up to $3,000 for referring a project they can't serve.

The network decides which projects you see, so acceptance alone fills nothing. Use these networks for the large-company work a solo practice rarely reaches, and keep your other channels running.

### Expert networks (channel 9)

Expert networks pay for knowledge by the hour. On [GLG](https://glg.com/for-experts), experts set their own rate for calls, meetings, surveys and longer fractional placements, and GLG says the experts who respond fastest are more likely to be picked. Its clients include investment firms, corporations, law firms and consulting firms paying to learn, not to hire, so a call rarely turns into a project. It does pay while the pipeline fills, and it keeps your view of the market current.

## The trust you earn: buyers who don't know you yet

### Executives showing the problem now (channel 3)

Some buyers announce the problem before they call anyone. They post about it, react to the people in your field who write about it, or post a job for the role you would otherwise fill. Each gives you a reason to write that doesn't depend on knowing them.

- **Posts about the problem.** A CFO writing about a forecast that missed twice. A founder asking how others survived a price increase.
- **Engagement with known voices.** People who comment on posts by the operators and writers your buyers follow.
- **A job post for the role you'd fill.** A company hiring an interim head of operations or a pricing manager has the budget and the problem. Some will take a consultant to bridge the gap or scope the role first. [Companies hiring for a role](/plays/companies-hiring) shows it as a search.
- **A fresh round.** [Recently funded startups](/plays/recently-funded-startups) have new targets and a board asking how they will hit them.
- **A lookalike of your best client.** Same industry, size and stage as the client who got the most from your work.

The first message asks one question about the signal and pitches nothing: "Saw the interim ops role. Is it there to fix something specific, or to hold things steady until a permanent hire?" Attach a capabilities deck to that note and the question becomes a pitch. ### Talks, roundtables and workshops (channel 7)

Forty minutes in front of a room of buyers lets them watch you think, which no brochure can do. Choose rooms where buyers outnumber consultants: a regional meeting of an industry association, a CFO roundtable, a session an investor runs for its portfolio companies.

End each talk with one concrete offer, such as a fixed-price diagnostic for the first three companies that ask. A paid half-day workshop on one problem can be the first engagement itself, if you price it below the amount that triggers a procurement review.

### A point of view in writing (channel 8)

Writing brings in clients when it takes a position on one problem: why a sales forecast fails in its second month, what a price increase does to churn. A buyer who has read three of your pieces arrives at the first call half-sold. It is the slowest channel to start. It also lifts the others, since prospects from every channel look you up before they reply.

Publish where your buyers read: a short newsletter, posts on the platforms they use, a guest column in their trade publication.

### Public-sector bids (channel 10)

Government buys consulting through formal proposals. The BLS describes the typical pattern: an organization requests proposals from several consultants and picks the one that best fits its needs and budget.

To bid directly on US federal contracts, you need an entity registration on [SAM.gov](https://sam.gov/entity-registration), renewed every 365 days. Cycles are long and past performance counts for a lot, so start as a subcontractor to a firm that already holds the contract: you build a record without writing the whole proposal.

## Signed work in 14 days

When the calendar is empty, the way to get consulting clients fast is to work the warm channels hard before anything else. In order:

1. **Day 1: write the offer in one line.** One problem, one buyer, one result, one time frame. "I rebuild sales forecasts for B2B software companies with 50 to 300 people, in three weeks."
2. **Days 1 and 2: list 40 names** from channels 1 and 2. Rank them by who owns a budget now.
3. **Days 2 to 5: write to all 40**, one at a time, each with a specific question. No deck, no rate card.
4. **Days 3 to 7: tell five firm owners and two advisors** what you take this month.
5. **Days 5 to 10: take the calls.** Offer a fixed-price diagnostic of two to three weeks, not a retainer.
6. **Days 8 to 14: send a one-page proposal** within two working days of each call, and follow up once, five working days later.

Two moves slow this down. A discount sets your price for the next project too. A free "strategy session" that does the diagnostic for nothing leaves the buyer nothing to pay for.
