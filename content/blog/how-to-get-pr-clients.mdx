**TL;DR:** PR clients are won before the news, not after it: before a funding round is announced, before a launch date is fixed, while a first comms hire is still a job post. Get close to those moments through the people who see them first (investors, past clients, partner firms), then write to founders whose rounds and launches leave public traces. Sell the work you control, meaning the story, the materials and the pitching, because reporters decide what runs.

Below is how to get PR clients if you run a PR firm or work as an independent consultant selling to B2B and tech companies: when companies buy, nine channels in one table, and a first project that turns one news moment into a retainer. If PR is one part of a broader marketing offer, the [marketing agency client playbook](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-marketing-agency) covers the rest.

---

## Companies buy PR around a date

A company hires a PR firm when it has news with a date attached. Get there before the date is set, and you shape the story. Arrive after, and you pitch the next one.

- **A funding round.** Between the close and the announcement, the founders decide when to announce, which angle leads and who runs it. Once the round is public, that story is told.
- **A launch.** A product, a new market, a rebrand. Launch dates get set ahead of time, and some founders post about them before launch day.
- **A first comms hire.** A job post for a communications manager means the company has decided press matters. It may still want a firm until the hire starts, or next to a one-person team.
- **A crisis.** Nobody schedules one. Crisis work arrives through referrals: law firms, investors, past clients.

B2B tech founders tend to post about rounds and launches in public, so the signals in this guide fit tech PR best. Consumer and lifestyle PR leans harder on relationships. [Pancake for PR firms](/for/pr-firms) shows the founder posts and comms openings those moments produce.

## 1. Past clients and the comms people you've worked with

The warmest new client has already watched you work. Marketing and comms leads from former clients change companies, and each one arrives with news to tell and a say in which firm tells it.

Current clients have a next story. Keep a short news calendar for each one: the next round, the next launch, the next executive hire, the next customer they can name. Raise the next story before they start planning it, so the retainer renews on a plan rather than a habit.

Ask for introductions when a placement lands and the client is forwarding it to their board. That week, "Who do you know with a launch on the calendar?" gets a name.

---

## 2. Investors and accelerators

For tech PR, investors sit closest to the news. A fund knows which of its companies has closed a round, and founders lean on their investors for introductions, PR firms included.

Make it easy for a fund to send you work:

- a 45-minute session for its portfolio on announcing a round: timing, embargoes, what a reporter needs on the day;
- a one-page checklist the fund can forward to every founder who closes;
- a fixed-fee announcement package with a clear scope, so a referral needs no scoping call.

Accelerator cohorts reach demo day together, each company with a story to tell the same week. One session reaches the whole cohort.

---

## 3. Partner firms that hear about the news first

Several kinds of firms learn about news before a PR firm does:

- **Branding studios** run renames and rebrands that need an announcement. The [branding client playbook](/blog/how-to-get-branding-clients) shows how they find those projects.
- **Web studios and product agencies** build whatever launches.
- **Law firms** see deals, lawsuits and data incidents early, and crisis work follows.
- **Event agencies and investor relations firms** plan the moments companies want covered.

Agree on what each side sends the other and how you'll say thanks: a fee, a share of the first invoice, or referrals back.

## 4. Outreach to companies with news coming

Outbound works for a PR firm when it arrives before the story is spoken for. Five signals tell you when:

- **A round that isn't announced yet.** In the US, a company raising under Rule 504 or 506 of Regulation D must file a Form D [no later than 15 calendar days after the first sale](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-230.503). The notice lists the executive officers, the offering size and the date of first sale, and anyone can look it up in the SEC's EDGAR database, as [Investor.gov explains](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/form-d). A company that waits to announce leaves you a window. [How to find startups that raised money](/blog/how-to-find-recently-funded-startups) walks through the search.
- **A round that was announced.** That story is told. Pitch the one after it: the first customers the money wins, the team it builds, the launch it pays for.
- **A launch with a date.** Founders post that they're launching soon, open waitlists and book trade show booths. A company paying for a booth wants something to say from it.
- **A comms job post.** Communications manager, PR manager, head of communications. Some companies will build in-house, so offer to cover the gap until the hire starts or to support a one-person team. Job posts that name Cision, Muck Rack or Meltwater point to a team that already works the press. [Job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) covers how soon to write.
- **Founders asking how to get press.** A founder who posts that they want coverage has named the need for you.

The first message asks about the story and keeps quiet about how you found it: "Congrats on the close. Are you planning to announce it, or holding the news for the launch?" If a founder wants the round kept quiet, respect that and ask about the launch instead.

## 5. Public requests for PR help

Founders ask for PR firm recommendations in founder communities, Slack groups and public posts. Reply the same day with one line on fit, one comparable placement and one question about their timing. If you're the wrong fit, name a firm that fits better.

---

## 6. Agency searches and RFPs

Larger companies hire PR firms through a formal search. The PR Council's [RFP best practices](https://prcouncil.net/rfp-best-practices/) ask buyers to share the budget and their non-negotiables, explain how agencies will be evaluated, set a realistic timeline, and put agencies in touch with the decision-makers.

Use the same list to qualify. An RFP with no budget, no criteria and a three-day deadline costs you a proposal and teaches you little. Before you write, ask for a call with the people who will choose.

## 7 to 9: overflow, the directory and your own placements

**7. Overflow from bigger firms.** Independent consultants get steady work from firms that win more than they can staff, often around a launch with a fixed date. Offer a niche the firm lacks, such as one sector, trade press or a language, and a day rate it can mark up.

**8. The PR Council directory.** Buyers can search the PR Council's [member firm directory](https://prcouncil.net/find-a-pr-firm/) by industry, service area, company size and location. Membership is for firms, not individuals: as of November 2026 it requires a PR or communications agency in the US or Canada with at least $1 million in annual revenue, and dues start at $3,500 a year, per its [membership page](https://prcouncil.net/membership/). Solo consultants can't join, so named references and a clear niche on your own site do that job.

**9. Your own placements and point of view.** A case study that ties coverage to a result persuades founders more than a wall of logos: the story that brought in demo requests, the trade piece a sales team still sends out. A short newsletter on how reporters in your sector pick stories gets forwarded to the founders who need you.

## Sell the first story before the retainer

A fixed-scope project gets a faster yes than a year-long retainer: a round announcement, a launch, a push around a founder's profile. Scope it around the work you control: the narrative, the materials, the media list, the pitching, the briefing prep, and a report of what went out and what came back. Reporters decide what runs, so write deliverables you can finish.

Book the retainer conversation for the week after the story runs, with the next story already outlined from the client's news calendar.
