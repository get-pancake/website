**TL;DR:** Sell to the person who runs the process, then to the CFO. A controller decides whether an accounting tool fits the close and will survive the audit, a head of FP&A decides for planning tools, and the CFO signs once cost, savings and risk hold up. Stay out of their inbox during the close, answer the control questions before the demo, and start from what finance teams leave in public: controller and FP&A job posts, job posts that name the ERP, and a fresh funding round.

The usual advice for selling to a CFO is to lead with ROI. That's half of it. The CFO is often not the first person to judge a finance tool, and the person who is cares about the close and the audit before the return. Here is how to sell to CFOs and the controllers who advise them, written for fintech and finance-software teams selling to companies of 50 to 2,000 people.

---

## Who owns the problem in a finance team

Before you write to anyone, find the person who feels the problem every month. In a company of a few hundred people, the finance team often looks like this:

| Role | Owns | Asks you | Part in the deal |
|---|---|---|---|
| CFO | Cash, capital, risk, the board | "What does it cost, what does it save, what can go wrong?" | Signs, or vetoes on risk |
| VP Finance or finance director | The team's day-to-day work | "Who on my team runs it, and for how long?" | Sponsors the project |
| Controller | Accounting, the close, controls, the audit | "Will it tie out to the ERP? Will the auditor accept it?" | Judges accounting tools |
| Head of FP&A | Budget, forecast, board reporting | "Can I trust the numbers it pulls?" | Judges planning tools |
| AP, AR and payroll staff | Invoices, collections, pay runs | "How much of my week does this give back?" | Use it, and can sink a rollout |

The [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/management/financial-managers.htm#tab-2) describes controllers as the people who direct the preparation of financial reports that summarize and forecast an organization's financial position. That's why selling to controllers means proving the tool won't break the reports they sign off on. For a planning tool, the head of FP&A plays the same role.

At a small startup, one person may hold all of these jobs, or an outside accountant does. Larger companies add a treasurer, a tax team and internal audit. [Pancake's page for fintech startups](/for/fintech-startups) shows example searches for these roles.

## Time your outreach around the close

Finance teams work to a calendar. Your outreach should follow it.

- **The close.** The first working days of each month go to closing the previous month's books. A message that lands then gets skimmed or resented. Send mid-month.
- **Quarter-end and year-end.** Closes run longer, public companies add earnings, and year-end brings the audit.
- **January.** The year-end close, audit prep and 1099s pile up together: the [IRS requires Form 1099-NEC](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec) to be filed and sent to recipients by January 31. It's a poor month to start an evaluation with an AP team, and a good month to note who complained.
- **Budget season.** For calendar-year companies, next year's budget usually takes shape in the fall. A tool that isn't in it waits a year or needs a discretionary line. Pitch before the budget is set.

**Before your next send:** check the date. If it falls in the first days of the month, move it to the middle.

## Six signals that a finance team will buy soon

A finance team's hiring and its systems show up in public, and some of its leaders post about the work. Six signals, roughly from strongest to weakest:

| Signal | What it suggests | Who to contact |
|---|---|---|
| A controller job post | The company is formalizing its close, or replacing the person who ran it | The CFO now, the new controller once hired |
| FP&A analyst or manager job posts | Planning has outgrown one person and a spreadsheet | Head of FP&A or CFO |
| Job posts that name an ERP or accounting system | The system you must integrate with; "migrating to" wording means a live project | Controller |
| A recent funding round | Board reporting, a first audit and finance hires are coming | CFO, or the CEO at an early startup |
| A new CFO or controller | A new leader inherits the stack and decides what stays | The new leader |
| Posts about the close or an audit | Live pain, in their own words | The author |

Read the ERP signal closely. A staff accountant post that asks for QuickBooks and lists NetSuite as a plus can mean a move is coming. One that asks for NetSuite, Sage Intacct or Microsoft Dynamics 365 tells you which integration you need before the first call. Either way, a job post names what candidates should know, not every system the company runs.

## How to sell to CFOs: a business case on their numbers

A CFO discounts a vendor's ROI slide. Bring a formula that runs on their numbers, and let their team fill it in. Three lines cover most finance tools:

1. **Time:** hours the process takes each month, times the loaded hourly cost of the people doing it.
2. **Errors and risk:** duplicate payments, late fees, audit adjustments, write-offs.
3. **Cash:** days to close, days sales outstanding for receivables tools, early-payment discounts for payables tools.

Say a 400-person distributor's AP team handles 3,000 invoices a month at 12 minutes each (a hypothetical example). That's 600 hours a month. If your product halves the handling time, it frees 300 hours, close to two full-time clerks, before you count late fees or discounts.

Put the math in a sheet the controller can edit. A CFO trusts a number their own team changed more than one you calculated. Then offer a paid pilot on one entity or one process, with the success measure agreed before it starts.

## Answer the control questions before the demo

A controller asks how your product affects the audit before they ask about features. Have these answers on one page:

- **SOC 1 or SOC 2.** If your product touches numbers that land in the financial statements, such as payables, billing, revenue or payroll, expect a request for a SOC 1 report. The [AICPA describes SOC 1](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/audit-assurance/audit-and-assurance-greater-than-soc-1) as an examination of a service organization's controls likely to be relevant to its customers' internal control over financial reporting, meant for those customers and their auditors. SOC 2 covers security and related criteria. Ask early which one their auditor wants.
- **Segregation of duties.** Show that the person who creates a vendor can't also approve its payment, and that roles can be locked down.
- **Audit trail.** Every change logged: who, what, when, and the values before and after.
- **The ERP integration.** Which systems you connect to, which way data moves, and what happens when a record fails to post.
- **Implementation.** Who does the work, how many hours their team gives, and whether go-live lands during a close.
- **Exit.** How they get their data out if they leave.

## First messages for selling to finance teams

A first message to a finance leader works when it names the signal, asks one question they can answer from memory, and pitches nothing.

- To a CFO, after a controller job post: "Saw the controller opening. Is the plan to tighten the close you have, or to rebuild it with the new hire?"
- To a controller, after an ERP shows up in job posts: "Your staff accountant posting asks for QuickBooks and lists NetSuite as a plus. Is a move between the two underway?"
- To a CFO, after a funding round: "Congrats on the round. Is a first audit on this year's list, or next year's?"
- To the author of a post about the close: "You wrote that the close slipped past day ten. What held it up most, reconciliations or approvals?"

Avoid "We help finance teams save time," which says nothing, and "Can I get 15 minutes?", which asks for the scarcest thing they have. Hold any ROI claim until you know their numbers.
