**TL;DR:** Build it in this order: fit rules, a market count, account sources, a dated reason to write, one to three named people per account, verified contact details, then a cleanup against your CRM. Size the list to what you can work well in two weeks, not to every company that fits. And date every row: in January 2026 the median US worker in a management job had been with their employer 6.1 years, so the people on a list move while the companies stay put.

That is how to build a prospect list for B2B sales, whether the seller is an agency, a consultancy or a startup. The table shows the finished product. The eight steps fill it in.

## What a B2B prospect list contains

Every column below earns its place by changing what you do with the row. Keep it in a spreadsheet or in your CRM: the columns matter more than the tool.

| Column | Example | Why it's there |
|---|---|---|
| Company and domain | A 120-person logistics software company and its .com | The domain drives email lookups and catches duplicates |
| Fit notes | US, 50 to 500 staff, sells to freight brokers | Shows the account passed your rules |
| Signal, date and source link | "Hiring two SDRs, posted Nov 12" plus the post's URL | The reason to write now, and how fresh it is |
| Person and title | The VP of sales, title as they write it | Who gets the first message |
| Profile link | Their public profile | Lets anyone recheck the person in seconds |
| Work email and status | The address, marked valid, catch-all or unknown | Decides whether you can send |
| Phone | A direct line, top accounts only | Spend phone lookups on the accounts you will call |
| Tier | A, B or C | Sets the order you work in |
| Owner and status | Who works it; new, contacted, replied, closed | Stops two colleagues writing to one person |
| Last checked | The date you last confirmed the row | Tells you when it needs another look |

## How to build a prospect list in eight steps

Work the steps in order. Each one removes rows the next would waste time on.

### Step 1: Write the fit rules on one page

Fit rules decide which companies get a row at all. Write three to five must-haves and three disqualifiers before you open a database, or its filters will choose for you.

Say you run a RevOps consultancy. Must-haves: a US B2B software company, 50 to 500 staff, a sales team of five or more, running HubSpot or Salesforce. Turn away: agencies, companies under 20 staff, current clients and partners. [Ideal customer profile examples](/blog/ideal-customer-profile-examples) shows nine ICPs written this way.

### Step 2: Count the market before you build

A count tells you whether your rules are too tight or too loose before you spend a week on rows. The Census Bureau's [County Business Patterns](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/cbp.html) reports US establishments with paid employees by industry and employment size, and its most recent release covers 2023.

Read the count with one caveat: an establishment is a location, so a company with three offices counts three times. If your rules match tens of thousands of establishments, add a rule. If they match a few dozen, widen the area or the size band.

### Step 3: Pull accounts from sources that match your buyers

Choose sources by the trail your buyers leave in public:

- **Startups:** funding announcements, Crunchbase, and Form D filings found with [EDGAR full-text search](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/).
- **Growing teams:** job boards and careers pages. [Hiring signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) shows which roles point to which purchases.
- **Teams on a tool you support:** job posts that name the tool.
- **Public companies:** EDGAR's company search, filtered by industry code.
- **Local and licensed businesses:** map listings, state license lookups and association member lists.
- **Trade-show industries:** exhibitor and sponsor lists from the events your buyers attend.
- **Companies like your best customers:** the [lookalike methods](/blog/how-to-find-similar-companies), from review-site categories to investor portfolios.

Pull more accounts than you plan to work. The later steps will throw some out.

### Step 4: Add a dated reason to write

Each row needs a reason to write this month, the date you saw it, and a link to the source. The reasons that hold up best are a job post for the team you support, a new funding round, a tool you work with named in job posts, and a public post about the problem you solve. [Buying signals in B2B](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) covers 18 of them, with where to see each one.

Write the reason as a sentence you could say out loud: "Hiring two SDRs, posted November 12." If you can't, the row isn't ready. An account that fits but shows no signal stays on the list as fit-only.

### Step 5: Name one to three people per account

The person who owns the problem gets the first message. Add the signer and, for larger deals, one reviewer such as IT or security. [How to find decision makers in a company](/blog/how-to-find-decision-makers) covers the titles by purchase and the sources for names.

Copy each title the way the person writes it, and keep the profile link. "Head of Revenue" and "VP of Sales" can be the same job at two companies, or two different jobs at one.

### Step 6: Find and verify contact details

Learn each company's email pattern from addresses it publishes, apply it to the name, and run every address through a verifier before it enters the list. Record the result next to it: valid, catch-all or unknown. Delete the invalid ones, and send to catch-all addresses only in small batches.

Check that each person still works there: a current title on the company's site or a recent public post will do. Leave phone numbers for the accounts you plan to call.

### Step 7: Clean the list against your CRM

Before the first send, remove current customers, open deals, partners, competitors, anyone who opted out, and accounts a colleague already works. Match on the company domain rather than the name, because one company can appear under three spellings of its name and one domain.

### Step 8: Tier the list and refresh it on a rhythm

Tiers decide what you work first:

- **A:** fits, has a fresh signal, and has a verified owner. Write to these this week.
- **B:** fits and has a verified owner, but no signal yet. Recheck monthly.
- **C:** fits on paper only. Recheck each quarter, or drop it.

People move faster than companies. In January 2026, the median tenure with the current employer was 6.1 years for US workers in management occupations and 4.1 years across all wage and salary workers ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/tenure.t06.htm)). Re-verify an email before each new sequence, remove a bounced address the day it bounces, and add new A rows as fresh signals appear.

For this week: build 25 A-tier rows by Friday, and write to them the following week.

## Why a bought list rarely works as the target list

A bought list answers "who exists", not "who needs you now". The same rows go to anyone who pays, nobody on it has a reason to hear from you this month, and you can't tell how old each record is. Bought data can fill gaps, such as a phone number for an account you already chose. Let your fit rules and your signals choose the accounts.
