**TL;DR:** Warm outreach goes to people who already have a reason to read you: they know your work, someone they trust sent you, or they did something in public that your message answers. Cold outreach goes to people with none of the three. The difference sits in the first line. A warm message opens with the reader's reason; a cold one opens with the sender. A signal, such as a job post or a post about the problem, is how a stranger's message becomes warm.

Below: warm outreach vs cold outreach side by side, the email rule both share, and ten templates, each tied to a reason.

---

## What warm outreach means: three sources of warmth

A message is warm when the reader can tell from its first line why it reached them. Three things can supply that reason.

1. **A relationship.** Past clients, former colleagues, people you met on a project or at an event. They know your work, so the reason is you.
2. **Borrowed trust.** An introduction or a referral. Someone the reader trusts vouched for you, so the reason is that person.
3. **A public signal.** They wrote about the problem you solve, commented on your post, opened a role you support or announced a round. The reason is their own words or plans.

Some things feel warm to the sender and read cold to the reader: working in the same industry, following each other's accounts, an email they opened last month, a compliment on the company's growth. None of them tells the reader why this message is for them.

## Warm outreach vs cold outreach, side by side

The four kinds of first message differ in who gets them, what the first line can say, and how many you can send.

| Kind of message | Who you write to | What the first line says | How many there are | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Warm: relationship | People who know your work | What changed since you last spoke | Dozens to a few hundred, and they run out | Treating friends as a pipeline |
| Warm: introduction | People one trusted contact away | Who sent you, and why | As many as people will vouch for | Asking for intros nobody wants to make |
| Warm: signal | Strangers showing a need now | Their post, their job opening, their round | New ones every week | Sounding like you were watching them |
| Cold | Strangers who fit a profile | Your company and your offer | As many as your list holds | Silence, spam folders, unsubscribes |

Read the table from top to bottom and two things trade places: the reader's reason to answer weakens while the supply grows. The signal row breaks the pattern. It keeps a reason in the first line, and it refills as companies hire, raise money and post. Your sales motion decides how much outreach you need, and the [startup go-to-market plan](/blog/go-to-market-strategy-for-startups) helps you pick one.

## Warm or cold, the email rules are the same

Warmth doesn't change the law. The [FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) says the law makes no exception for business-to-business email, and its own example of an email that must comply is a message to former customers announcing a new product line.

So any email whose main purpose is to promote your service needs, among other things, honest sender details and subject line, your physical postal address, and a clear way to opt out. Opt-outs must be honored within 10 business days, and each email that breaks the rules can cost up to $53,088 in penalties, as of November 2026. The test is the message's primary purpose, not how well you know the reader.

## How a signal makes cold outreach warm

A signal warms a stranger's message when it passes three tests: it's recent, it belongs to this person or company, and it lets you ask something only they can answer.

- **Recent.** The job post is still up, the round was announced this quarter, the post went up in the last week or two. [Outbound sales strategy](/blog/outbound-sales-strategy) shows how to rank signals and date each one from the event.
- **Theirs.** It names their company, their team or their words, not their industry.
- **Answerable.** They can reply in one line from their own work, without opening a link.

Two rewrites:

> Cold: "Hi Marcus, we're a fractional CFO firm that works with funded startups. Can I get 15 minutes next week to walk you through our approach?"
>
> Warm, from a signal: "Hi Marcus, congrats on the seed round. Who's putting together the numbers for the next board meeting: you, or someone you're about to hire?"

> Cold: "Hi Priya, our platform helps operations teams automate order routing. Worth a quick chat?"
>
> Warm, from a signal: "Hi Priya, saw the order operations opening. Is that to keep up with volume, or because routing still happens by hand?"

Open with the topic, not with how you found them. A note that cites someone's comment under a competitor's post reads as surveillance; [buying signals in B2B](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) covers the etiquette. Engagement with your own posts is different: a person who answered you in public expects to hear back.

## Warm outreach templates, each tied to a reason

Each template opens with its reason and ends with one question. Swap the brackets, keep it short, and leave links out of the first message. For hiring signals, [job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) maps roles to purchases.

**1. A past client, a year after the project.** The reason is time, and what changed.

> "Hi [name], it's been about a year since we [launched the new onboarding flow]. Now that [the team has doubled], what would you change if we did it again?"

**2. A former colleague in a new job.** The reason is their move.

> "Hi [name], congrats on the move to [company]. At [old company], [problem] ate half our quarter. Is it the same fight at [company], or a new one?"

**3. Someone you met at an event.** The reason is the conversation you had.

> "Hi [name], good talking at [event]. You said [team] was about to [plan]. Did it go ahead?"

**4. Asking a client for an introduction.** The reason is a person they know. Investor Fred Wilson made the case for the [double opt-in introduction](https://avc.com/2009/11/the-double-optin-introduction/): the person in the middle asks both sides before making the introduction. Make that easy with a short note they can forward unchanged.

> "Hi [name], you mentioned [contact] at [company] is dealing with [problem]. Would you forward the two lines below and ask whether they'd like an intro? No pressure either way."
>
> "[Your name] runs [firm], which [one-line result] for companies like [client]. Glad to compare notes on [problem] if that's useful."

**5. Replying to an introduction.** The reason is the person who introduced you.

> "Thanks, [introducer], moving you to bcc. Hi [name], [introducer] mentioned you're [rebuilding X]. Which part would you most like an outside view on?"

**6. A name a client passed you.** Say who sent you in the first five words.

> "Hi [name], [client] suggested I get in touch about [project]. Where are you in it: still scoping, or comparing firms?"

**7. Someone who commented on your post.** The reason is their own comment.

> "Hi [name], thanks for the point about [their comment] under my post on [topic]. How does your team handle [problem] today?"

**8. Someone who posted about the problem.** The reason is their own words, so quote a phrase, not the whole post.

> "Hi [name], you wrote that [every customer audit eats two weeks of engineering time]. Which part lands on the engineers: the questionnaires or the evidence?"

**9. A company hiring for work you support.** The reason is a role with a budget behind it.

> "Hi [name], saw the [role] opening on your team. Is the hire meant to take over [current work], or to build [new function] from scratch?"

**10. A company that raised a round.** The reason is a plan with money behind it.

> "Hi [name], congrats on the [Series A]. When the plan says [double the sales team], what breaks first on your side: [onboarding] or [reporting]?"

## Follow-ups that keep the thread warm

Two follow-ups are enough, and each should add something the first message didn't have.

- **A few days later:** a narrower question, or one useful thing, such as a short example from similar work.
- **About a week after that:** close the loop and make "no" easy: "If this isn't on your list this quarter, no need to reply. I'll leave it here."

Skip "bumping this up", "circling back" and fake "Re:" subject lines. A reply, even a no, ends the sequence, and the next message needs a new reason, such as a new job post or round. With people who know you well, stop after one follow-up and wait for the next reason, or call them.
