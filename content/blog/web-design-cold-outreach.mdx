**TL;DR:** Name the reason in the first line, ask one question, and stop there. A web design cold email template works only when the reason is real and recent: a job post, a redesign post, a launch, a funding round, a broken page, a referral. Leave mockups, audits and prices for after the reply. Below: 8 templates with DM versions, two follow-ups, what never to send, and the email rules for the US and the EU.

Cold outreach is one channel among many. [How to get web design clients](/blog/how-to-get-web-design-clients) ranks the others by speed and cost.

---

## What a first message needs: a reason, a question, a name

A first message needs three parts: the reason you are writing now, one question they can answer in a line, and who you are. Everything else waits for a reply.

- **Subject line.** Plain and specific, in lowercase: "your web designer opening", "the form on your contact page". No tricks.
- **First line: the reason.** The signal you saw, in their words if you can. If you can't name a reason, don't send. [How to find businesses that need a website](/blog/how-to-find-businesses-that-need-a-website) lists 12 reasons and where to see each.
- **One line of fit (optional).** A similar company you built for, or the platform you work in.
- **The question.** About their situation, never about a call.
- **The signature.** Your name and studio. In email, add your postal address and an opt-out line (more on the rules below).
- **Email or DM.** A DM follows the same rules and runs shorter. Send it on the platform where the signal happened, skip links in the first one, and pick one channel per person, not both on the same day.

Four or five lines, readable on a phone without scrolling.

## 8 web design cold email templates, each with a DM version

Replace the bracketed parts, and cut any line you can't make true.

### 1. They posted a job for a web designer

**Subject:** your web designer opening

> Hi [First name], saw [Company] is hiring a web designer. Does the new site need to be live before that person starts, or will they own the rebuild?
>
> We build B2B sites in [platform] and often work alongside a new in-house hire.
>
> [Your name], [Studio]

**DM:** "Saw the web designer role at [Company]. Does the new site need to be live before they start, or is the rebuild theirs?"

A company [hiring for a web role](/plays/companies-hiring) has budget for the site. Some will build in-house, which is why the question offers both paths.

### 2. They posted about a redesign

**Subject:** your post about the new site

> Hi [First name], read your post about rebuilding [Company]'s site. What's driving it most: the design, the messaging, or how slow it is to update?
>
> [Your name], [Studio], we rebuild B2B sites in [platform]

**DM:** "Read your post about the new site. What's pushing the rebuild: the design, the messaging, or slow updates?"

### 3. They launched something

**Subject:** the [product] launch

> Hi [First name], congrats on launching [product]. I noticed the homepage still leads with [old product]. Is a site update on the way, or is that a deliberate choice?
>
> [Your name], [Studio]

**DM:** "Congrats on the [product] launch. Is the site update coming, or is the homepage staying as it is on purpose?"

### 4. They raised a round

**Subject:** after the [round]

> Hi [First name], saw [Company] closed its [round]. Is the website part of the plan for the new money, or a later problem?
>
> [Your name], [Studio]

**DM:** "Saw the [round] news. Is the website on this year's list, or next year's?"

More on timing outreach to [recently funded companies](/plays/recently-funded-startups).

### 5. Something on their site is broken

**Subject:** the form on your contact page

> Hi [First name], on an iPhone the submit button on your contact page sits off-screen, so the form can't be sent. Screenshot below in case nobody has flagged it. Who looks after the site on your side?
>
> [Your name], [Studio]

**DM:** "Heads-up: your contact form's submit button is off-screen on iPhone. Who looks after the site on your side?"

Check it twice before you send. Reporting a bug that isn't there ends the conversation.

### 6. Someone referred you

**Subject:** [Referrer] suggested I write

> Hi [First name], [Referrer] mentioned you're planning a new site for [Company] and thought we should talk. We built [Referrer's company]'s site last year. Is it early, or would 20 minutes next week help?
>
> [Your name], [Studio]

**DM:** "[Referrer] said you're planning a new site and suggested I say hello. Early days, or worth 20 minutes next week?"

A warm introduction earns the right to ask for a call. A cold message doesn't.

### 7. They commented on a rival studio's post

**Subject:** moving a B2B site off WordPress

> Hi [First name], you commented last week on a thread about moving a B2B site off WordPress. Is that a move you're weighing at [Company]?
>
> [Your name], [Studio]

**DM:** "Saw your comment in the WordPress-to-Webflow thread. Is that move on the table at [Company]?"

Talk about the topic, never about the rival studio whose post it was.

### 8. They use your template

**Subject:** [Company] on our [Template] template

> Hi [First name], [Company] runs on our [Template] template, which made my week. What's one thing you wish it did that it doesn't?
>
> [Your name], [Studio]

**DM:** "Spotted [Company] on our [Template] template. What's one thing you'd want it to do that it doesn't?"

Template buyers already trust your design. When they outgrow the template, you are the obvious next call.

## Two follow-ups that add something

Send two follow-ups at most, each with something new, then stop. Reply in the same thread so the context travels with it.

**Follow-up 1, a few business days later:**

> Hi [First name], one example in case it's useful: [link to one page you built for a similar company], rebuilt in [platform] in [weeks]. Worth a look before you brief anyone?

**Follow-up 2, about a week after that:**

> Hi [First name], I'll leave it here. If the site moves up the list, reply "later" and I'll check back in [month].

A no is an answer: thank them and stop. A yes leads to a discovery call, covered in [how to sell web design services](/blog/how-to-sell-web-design-services).

## What never to send

Never send an unsolicited mockup, a long audit, a price, or a message that could go to anyone.

- **Unsolicited mockups.** You design without a brief, so you guess at goals you don't know. Each one costs you hours, and it can read as an attack on whoever built the current site.
- **Long audits.** A 15-point PDF from a stranger asks for an hour of reading. Pick the one problem they would agree with and ask about it.
- **A price.** Without scope, any number is wrong, and a low one anchors the whole deal.
- **"Quick call?"** A stranger asking for 30 minutes gives them nothing to answer. Ask about their site instead.
- **Fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" subjects.** They burn trust, and US law bars deceptive subject lines (see below).
- **Merge mistakes.** "Hi FNAME" or the wrong company name tells them it's a blast. Read every message before it goes.

## Cold email rules: CAN-SPAM, Gmail and the EU

In the US, the CAN-SPAM Act sets the rules for commercial email, and the [FTC's compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) says it makes no exception for business-to-business email. The main rules, as of November 2026:

- Don't use false or misleading header information: From, To and Reply-To must identify you.
- Don't use deceptive subject lines.
- Identify the message as an ad, clearly and conspicuously.
- Include a valid physical postal address: a street address, a PO box registered with the Postal Service, or a private mailbox registered with a commercial mail receiving agency.
- Explain how to opt out, and keep the opt-out working for at least 30 days after you send.
- Honor opt-outs within 10 business days, without a fee and without asking for more than an email address.
- You stay responsible when someone else sends for you.

The FTC puts the penalty at up to $53,088 for each separate email in violation.

Delivery is a separate gate. Google's [email sender guidelines](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/81126) require anyone sending to Gmail accounts to set up SPF or DKIM for the sending domain and to keep the spam rate reported in Postmaster Tools below 0.3%. Send a handful of messages a day from a domain you have set up properly, not a blast from a new one.

For prospects in the EU, take more care: if the address came from a list or a tool, the [European Commission's guidance](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/legal-grounds-processing-data/can-data-received-third-party-be-used-marketing_en) says you must tell people by your first message that you hold their data to send them adverts, and stop if they object; marketing email also falls under the ePrivacy Directive's rules. None of this is legal advice.
