**TL;DR:** AI lead scoring ranks leads by how likely they are to buy. Predictive models learn from your past wins and losses, so they need history first: Microsoft's version wants at least 40 qualified and 40 disqualified leads closed in the training window, and HubSpot's sits on Enterprise plans. A language model can instead check each lead against a written ideal customer profile and say why it fits. Until you have that history, score fit with a reason, not a number.

---

## How AI lead scoring works: three kinds of score

Three methods hide behind the term, and only two use AI. They differ in what they learn from and whether you can see why a lead scored the way it did.

| | Rules-based points | Predictive model | LLM fit check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learns from | Points you assign by hand | Your closed leads, won and lost | A profile you write in plain words |
| Needs | A guess at what matters | Dozens to hundreds of closed outcomes | Public facts about each lead |
| Returns | A total, such as 72 | A probability or a tier | A verdict and a reason |
| Can you see why? | Yes, it's your arithmetic | Often not | Yes, if you ask for the source |
| Best for | Teams that know their buyer well | Teams with history and steady volume | Small teams and new markets |

**Rules-based scoring** isn't AI. You give 10 points for the right title and 5 for a pricing-page visit, and the total decides who gets a call. It's transparent and it's only as smart as your guesses.

**Predictive lead scoring** is machine learning on your own history. [HubSpot's predictive lead scoring](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/properties/determine-likelihood-to-close-with-predictive-lead-scoring) gives each contact a "Likelihood to close": the percentage chance it becomes a customer within 90 days. A second property sorts contacts into four tiers of 25% each, from Very High to Low. As of November 2026 it requires Marketing Hub or Sales Hub Enterprise.

**An LLM fit check** is the newest kind. A language model reads what is public about a lead (size, industry, open roles, tools named in job posts, recent changes) against your profile, and answers fits, doesn't fit or unsure, with the fact behind the verdict. It needs no history, which is why it suits small teams.

## What data an AI lead scoring model needs

A predictive model needs outcomes, and plenty of them. [Microsoft's predictive lead scoring](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics365/sales/configure-predictive-lead-scoring) in Dynamics 365 Sales needs at least 40 qualified and 40 disqualified leads, created and closed within the training window you pick, which runs from three months to two years. Microsoft adds that more leads give better predictions.

The inputs matter as much as the count. HubSpot's model reads page views, site visits, email opens and clicks, form submissions, meetings, logged notes and lifecycle stages, plus firmographics about each contact's company. Each input has to be recorded the same way across the team. If half the reps never log a lost deal, the model learns from a distorted history.

An LLM fit check needs three things instead:

1. **A written profile** with must-haves, deal-breakers and nice-to-haves, each one a fact you could check in public.
2. **Public facts per lead**, gathered with sources. The routine in [AI for account research](/blog/ai-account-research) produces them in about 15 minutes per account.
3. **A test set** of 20 or so past leads whose outcome you know, to check the verdicts before you trust them.

## Where predictive lead scoring breaks

Predictive scores fail in predictable ways. Five come up most:

- **Too little history.** Below the minimum, there is no model. Near it, a few odd deals move every score.
- **It learns your past, bias included.** If you only ever sold to agencies, agencies score high, and the market you want to open scores low because you never sold there.
- **Attention passes for intent.** Opens, clicks and page views measure attention. A student researching your category can outscore a buyer who never opens email.
- **Nobody can explain the number.** HubSpot calls its model blackbox machine learning: you can see the inputs and the output, not how one became the other. Reps stop trusting a score they can't question.
- **Drift.** When your offer or your buyer changes, the model keeps scoring for the old one until enough new outcomes pile up.

None of these is a reason to avoid predictive scoring once you have the volume. Each is a reason to keep a human reading the top and bottom of the list.

## Fit before score: an AI lead scoring model for small teams

Until your CRM holds well over the history a predictive model asks for, ask two questions instead of computing one number: does this company fit, and is there a reason to talk now? AI handles both, and you can audit each answer.

Take a hypothetical. Say you run a video production company that makes product demo videos for B2B software companies. Your profile might read:

- **Must-haves:** B2B software, 50 to 500 employees, a marketing team of at least three.
- **Deal-breakers:** a consumer product, or headquarters outside the US.
- **Nice-to-haves:** a product launch in the last six months, an open video or product marketing role.

Then give the model one instruction per lead:

> Our ideal customer: [must-haves]. Deal-breakers: [list]. Nice-to-haves: [list]. For the company below, answer fits, doesn't fit or unsure. Give one sentence that names the fact behind the verdict and its source. If a must-have can't be confirmed, answer unsure and say which one.

Test it on 20 past leads, 10 that bought and 10 that didn't. If lost leads keep coming back "fits", a deal-breaker is missing from the profile. If buyers come back "doesn't fit", a must-have is too strict. Fix the profile, not the prompt.

Timing is the second question, and it shouldn't be blended into the fit score. A company that fits and just posted a job for a product marketing manager has a reason to talk this month. One that fits and shows no change can wait. [Buying signals for B2B](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) lists the signals that answer "why now", and [intent data](/blog/intent-data) covers what third-party feeds add.

## AI lead scoring tools and what each requires

Where the score lives depends on where your leads live. As of November 2026:

- **HubSpot:** predictive Likelihood to close and Contact priority, on Marketing Hub or Sales Hub Enterprise.
- **Dynamics 365 Sales:** predictive lead scoring once you meet the 40-and-40 minimum. A Sales Enterprise license can turn it on from quick setup for 1,500 scored records a month.
- **Apollo:** AI lead scoring inside its database. The Free and Basic plans cap it, and Professional, at $79 per seat a month billed annually, makes it unlimited ([Apollo pricing](https://www.apollo.io/pricing)).
- **Common Room:** AI scoring on top of its signals. Its plans start at $2,500 a month billed annually.
- **A general LLM:** Claude or ChatGPT running the fit-check prompt above, on your own list.

[AI for sales prospecting](/blog/ai-for-sales-prospecting) places scoring in the rest of the prospecting week, from list building to the first message.
