**TL;DR:** Sell a switch, not an addition. Marketing budgets are flat, and marketers already choose among some 15,500 martech products, so a new tool has to replace something or prove its return fast. The rest of how to sell to marketing teams follows from that: reach marketing ops before the CMO, clear the privacy review early, and time your approach to a change you can see in public, such as a marketing ops hire, a job post naming the tool you replace, or a new CMO.

This is for founders and first sales hires at martech startups. The [B2B SaaS first-customers playbook](/blog/how-to-get-first-customers-b2b-saas) covers the basics this builds on, and [our martech startups page](/for/martech-startups) shows these signals as example Plays.

## Why marketing teams are a hard sale in 2026

Your buyer is choosing among thousands of tools with a budget that didn't grow. Two numbers set the scene:

- **Flat budgets.** [Gartner's 2026 CMO Spend Survey](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-11-gartner-2026-cmo-spend-survey-finds-cmos-allocate-15-point-3-percent-of-marketing-budgets-to-ai-but-only-30-percent-are-ready-to-scale-ai-capabilities) put marketing budgets at 7.8% of company revenue in 2026, up from 7.7% in 2025. The sample was 401 marketing leaders, most at companies with more than $1 billion in revenue, so treat it as the view from large companies.
- **A crowded shelf.** [chiefmartec's 2026 count](https://chiefmartec.com/?p=6211) found 15,505 marketing technology products, up 0.79% in a year, with 1,488 added and 1,367 removed.

A flat budget means every new line item needs an old one to cut, or a return the CFO accepts. A crowded shelf means your category has been pitched to this buyer before. And the buyer writes campaigns for a living, so a templated first message reads as one at a glance.

## Who signs, who evaluates, and who can stop a martech deal

The CMO or VP Marketing signs, but marketing operations decides whether your tool works, and three other teams can stop it.

- **CMO or VP Marketing.** Signs the contract and answers for pipeline and budget. Wants the one-page case, not the demo.
- **Marketing operations.** Runs the evaluation, owns integrations and data, and maintains the tool after you leave. Their technical "no" ends a deal.
- **Demand gen, lifecycle and content managers.** The daily users and your likeliest champions.
- **RevOps or sales ops.** Own the CRM. Anything your tool writes there goes through them.
- **IT, security and privacy.** Single sign-on, data access, data processing terms and consent.
- **Procurement.** Arrives at contract, sooner at large companies.

At a startup, the head of marketing may play every one of these parts. At a large company, expect each to be a different person with a different calendar.

## How to sell to marketing teams at the moment their stack changes

Marketing teams buy when something changes: a person, a tool, a budget, or a public complaint. Each change leaves a trace, and each trace suggests a first question that pitches nothing:

| Signal | What it suggests | Where you see it | First question to ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| A marketing ops or automation manager opening | The stack needs an owner, or a rebuild | Job posts | "Is the new hire there to run the current stack, or to rebuild it?" |
| A job post naming the tool you replace | The incumbent is in place, and its renewal is a live question | Job posts | "Your posting asks for Marketo. Is it still running your nurture programs, or mostly one-off sends?" |
| A funded startup's first marketing hire | A stack gets chosen from scratch | Funding news and job posts | "Will your first marketer pick the tools, or inherit them?" |
| A new CMO or VP Marketing | A review of agencies, tools and budget | Announcements, tracked by hand | "What's the first thing you want to measure differently?" |
| Posts about broken reporting or a stack change | The pain is public | Social posts | "Which report took your team longest to build this quarter?" |
| Engagement with a rival vendor's posts | Interest in your category | Social posts | "How does your team decide which channel gets credit for a deal today?" |

Two rules for that last row. Never name the rival or the post they reacted to, and ask about their problem rather than their browsing. ## A new CMO is a stack review with a start date

Leadership changes are a steady source of timing in marketing. [Spencer Stuart's 2026 study](https://www.spencerstuart.com/research-and-insight/cmo-tenure-2026-snapshot-of-an-expanding-role-for-marketing-leaders) puts average CMO tenure at S&P 500 companies at 4.1 years, against 5.0 years for the C-suite overall, based on 346 CMOs in post on June 30, 2025.

A new CMO inherits a stack and an agency roster they didn't choose. The review that follows is your opening, and the marketing ops hire they make next is the confirmation. Give them a few weeks to learn what's broken before you write, then ask about their plan, not your product. Write to their marketing ops lead in the same week, since that's who will run the evaluation.

## The privacy review every marketing tool faces

Marketing data is personal data: email addresses, cookies, device IDs and ad audiences. Expect a privacy and security review that asks:

1. What personal data your tool collects, where it is stored, and which sub-processors touch it.
2. Whether you sell or share data with ad platforms, and how you honor opt-outs. Under California law, a covered business that sells or shares personal information [must honor the Global Privacy Control signal](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa) as a valid request to stop.
3. How you handle deletion and access requests from the customer's own customers.
4. What your tool writes into the CRM, and who can change those fields. RevOps will ask this one.

Answer in writing, on a page marketing ops can forward. A privacy question answered in a week keeps the deal warm, while one answered in a month starts it over.

## Where marketers trade notes on tools

Marketers ask other marketers before they shortlist anything. Three places matter most for a martech startup:

- **User groups of the tools they run.** [HubSpot's community programs](https://events.hubspot.com/) include local HubSpot User Groups, or HUGs. Users of the platform you integrate with, or replace, meet there.
- **Agencies and consultants.** They build stacks for clients. One who likes your product recommends it in every build, which makes a partner program worth starting early.
- **Integration marketplaces.** A listing in the app marketplace of the platform you plug into puts you where its users look for add-ons.
