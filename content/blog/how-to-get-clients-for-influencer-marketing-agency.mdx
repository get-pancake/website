**TL;DR:** The easiest brands to sign already pay creators, and you can see them: Meta's Ad Library has a searchable database of active, public branded content on Facebook and Instagram with a paid partnership label. Next come brands hiring an influencer or creator partnerships manager, and brands posting about creator or ambassador programs. Win them with a small paid test measured in sales or sign-ups, and a compliance process that takes FTC risk off the brand's plate.

Influencer work sells to a narrower buyer than most agency services, so how to get clients for an influencer marketing agency comes down to finding brands with a creator budget before they go looking. This guide covers who buys, seven channels ranked, how to spot the brands already spending, and how to make the FTC's rules part of your pitch. The ranking for agencies in general is in [how to get clients for a marketing agency](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-marketing-agency).

## Who buys influencer marketing from an agency

Three kinds of buyers sign most influencer agency contracts. Know which one you're pitching before you write.

- **Consumer brands with a creator budget.** Beauty, food and drink, fashion, apps, games. The buyer is a brand manager, a head of social or a growth lead. They judge you on sales, sign-ups and content they can reuse in ads.
- **B2B brands trying creators.** Some software and fintech companies pay creators who post for founders, marketers or engineers. The buyer is a head of brand, demand gen or community, often running creators for the first time. They need help choosing creators and tying posts to pipeline.
- **Agencies without a creator team.** PR firms, paid social shops and full-service agencies take briefs that include creators they can't staff. They buy your capacity under their own brand.

Two kinds of brands rarely pay off: those that want gifting only, with no budget for creator fees, and those with no way to measure a sale or a sign-up.

## 1. Brands that already pay creators

A brand that paid creators last quarter has a budget, a habit and often a gap you can name. Find them before you write:

- **Meta's Ad Library.** Besides ads, it holds [a searchable database of active, public branded content](https://transparency.meta.com/researchtools/ad-library-tools/) on Facebook and Instagram that carries a paid partnership label. Search the top brands in your category and note who they pay, how often, and in which formats.
- **YouTube's disclosure label.** [YouTube's help center](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/154235) requires creators to flag paid promotion in the video details, which adds a disclosure label at the start of the video. Each label on a channel in your category names a brand that pays creators.
- **Your own creators' sponsors.** The creators you'd recommend already work with brands, and their sponsored posts are a list of buyers who chose creators like them.

Read what you find for a pattern. A brand that paid ten creators for the same unboxing format, a brand that stopped after one burst, a brand whose closest rival just started: each one hands you a first question. "Your last eight creator posts were all unboxings. Has any format beaten them on sales?"

## 2. Brands hiring an influencer or creator partnerships manager

A job post for an influencer marketing manager, a creator partnerships lead or a UGC manager means creators have a line in the budget. The person hiring is usually the head of brand, social or growth: write to them, not the recruiter. [Companies hiring](/plays/companies-hiring) shows how to run this as a search, and [job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) covers which roles point to which purchase.

The brand may build in-house, so offer something that fits around the hire:

- **Before the hire starts:** run the first program while the search goes on, so the new manager inherits results and a creator shortlist.
- **After the hire starts:** cover what one person can't, such as sourcing at volume, contracts and usage rights, and reporting.

Read the post as a brief. The platforms it names, the goal it states ("drive TikTok Shop sales" or "build awareness with Gen Z") and whether the role manages agencies tell you what to pitch.

## 3 and 4. Brands talking about creators in public

Marketers post when they plan: "launching our first ambassador program," "looking for UGC creators," "what worked for your creator program?" Each post is a dated brief from someone with a budget. The person asking what worked is still deciding, which makes them the best one to answer. Pancake can search for posts with phrases like these; [Pancake for marketing agencies](/for/marketing-agencies) shows the setup.

A slower version of the same signal: marketers who comment on posts by creator-economy writers and analysts. They're learning. Write to them about the question they raised, and keep the pitch for later.

Answer in the thread first if you can add something useful, then follow up with a short direct message. Never paste your roster. To decide which of these leads deserve a proposal, use the five qualifying questions in [lead generation for marketing agencies](/blog/lead-generation-for-marketing-agencies).

## 5. Referrals from agencies next to yours

Three kinds of agencies meet brands that need creators:

- **PR firms** get asked for creators as part of launches, and some would rather hand that piece to a specialist.
- **Paid social agencies** need creator content to feed ads, and managing creators isn't their trade.
- **E-commerce agencies** that build stores for consumer brands see the launch calendar before anyone else.

Offer each one a clear choice: a referral fee, or white-label creator work billed to the agency. Put non-solicitation terms in writing either way, so neither side approaches the other's clients.

## 6 and 7. Proof, and rooms full of buyers

**Case studies measured in sales.** Brand leads have seen plenty of reach screenshots. A case study that moves them states the goal, how you chose the creators, the cost, and the result in the brand's terms: sales, sign-ups, cost per acquisition, content reused in ads. One measured program in a category beats ten logos.

**Category trade shows.** Creator events are full of creators. Brands gather at the shows for their own category: beauty, natural products, toys, consumer tech. Book meetings with brands from your Ad Library list before you go.

## Put compliance in the proposal

Brands carry legal risk in creator work, and an agency that manages it is easier to hire. Three FTC points to build into your process, as of November 2026:

- **The brand stays responsible.** The FTC's [Endorsement Guides FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking) says advertisers need reasonable programs to train and monitor the influencers in their network, and that delegating part of a promotional program to an outside company doesn't relieve them of responsibility.
- **The disclosure goes in the post.** [Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) asks for the disclosure with the endorsement itself, in plain words such as "ad" or "sponsored," and warns against assuming a platform's disclosure tool is good enough on its own. In a video, it belongs in the video, not only in the description.
- **Bought followers are a legal problem.** A rule [the FTC announced in August 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials) bans buying or selling fake indicators of social media influence, such as followers or views from bots or hijacked accounts, when the buyer knew or should have known they were fake and uses them to misrepresent influence for a commercial purpose.

Turn this into one page of your proposal: the disclosure wording each creator will use, how you check posts after they go live, and how you screen audiences for bought followers. A brand manager can forward that page to their legal team as it stands. This isn't legal advice.
