**TL;DR:** Staffing clients are won one site at a time, through the operations, warehouse and plant managers who own the shifts. You find them through volume hiring, seasonal peaks and new sites, and you pitch before the peak, not during it. The first order is usually a trial shift. Fill it on time and safely, report the numbers, and the account grows from there. That is how to get clients for a staffing agency, and it differs from permanent recruitment at almost every step.

This guide covers temp, contract and volume staffing. For permanent placement, start with [how to get clients for a recruitment agency](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-recruitment-agency).

## How to get clients for a staffing agency: why the sale differs

A permanent search sells one hire for one fee. Staffing sells hours, week after week, for as long as the client needs them, so the buyer, the trigger and the test are all different.

| | Permanent recruitment | Temp, contract and volume staffing |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer | Hiring manager, founder, talent team | Operations, site, warehouse or plant manager; HR; procurement |
| Trigger | One hard role | Shifts to cover, a peak, a new site, turnover |
| Decision time | Weeks | Days, sometimes hours |
| What they compare | Candidate quality and fee | Fill rate, speed, bill rate, safety record |
| Contract | Terms of business per placement | A master service agreement and a rate card |
| Revenue | A fee per hire | Billed hours, for as long as the order runs |

The volume is large. The [American Staffing Association's figures](https://americanstaffing.net/research/fact-sheets-analysis-staffing-industry-trends/staffing-industry-statistics/) count nearly 2.0 million temporary and contract employees working for US staffing companies in an average week of 2025, and 9.5 million hired over the year. Industrial jobs made up the largest share of staffing employees, at 36%.

## Who signs for temp labor

Three people decide, and the order you meet them matters.

- **Operations and site managers.** They own the output and the shift schedule. An unstaffed line or a dock short of pickers is their problem tonight, so they give the first yes, usually to a trial order.
- **HR managers at mid-size companies.** They own onboarding, compliance and the contract. Bring them in once the site manager wants you; they sign the master service agreement.
- **Procurement and program managers at large employers.** They buy through a supplier program with approved agencies and set rates. More on that below.

At a company with one plant, the plant manager and HR decide together. At a company with twenty warehouses, win one site manager and earn the next site with that site's numbers.

## Signals that a site needs workers

The strongest signal is a company trying to hire hourly workers itself, at volume, and falling behind. Each of these shows up in public, often before the company calls an agency:

- **Volume posts.** Several posts for the same hourly role at one address, such as forklift operators, pickers or machine operators. The company has a need and is trying to fill it alone.
- **Reposts every few weeks.** The same role, posted again and again, points to turnover, or to a pay rate that draws too few applicants. Either way, the site is short.
- **New shifts.** "Second shift" or "weekend shift" in a job title means added capacity, and added capacity needs people.
- **New sites.** A distribution center, plant or store needs a workforce before opening day. Watch press releases, local business news and economic development announcements, then the job posts in the new city.
- **A peak on the calendar.** Covered in the next section.

Read the post itself for the details that size the order: the shift times, the pay rate, the start date, the number of openings if it is listed. [Job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) goes deeper on reading posts as a seller. Employer job posts are also where [Pancake for recruiting agencies](/for/recruiting-agencies) looks first, and the same search works for hourly roles.

## Sell before the peak

The order for a peak is placed before the peak, so the sale happens a season ahead. For the 2026 holidays, [the National Retail Federation expects retailers to hire between 325,000 and 375,000 seasonal workers](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/nrf-forecasts-2026-holiday-sales-growth-as-consumers-remain-resilient). NRF published that forecast on October 15, while retailers were already hiring.

Retail is one calendar among several. Map your clients' peaks before you plan outreach:

- **Retail and parcel delivery:** the fourth quarter.
- **Food processing and agriculture:** the harvest months for each crop.
- **Accounting and administrative temps:** tax season.
- **Events and hospitality:** summer and conference season.

Pitch a fourth-quarter peak in late summer, with a plan the manager can approve: crew size, shifts, start dates and how you replace a worker who doesn't show. When the peak ends, ask to keep the best workers on, and agree the date for next year's planning call.

## Getting onto supplier programs at large employers

Large employers often buy temp labor through a managed service provider (MSP), which runs the supplier program, and a vendor management system (VMS), the software that sends each order to approved suppliers. Inside a program, you don't sell to the site manager. You get approved, then compete on each order.

1. **Find who runs the program.** The MSP's program manager, not the site.
2. **Ask when supplier onboarding opens** and what it requires, such as insurance, safety records and the area you cover.
3. **Pitch a gap.** A role or shift the current suppliers fill slowly is the best case for adding one more.
4. **Win on the numbers.** Once you are in, speed and fill rate on each order decide your share of the next ones.

Expect thinner margins inside a program than with a direct client, since the program sets the rates. Some agencies run both: programs for volume, direct site accounts for margin.

## Safety belongs in the pitch

Every plant or warehouse manager weighs safety before saying yes, and you share the duty. [OSHA's guidance on temporary workers](https://www.osha.gov/temporaryworkers) says the staffing agency and the host employer are joint employers, both responsible for a safe workplace. It recommends that the two set out who handles which safety duties in their contract, and it says agencies have a duty to look into the conditions at the sites where they send workers.

Turn that into a sales step. Offer a walk of the floor before the first order, ask which training the site gives and which it expects from you, and write the split into the agreement. The manager sees an agency that takes the site's hazards seriously, and you learn what the job involves before your workers arrive.

## Win the first order, then the site

A trial order is the cheapest way for a manager to test you, so make it easy to say yes to.

1. **Ask small.** One shift, one line, or a crew of five for two weeks.
2. **Confirm arrivals before each shift starts,** and replace a no-show within the same shift.
3. **Send a one-page report at the end of each week:** shifts ordered, filled, on time, replaced.
4. **After four clean weeks, ask for more:** the next shift, the weekend crew, or an introduction to the next site's manager.

The report is your sales material for the next site. A manager at a sister site trusts a colleague's numbers more than your brochure.
