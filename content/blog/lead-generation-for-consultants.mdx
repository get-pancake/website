**TL;DR:** Start from events, not lists. Your best consulting leads are people caught at the moment the problem shows: a company hiring for the role you would otherwise fill, an executive posting about the problem you fix, a startup that closed a round last month. Pick the three or four triggers that came before your best past engagements, give the search three fixed hours a week, and write to the person who owns the problem while the event is fresh. Add a five-number tracking sheet and a habit of keeping every "not now" warm, and that is lead generation for consultants that runs around billable work.

This is the system behind the outbound channel in [our ranked guide to getting consulting clients](/blog/how-to-get-consulting-clients). Referrals and partners stay your base. The routine below fills the gaps between them.

---

## What counts as a consulting lead

A consulting lead is a named person who owns the problem you fix, at a company that fits your practice, with a visible reason to act soon. Miss any of the three and you have a contact, not a lead.

Grade each one before you write:

- **Ready.** They named the problem and a time frame: "We have to fix the forecast before the board meeting in March."
- **Warm.** There is a signal, such as a job post or a funding round, but no stated pain yet.
- **Fit only.** Right size, industry and title, with no signal at all.

Ready and warm leads get a message this week. A fit-only lead goes on a watch list until something happens at that company.

## Start from the triggers behind your past engagements

Your best list of signals is your own client history. Write down your last five engagements and, next to each, the event that came right before the first call.

Say you run a two-person revenue operations practice. Your last five clients looked like this: two had hired a first VP of Sales, one had raised a Series B, one was moving to a new CRM, and one had a board member who asked for a forecast the team couldn't produce. That gives you four triggers to watch: a new sales leader, a funding round, a CRM named in job posts, and executives posting about forecast accuracy.

Then pin down who you sell to: company size, industry, region and the title that signs. "VPs of Sales at US software companies with 100 to 500 people" is a profile you can search. "Growing companies" is not.

## Eight signals that produce consulting leads

Each signal below says something different about timing. The last column is a first question that fits it, with no pitch.

| Signal | What it suggests | Where to see it | A first question that fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| An executive posts about the problem | The pain is on their mind this week | Public posts on the platforms your buyers use | "What have you tried so far?" |
| They engage with a known voice in your field | They are reading about the problem | Comments under posts by writers your buyers follow | "Did that post match what you see at your company?" |
| A job post for the role you would fill | Budget, and a gap until the hire lands | Job boards and careers pages | "Is the role there to fix something, or to run it once fixed?" |
| A job post naming a tool you know | A project on that tool, now or soon | The requirements section of job posts | "Is the move to that tool done, or still underway?" |
| A funding round | New targets and a board asking how | SEC Form D filings, press releases | "What does the round have to fix first?" |
| A new leader in the seat | A 90-day plan in the making | Press releases, company news, by hand | "What did you inherit that you would change first?" |
| A lookalike of your best client | Likely the same problem, not yet named | Your client list, company databases | "How do you handle the problem today?" |
| An expansion: new market, new site, acquisition | Integration and process work | Press releases, job posts in a new city | "Who owns the integration?" |

Funding is the easiest signal to check at the source. A US company raising money under Regulation D [must file a Form D within 15 days after the first sale](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/capital-raising-building-blocks/what-form-d), and the filing is public on the SEC's EDGAR system. [How to find recently funded startups](/blog/how-to-find-recently-funded-startups) covers the other sources and the timing after a round.

Hiring is the richest. A post for an interim head of operations or a pricing manager tells you the budget exists and who will feel the gap. [Searching companies by the roles they are hiring for](/plays/companies-hiring) and [by the tools named in their job posts](/plays/tech-stack) turns both into lists you can review, and [our page for consulting practices](/for/consultants) shows those signals set up for a specialty. For more triggers and how to act on each, see [the guide to B2B buying signals](/blog/buying-signals-b2b).

## Lead generation for consultants in three hours a week

The routine fits around billable work because it runs on fixed blocks, not on spare time. Put the blocks in your calendar as meetings with yourself.

1. **Monday, 60 minutes: review.** Go through the new signals from your saved searches. Grade each lead ready, warm or fit only. Pick the ten best.
2. **Tuesday, 45 minutes: write.** One first message per lead, each asking one question about its signal. No deck, no case study, no calendar link.
3. **Thursday, 45 minutes: follow up.** Send one follow-up to anyone who hasn't answered in a week, with something new: a short observation, not "bumping this." Replies don't wait for Thursday; answer those within a working day.
4. **Friday, 30 minutes: update the sheet.** Log what happened. Move each "not now" to a dated follow-up.

Ten leads a week is a starting point, not a quota. A narrow specialty may only produce five good ones, and five good ones beat thirty weak ones.

## The tracking sheet and the five numbers

A spreadsheet is enough. One row per lead: name, company, signal, date seen, date of first message, reply, call booked, proposal sent, signed, next touch.

Read five numbers on Fridays: leads reviewed, messages sent, replies, calls booked and proposals sent. Each gap points at one fix.

- **Few replies:** the signal is weak or the first question is generic. Rewrite the question before you send more.
- **Replies, but few calls:** the offer is vague. Name one problem, one result and one time frame.
- **Calls, but few proposals:** you are talking to people without the budget. Grade harder on Monday.

Say a month gives you 40 leads reviewed, 30 messages, 2 replies and 1 call. The leak sits between messages and replies, so the fix is a stronger signal or a sharper question, not more sending.

---

## Keep the "not now" leads warm

A "not now" with a reason is a dated lead. If someone says the project waits until after the board meeting, set the next touch for the week after it, and open with the board meeting.

Between touches, send one useful thing per quarter: a finding from your work, a benchmark, a two-line note on something that changed in their market. If those notes go out as marketing email from your own inbox, the [FTC's CAN-SPAM rules](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) apply, and they make no exception for business-to-business email: include your postal address and an opt-out, and honor opt-outs within 10 business days.

## What a purchased consulting lead list can't tell you

A bought list answers the wrong question. It tells you who fits your profile, not who has a reason to talk this quarter. So every message on it has to invent a reason, and anyone else who bought the same list is inventing the same ones.

Lists also age. Titles and emails go stale as people change jobs, and the older the list, the more of it points at people who have moved on. Use data to find the right person at a company that already shows a signal. Don't use it as the reason to write.
