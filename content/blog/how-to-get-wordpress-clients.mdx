**TL;DR:** Pick buyers who can pay for upkeep, then sell them the upkeep. WordPress runs [39.5% of all websites](https://w3techs.com/technologies/details/cm-wordpress), so builders are everywhere and one-off builds get priced down. B2B companies whose job posts name WordPress or WooCommerce, or who are hiring a website manager, have a team and a budget. Win the build, sign a care plan in the same proposal, and join the Automattic and WP Engine partner programs early so your hosting revenue counts toward their lead tiers.

Most of the work in getting WordPress clients is choosing which ones. Below: how to get WordPress clients who pay for the site and for its upkeep, from the partner directories and Codeable to the care plan model and the job posts to watch. ## Why WordPress work skews cheap, and how to stay out of that end

The pool is huge, so the bottom of the market is crowded. W3Techs counts WordPress on 58.2% of the sites whose CMS it can identify, as of November 2026. Themes and page builders let almost anyone ship a five-page site, and that kind of site sells on price.

The budget sits with companies that need the site to keep working after launch. Three filters keep you there:

- **Someone owns the site.** A marketing lead or website manager answers for it.
- **The site makes or supports money.** A store, a lead form, a recruiting page, a content program.
- **There is a reason to act now.** A launch, a new hire, a hack, a migration, a funding round.

A post asking for "a simple 5-page site, cheapest quote" fails all three. Pass on it. Our guide to [higher-paying web design clients](/blog/how-to-get-high-paying-web-design-clients) covers the buyers above that line.

## WordPress partner programs that send work

Two programs matter most for studios, and both reward revenue you already bring them. As of November 2026:

**[Automattic for Agencies](https://automattic.com/for-agencies/)** calls itself the official WordPress partner program. It has no minimum to join and no exclusivity. Its tiers run Activated, Partner, Pro Partner, VIP Pro Partner and Premier Partner. From the Partner tier you get listings in Automattic's directories on WooCommerce.com, WordPress.com, Pressable.com and Jetpack.com. Qualified leads and an assigned partner manager start at Pro Partner. It also pays recurring commissions of up to 50% on products you refer, and Automattic says it has delivered over $6 million in pre-qualified projects to agency partners since June 2024.

**The [WP Engine Agency Partner Program](https://wpengine.com/agency-partner-program/)** is free to join. Its tiers follow the hosting you refer:

| WP Engine tier | What it takes | Commission per referred plan | Agency Directory |
|---|---|---|---|
| Member | No minimum | 8% | No listing |
| Preferred | 3 referral submissions | 8% | Listing |
| Advanced | $1,200 of referred monthly revenue | 10% | Optimized listing |
| Strategic | $5,000 of referred monthly revenue | 12% | Highest-ranking listing, plus lead referrals |

Commissions recur for 12 months per referral, and every tier includes one free agency hosting account to build and demo sites. Lead referrals come only at Strategic.

Read both programs the same way: a listing comes early, and the leads go to studios that already bring the host a lot of revenue. So join early, while it costs nothing, and put new client sites on one host so your referred revenue adds up in one place.

## Codeable and the marketplaces

Codeable is a freelance marketplace for WordPress work. Its [vetting process](https://help.codeable.io/en/articles/1143592-codeable-expert-vetting-process) runs from an application and a coding quiz to a code project and a live interview. Clients arrive with a defined task: a WooCommerce fix, a custom plugin, a migration. That suits a studio filling the gaps between builds. You work inside Codeable's process and rules, so keep your own pipeline running beside it.

Upwork and Fiverr reach every budget, the lowest included. Use them for one narrow specialty with a clear outcome, such as WooCommerce subscription migrations, rather than "WordPress websites."

## Care plans: how to win and keep WordPress maintenance clients

A care plan turns one build into recurring revenue, and WordPress hands you the reason to sell it. [Patchstack counted 11,334 new vulnerabilities](https://patchstack.com/whitepaper/state-of-wordpress-security-in-2026/) in the WordPress ecosystem in 2025, up 42% on 2024, and 91% of them sat in plugins. Its report puts the median time to mass exploitation of heavily exploited flaws at 5 hours. Someone has to watch the plugins, and it shouldn't be the marketing manager.

One example of how to shape it: a Basic plan covers core and plugin updates, backups and uptime monitoring. A Growth plan adds a fixed number of content edits a month. A Priority plan adds a faster response time when something breaks. Price each tier by what it covers and how fast you answer, not by the hour.

Three rules make the model work:

1. **Sell the plan in the proposal.** Fold the first three months into the project price, so the plan starts at launch instead of after a hard conversation.
2. **Write the scope.** List what's included, the response times and what costs extra, like new features or a redesign.
3. **Run hosting in one place.** Updates and backups go faster on one stack, and your referred revenue counts toward a partner tier.

Maintenance clients also come from sites someone else built. Offer the owner a paid audit, then the plan. And agencies that build but don't maintain can resell your plan under their own name: see [white label web design partners](/blog/white-label-web-design-partners).

## Signals that a company needs a WordPress studio now

These signals point to a team with a budget and a reason to move. Our [page for web design agencies](/for/web-design-agencies) shows how the first two work for B2B studios.

- **Job posts that name WordPress or WooCommerce.** A content manager post asking for WordPress skills says the team publishes there. A WooCommerce role says the store matters.
- **A website manager or web developer role.** The company has more site work than people.
- **Posts about a slow, broken or hacked site.** The owner has a problem today.
- **A new head of marketing.** New leaders tend to look hard at the site in their first months.

A job post shows what the team uses, not what the live site runs on. View the page source and search for "wp-content": WordPress keeps themes and plugins in that folder.

### Migration work runs both ways

- **Into WordPress:** teams whose job posts name an enterprise CMS such as Sitecore or Adobe Experience Manager may want a cheaper stack their marketers can run.
- **Out of WordPress:** if you also build in Webflow, a WordPress team that complains about publishing speed is a rebuild lead. The [Webflow playbook](/blog/how-to-get-webflow-clients) covers that pitch.
- **Within WordPress:** moving a site off an aging page builder, or to a headless setup for teams with engineers.

Price every migration as a fixed project, and start with a content inventory so nothing gets lost.
