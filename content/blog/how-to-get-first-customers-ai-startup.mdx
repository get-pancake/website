**TL;DR:** Sell the job, not the model. Your first customers are teams already paying people to do the work your product does, and they show it in job posts for that work and in posts about the backlog. Win them with a short paid pilot measured on their own files, a written answer to "do you train on our data?", and a security packet that's ready before anyone asks. Plan for two people in every deal: the leader who owns the work and the reviewer who can stop it.

This is for founders selling AI products to businesses: document processing, support automation, agents that take over a back-office workflow. Below: who buys first, the signs of a ready team, a pilot that converts, and the trust questions. For the channels every B2B software startup shares, see the [first-customers playbook for B2B SaaS](/blog/how-to-get-first-customers-b2b-saas).

## Who buys an AI product first

The first buyer owns a manual process and its cost. That cost already sits in a budget as salaries, overtime or an outsourcing contract, so the owner can compare your price with a number they know.

Say your product reads supplier invoices and enters them in the accounting system. Your buyer is the accounts payable manager with three clerks keying invoices, not an innovation team running experiments. The AP manager feels the month-end backlog and can change how the work gets done.

Expect three people in the deal, and you need all three:

- **The owner,** such as a VP of operations or a claims director, who sponsors the purchase and may sign it.
- **The checker,** the team lead who will review the output and decide whether it works.
- **The reviewer,** from security, IT or privacy, who decides whether it's allowed.

Sell only to the owner and the reviewer can stop the deal late. Bring the checker into the pilot from day one, and ask the owner in the first call who handles vendor security reviews.

## Signals that a team is ready for your product

AI buyers leave public traces like any software buyer. The strongest is a company hiring people to do the work your product does. [Job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) covers how to read those posts in detail.

| Signal | What it tells you | First question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Job posts for the manual role: order entry, AP clerk, claims processor | The work is growing, and it's budgeted as headcount | "How many orders does the team enter by hand in a normal week?" |
| Several openings for the same manual role | Volume has outrun the team | "Are these hires for volume you have now, or volume you expect?" |
| Older capture or automation tools, like Kofax or UiPath, named in job posts | The team has automated part of the work already | "Which documents still go around the current tool and get typed in?" |
| Posts about the backlog or manual work | The owner feels the problem this month | "You wrote about the month-end backlog. Which step takes longest?" |
| Posts about running an AI pilot | They're evaluating now, maybe with a rival | "What did the pilot start with, and what would make it a yes?" |
| Engagement with the incumbent vendor's posts | They know the category | "Which part of the current setup does your team still check by hand?" |

Start with the first row. A post for an order entry specialist can list the volume, the systems and the hours: much of what you'd ask on a discovery call. ## The first conversation: ask about the work, not about AI

Buyers have read the same "AI-powered" claims from vendor after vendor. Lead with their process instead: the volume, the error rate, who checks the work, and what happens when it falls behind.

Then ask for files early. "Send me five real documents, and I'll show you the output on our next call" turns a skeptical buyer into an evaluator. Their files beat your demo set, because the hard cases are theirs: the handwritten note on a purchase order, the scanned page upside down.

Expect the trust question in the first call, sometimes before the demo. Have a one-sentence answer ready on where their data goes and whether it's used for training, then offer the written version.

## Pilots that convert: scope, measure, price

A pilot is a sale with a test inside it. Design it so the decision is easy on the agreed date.

1. **One workflow, one team.** "Invoices from the 20 largest suppliers," not "accounts payable automation."
2. **A baseline before day one.** Count how many items arrive per week, the minutes each one takes and the error rate, using their current process.
3. **Their files, in their system.** A pilot on clean sample data proves nothing the buyer cares about.
4. **A length that fits the workflow's cycle.** A month-end process needs two month-ends. Set the decision date at the start.
5. **A price.** Charge something, even if small, and credit it against the first contract. Paying gets you an owner on their side.
6. **Human review stays on.** Their checker reviews the output, and you log every error by type.
7. **Conversion terms in writing.** If the measure is met by the date, the contract starts at the price you agreed before kickoff.

Pilots stall for predictable reasons: no owner, no baseline, no date, or a success measure nobody wrote down. If you can't get all seven, call it an evaluation and give it less of your time.

## The security review: answer it before it arrives

AI products draw questions a plain web app never gets. Write these answers before your first pilot:

- where data is processed and stored, and in which regions;
- which model providers and other sub-processors see customer data;
- whether anyone trains models on that data;
- how long you keep inputs and outputs, and how a customer deletes them;
- who on your team can access customer data, and how that access is logged;
- what happens if your product makes a mistake or a provider has an outage.

Quote your providers' own terms on training. Anthropic, for example, says that by default it does not use inputs or outputs from its [commercial products to train its models](https://privacy.anthropic.com/en/articles/7996868-is-my-data-used-for-model-training), including the Anthropic API, as of November 2026. Check the terms for every provider you use, and list each one as a sub-processor.

For the questionnaire itself, the Cloud Security Alliance publishes an [AI Controls Matrix](https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/artifacts/ai-controls-matrix-v1-1), version 1.1 as of November 2026, with 247 control objectives across 18 security domains. It is free to download and comes with a questionnaire for AI, the AI-CAIQ, that buyers can use to evaluate vendors. Filling it in before anyone asks gives you a ready answer and shows you the gaps, and CSA explains how to submit the self-assessment to its public STAR Registry.

Larger buyers will ask about SOC 2. The [AICPA describes a SOC 2 report](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/audit-assurance/audit-and-assurance-greater-than-soc-2) as an examination of a service organization's controls relevant to security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality or privacy, carried out by a CPA firm. If you don't have one, say so, say when the examination starts, and send what you have: a data-flow diagram, the sub-processor list, your policies and the questionnaire answers. Risk teams may also mention the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework), a voluntary framework released on January 26, 2023. A short note on how your product maps to it helps those reviews.

## Proof that travels inside the buyer's company

Your champion has to sell you to people who never saw the demo. Give them material they can forward:

- **A one-page pilot result:** the baseline, the pilot number, the error types and the hours returned to the team.
- **A two-minute recorded walkthrough** on their own files, with sensitive fields hidden.
- **A reference call** with another customer, once you have one who will take it.

Make every accuracy claim specific: which files, which weeks, which errors were left for a human. "Ninety-something percent accurate" with no files behind it sounds like every other vendor. Negotiate case study rights in the pilot agreement, while the buyer is still excited.

## AI startup go-to-market after the first five

Stay narrow until it repeats. One workflow, one industry, one buyer title, until the path from first message to signed contract looks the same three times in a row. The founder runs those deals, which the [founder-led sales playbook](/blog/founder-led-sales) covers step by step.

Price the way buyers already budget for the work. If they think in clerks and hours, price per document or per case, and show the comparison with the cost of the role. Seat pricing fits when people work alongside the product all day. Widen to a second workflow only once the first sells without you on every call.
