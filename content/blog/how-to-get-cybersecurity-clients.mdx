**TL;DR:** Security buyers hire firms that someone they trust has vouched for, so the first clients come from past clients, audit firms, MSPs without a security bench and the compliance platforms your clients already use. Outbound works when it starts from a goal the buyer stated in public: SOC 2 in a job post, a first security hire, a trust page, a funding round. That is how to get cyber security clients without fear tactics: answer the goal they named, and leave breaches out of your first message.

Below: who buys and why, the rules that create work (stated narrowly), nine channels ranked in one table, the signals worth watching, and six rules for selling without scaring anyone.

## Who buys security work, and what each buyer has to prove

Security buyers fall into four groups, and each wants a different outcome from you.

**Companies that must prove security to their own customers.** A B2B software company with 30 to 300 staff stalls in deals until it can answer a security questionnaire or hand over a SOC 2 report. The buyer is the CTO, the VP of engineering or a first compliance hire. The goal is a clean report by a date a customer set.

**Companies with a rule behind the work.** Public companies, regulated firms and EU entities in covered sectors carry obligations that reach the board. The buyer is a CISO, a general counsel or a CFO, and the goal is a program they can defend.

**Companies building their own team.** A firm hiring its first security engineer, or its first SOC analysts, still buys penetration tests, managed detection, incident response retainers and a fractional CISO while the team grows.

**Small businesses with no security owner.** A 15-person office rarely hires a security firm directly. It buys through its MSP, which makes the MSP your route into that market.

Name the group you serve best before you pick channels. A SOC 2 readiness firm and a 24/7 SOC sell to different people on different calendars.

## Rules that create security work, stated narrowly

Frameworks and rules set deadlines, and deadlines become projects. Quote them with care: your buyers have read the source text, and an overstated rule in a pitch costs you the meeting. As of November 2026:

- **SOC 2.** The AICPA describes SOC as a suite of services that [CPAs may provide on a service organization's controls](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/resources/landing/system-and-organization-controls-soc-suite-of-services), and its SOC 2 guide covers controls relevant to security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality or privacy. A CPA firm issues the report. The readiness work before it, from policies to remediation to a pentest ahead of fieldwork, is where consultancies come in.
- **ISO/IEC 27001.** The 2022 edition [specifies the requirements for an information security management system](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/79694), including how an organization assesses and treats information security risks. When a customer asks for ISO 27001 rather than a SOC 2 report, the company needs a working management system, and setting one up is consulting work.
- **SEC disclosure rules.** Under the SEC's [2023 cybersecurity rules](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2023-139), public companies disclose material cybersecurity incidents on Form 8-K, generally within four business days of determining materiality, and describe their risk management and board oversight in the annual 10-K. That creates work on incident response plans, tabletop exercises and governance.
- **NIS2.** EU Member States had until 17 October 2024 to transpose the [NIS2 Directive](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/nis2-directive). As a rule, medium-sized and large entities in its sectors must take cybersecurity risk-management measures and notify authorities of significant incidents, and the directive makes top management accountable for compliance.

Check the current text before a rule goes into a pitch, and name the regulator or country you mean.

## 1 to 4: borrow trust from people buyers already believe

### 1. Past clients and their peers

Security work comes back. Audit periods repeat, pentests tend to follow them, and a new framework arrives with each new market. Put the next date for every client in your calendar and raise it a quarter ahead: "Your SOC 2 window opens in March. Want the pentest finished before fieldwork starts?"

Ask for introductions by role. CTOs know other CTOs from their investors' portfolios and their former employers. "Which other CTO in your portfolio has a first audit this year?" gets a name. "Know anyone who needs security?" doesn't.

### 2. Audit firms

CPA firms that issue SOC 2 reports meet companies before the audit, often before the controls exist. That makes them a natural source of readiness, policy and pentest work, and you can send audit work back once a client is ready. Keep the roles clean: the auditor tests, you build, and neither of you blurs the line. ISO 27001 certification bodies sit in the same position for management-system work.

Start with two or three audit firms that serve your client size. Offer to send them every client who finishes readiness with you, and ask for nothing until you have sent the first one.

### 3. Compliance platform partner programs

A company that buys a compliance platform still needs someone to set it up, write policies and fix what it flags. Vanta's [service provider program](https://www.vanta.com/partners/service-providers) is aimed at vCISOs, MSPs and MSSPs, with deal registration, joint work with Vanta's sales team, and a public "Find a service provider" directory next to its auditor directory, as of November 2026. Other compliance platforms run their own partner programs, so read each one's terms before you join.

A job post that names the platform is a signal of its own. A company hiring a GRC analyst to "own our Drata instance" has bought the tool and needs the work around it, and [finding companies by the tools in their job posts](/plays/tech-stack) turns that into a search.

### 4. MSPs without a security bench

An MSP looks after the laptops and email of hundreds of small offices, and those offices ask it about phishing, insurance applications and compliance. An MSP that doesn't want to staff a 24/7 SOC or a pentest team needs a partner who does. Offer white-label or co-managed services under clear terms: who owns the client, who answers the 3 a.m. alert, who signs the report. The [playbook for getting MSP clients](/blog/how-to-get-msp-clients) shows how those firms win their own business, which tells you where they need help.

## 5: outreach that starts from a goal the buyer stated

This is how to find cybersecurity clients beyond your network. Signal-based outreach works in security when the signal is a goal, not a weakness. Five public goals are worth watching:

- **Job posts that mention SOC 2 or ISO 27001.** A compliance manager opening, or an engineering post that says "help us achieve SOC 2," puts the audit on this year's plan.
- **A first security engineer or IT manager.** The company is building the function, and the new hire will want pentests, policies and an outside second opinion.
- **A recent funding round.** Money raised to sell to larger customers brings larger customers' security questionnaires. [Recently funded startups](/plays/recently-funded-startups) are easy to list.
- **A trust page that lists SOC 2.** The company has a report and now lives on a cycle: the next audit period, a yearly pentest, perhaps ISO 27001 for a new market. Read the page itself and keep the link in your notes.
- **Posts about security questionnaires.** A founder complaining about a 300-question spreadsheet from a prospect has told you what to offer.

Write to the person who owns the goal: the CTO at a 50-person software company, the head of security or compliance at a larger one. The first message asks about the goal and sells nothing: "Saw that the compliance manager role mentions SOC 2. Is this the first audit, or a new scope on an existing report?"

## 6 to 9: slower channels that compound

### 6. Insurance brokers and law firms

Cyber insurance brokers see the application questions their clients can't answer, and they want the renewal to go through. Privacy and commercial lawyers see contracts with security clauses their clients signed without a plan to meet them. Both can introduce you when the client needs help, before anything has gone wrong. Give each a short checklist they can hand over, and report back after every introduction.

### 7. Talks and local chapters

BSides events and ISSA, ISACA and OWASP chapter meetings fill with practitioners who hire consultants, refer them, or become clients when they change jobs. Give a talk that teaches one thing well, such as how to scope a pentest so the report gets used, or what auditors ask for in a first SOC 2. Leave out the slide about your services; that audience notices.

### 8. Useful public work

Buyers research before they talk to anyone. Publish what helps them decide: a redacted sample pentest report, a readiness checklist by company size, an honest post on how many hours a first SOC 2 takes from the team. Sector news belongs here too. A breach in your clients' industry is a fair subject for an analysis post or a webinar written for everyone, never aimed at the company it happened to.

### 9. Vendor partner programs

Endpoint, SIEM and cloud security vendors run partner programs for resellers and managed providers. They pay off when you already deploy that vendor's product for clients, and they rarely create demand on their own.

## Selling security without fear

Fear can win a click. It never wins the trust a security engagement needs. Security buyers have seen every scare tactic, and they hire firms that behave the way they want an incident responder to behave: calm, exact, discreet. Six rules:

1. **Never mention a breach in a first message.** Not theirs, not a competitor's. If they want to discuss it, they will.
2. **Never test or scan their systems without written permission.** An unsolicited list of their open ports shows the judgment buyers fear most.
3. **Quote the goal they stated.** The SOC 2 line in the job post, the questionnaire they complained about, the trust page they're proud of.
4. **Cite only numbers you can source.** An unsourced statistic about small businesses closing after a breach tells a CTO you don't check facts.
5. **Make the first offer small and useful.** Triage of one questionnaire, a review of their audit scope, an hour on timing.
6. **Respect their calendar.** Audit fieldwork and quarter-end are bad weeks to ask for time.
