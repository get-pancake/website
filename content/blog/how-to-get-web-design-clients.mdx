**TL;DR:** The quickest answer to how to get web design clients is to go back to people who already trust your work: past clients, the people they refer, and agencies that sell the services around a website. After that, pick channels by who you sell to. Local studios win with a Google Business Profile and local referrals. Studios that sell to B2B companies win with outreach to companies that show a need right now, plus a listing in Webflow's or Framer's partner directory.

Below are the 11 channels, ranked and compared in one table, and the three to start with. For a signed project within 30 days, the [30-day plan for getting web design clients fast](/blog/how-to-get-web-design-clients-fast) turns the top of this list into a schedule.

---

## Who you sell to decides which channels work

Two studios can follow the same advice and get opposite results. The difference is the buyer.

**Local businesses.** A dentist, a roofer or a restaurant owner buys a website rarely. They search Google, ask a friend or their accountant, and hire someone nearby. They seldom post jobs or announce a redesign in public, so there is no trace to watch for. Visibility and word of mouth win here.

**B2B companies with 20 to 200 staff.** The buyer is a head of marketing or a founder, and the site is a sales tool. A funding round, a rebrand or a new product line can make it feel out of date, and those moments leave public traces: a job post for a web designer, a post about the redesign, a press release. You can reach these buyers before they ask anyone for quotes. [How to get high-paying web design clients](/blog/how-to-get-high-paying-web-design-clients) covers what moving up to them takes.

Most studios sell to a mix. Rank the channels for the buyer who brings most of your revenue, then build a [repeatable lead flow for your web design agency](/blog/lead-generation-for-web-design-agencies) around the winners.

## Which three channels to start with

An hour a week spread across eleven channels produces nothing. Pick three: one warm, one inbound, one outbound.

- **Local studio:** past clients and referrals, a Google Business Profile with reviews, and a seat in a local business group.
- **B2B studio:** past clients and referrals, two or three partner agencies, and outreach to companies showing a signal.
- **New freelancer with no portfolio:** two self-initiated projects or one pro bono site for a nonprofit, small paid jobs on a marketplace, and a referral ask at the end of each.

The same rule holds if you already have clients and want more web design clients: give each channel six weeks of steady work before you judge it. A channel with no conversations after six weeks needs a different offer or audience, not more hours.

## 1 to 3: the channels that pay back first

### 1. Past clients and their referrals

The cheapest client is one who already trusts you. Past clients need new pages, landing pages, care plans and, sooner or later, a rebuild, and they know other business owners. Ask at the moments they feel best about you: launch day, and the first time the new site brings them a lead or a sale.

Make the ask specific. "Know anyone who needs a website?" gets a polite shrug. "Do you know another clinic owner whose site is older than yours was?" gets a name. Reward the referrer if it fits your market: a flat fee, a share of the first invoice or a free month of care plan. The [web design referral program guide](/blog/web-design-referral-program) has the scripts, the fee options and a tracking sheet.

**Monday task:** list every client from the last three years with the date of their last project. Write to the five oldest with one useful observation about their site and one question.

### 2. Partner agencies

SEO agencies, branding studios, PPC agencies and copywriters often see a website project coming before the client calls a designer. A branding studio that delivers a new identity leaves its client with a site that no longer matches. An SEO agency stuck with a slow theme needs a builder who can fix it.

Two models work. In a referral partnership, each side sends clients to the other, sometimes for a fee. In a [white-label web design partnership](/blog/white-label-web-design-partners), you build under the agency's brand and invoice the agency instead of the end client. White label pays less per site because the agency keeps a margin, but the agency does the selling.

Start with three agencies whose clients look like yours, and offer one small project at your normal rate so they can see how you work.

### 3. Signal-based outreach

Outreach works when it starts from a reason. A company hiring a web designer, a marketing head posting about a rebrand, a startup announcing a round: each says the site is on someone's list this quarter. A message that names the reason reads as relevant; one that doesn't reads as spam.

This is the main outbound channel for B2B studios, and it does little for local ones, since a bakery leaves no such trace. [How to find businesses that need a website](/blog/how-to-find-businesses-that-need-a-website) lists the signals and where to see each. Hiring is the clearest, and [finding companies by the roles they're hiring for](/plays/companies-hiring) shows it as a search. A company with a web designer role open may build in-house, so pitch the build while they hire, or support for the new hire.

The first message asks one question about the signal and pitches nothing: "Congrats on the Series A. Is the website part of the plan for the next six months, or does it wait?" A portfolio link in message one usually ends the conversation. [Web design cold outreach templates](/blog/web-design-cold-outreach) has first messages for each signal, plus follow-ups.

## 4 to 7: inbound that builds over months

### 4. Google Business Profile and local search

For a studio that sells to local businesses, this is the first inbound channel to set up. A Business Profile is how you show up in Google Maps and in the map results on Search, and [creating one is free](https://business.google.com/us/business-profile/).

Fill in every field and pick the category that matches what owners search for. Ask every local client for a review the week their site launches, and reply to each review. Then give each service and each town you cover its own page on your site, with a real project from that town on it.

For a B2B studio this channel matters less, because its buyers rarely limit the search to studios nearby.

### 5. Webflow and Framer partner directories

If you build in one tool, its partner directory puts you in front of companies that already chose it. Webflow reworked its program in 2026, so check the current terms of both before you plan around them.

[Webflow's Partner Program](https://webflow.com/certified-partners/apply) starts with a Foundations tier that pays 10% commission on the Site plans and add-ons you bring in, for the first 12 months of each subscription, as of November 2026. The directory listing and client matching come with the Certified tier, which pays 20%. Getting there takes an active Freelancer or Agency Workspace plan, a passed pre-qualification assessment, and a portfolio review of three new client sites built in Webflow after you join Foundations.

[Framer's Experts program](https://www.framer.com/help/articles/how-to-become-a-framer-expert/) asks for a complete Community profile with at least two published Framer sites on custom domains. Experts appear in the official directory, receive client requests matched to their profile, and earn up to 50% commission when a client they hand a site to buys a plan.

The [guide to getting Webflow clients](/blog/how-to-get-webflow-clients) and the [guide to getting Framer clients](/blog/how-to-get-framer-clients) go deeper on each program. WordPress studios rely on other programs, from hosting partners to WooCommerce: see [how to get WordPress clients](/blog/how-to-get-wordpress-clients).

### 6. Clutch and review directories

Clutch lists agencies by service and location next to verified client reviews, and [a basic profile is free](https://help.clutch.co/knowledge/free-features-clutch-offers). You ask clients to review you, and Clutch verifies each review before it goes live.

The listing alone does little; the reviews do the work. Ask your three happiest past clients this month, and leave paid placement until the profile has reviews worth showing.

### 7. Marketplaces: Upwork, Contra and Fiverr

Marketplaces can land a new freelancer a first paid project within weeks, but they are the hardest place to charge well: buyers compare proposals side by side, with every price in plain view.

Know the costs before you start. [Upwork's freelancer service fee ranges from 0% to 15% per contract](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/211062538-Learn-about-the-Freelancer-Service-Fee), and you see the rate before you send a proposal. Contra and Fiverr price differently, so compare their terms before you commit time to either.

Use a marketplace to earn reviews and portfolio pieces, then let your other channels bring in the larger projects. Read the platform's terms before you work with a client you met there outside it.

## 8 to 11: slow channels that compound

### 8. Case studies and a niche

A case study turns one finished project into proof for the next one. Write it as the client's problem, what you changed, and what happened after launch, with a number if the client allows it. Three case studies from one industry persuade more than twenty unrelated screenshots, because a buyer from that industry sees their own problem in them.

That is the case for a niche. A studio known for law firm sites, or for Framer sites for seed-stage startups, hears about projects inside that world and can charge for what it knows. [The best niches for web design agencies](/blog/best-niches-for-web-design-agencies) compares the options and shows how to test one before you commit.

Blog posts and teardown videos belong here too. They are slow to start, but prospects look at your work before they reply, so they lift every other channel.

### 9. Events and local groups

For local studios, the owners who need sites are at the chamber of commerce mixer, not at design meetups. Referral groups such as a BNI chapter exist to pass business between members, and a web designer is a useful member to have. For B2B studios, go where your buyers go, such as marketing conferences and founder events, rather than design conferences full of competitors.

Set a target before you walk in: three real conversations, and a follow-up to each within two days.

### 10. Communities

Answering questions where your buyers ask them builds a name one thread at a time. The Webflow and Framer forums count, as do Slack groups for founders or marketers. On Reddit, design subreddits mostly put you in front of other designers. Buyers post in hiring subreddits such as r/forhire and ask for advice in subreddits about running a business.

Read each community's rules on self-promotion first. Then help in public, and let people find your studio from your profile.

### 11. Templates

A template is slow to pay off, but each one keeps working. Its buyer has already chosen your builder and your style, and some will want help adapting the site to their business. Put your studio's name, a contact link and a clear offer for customization in the template's documentation and preview pages.

## From first reply to signed project

A channel only gets you a conversation. What happens next decides whether it becomes a client: a short discovery call, a written scope, and a proposal with options within two days of the call. [How to sell web design services](/blog/how-to-sell-web-design-services) walks through each step, from qualifying questions to the deposit.

Two habits matter whatever the channel. Answer new inquiries the same day, since a buyer comparing three studios notices who answered first. And never send free designs to win a project: a small paid discovery phase earns the same goodwill.
