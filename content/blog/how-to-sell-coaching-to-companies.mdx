**TL;DR:** Companies don't buy coaching. They buy a fix for a named problem: a senior hire who has to land, managers promoted faster than they were trained, a gap in the succession plan. Sell to the person who owns that problem (the CEO at a small company, a People or L&D leader at a larger one), propose a paid pilot with three to five leaders and measures agreed before it starts, and write down what the sponsor will see and what stays between you and the coachee. The pilot's results sell the program.

Below is how to sell coaching to companies, step by step, from the first brief to the renewal. For the channels that bring you to these companies, see [how to get executive coaching clients](/blog/how-to-get-executive-coaching-clients).

## Who buys coaching inside a company

The buyer changes with company size, and so does what they ask you for. Titles vary, so read this as the usual pattern, not a rule.

| Company size | Who usually signs | What they are solving | What they ask for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 100 people | The founder-CEO | Their own growth, then their first executives | Chemistry, references, a short agreement |
| 100 to 500 | The CEO or the first People leader | New executives landing, new managers, keeping key people | A clear process, a price per leader, progress updates |
| 500 to 5,000 | A VP of People, head of L&D or talent lead | Leadership programs, onboarding, high-potential groups | A proposal, a pilot, credentials, reporting, vendor setup |
| Above 5,000 | L&D or talent, with procurement | Coaching across levels and regions | A formal bid, data and security terms, a pool of coaches |

At every size, one more person shapes the outcome: the coachee's manager. A manager who agrees with the goals and gives feedback at the midpoint makes the engagement work. A manager who was never asked can quietly sink it. [Our page for executive coaches](/for/executive-coaches) shows how to find both the founder-CEO and the People leader at companies that fit your practice.

## How to sell coaching to companies in seven steps

A corporate coaching deal moves through the same seven steps whatever the size. A small company can run them in a week; a large one can take a quarter.

1. **A named need.** Find the business problem behind the request. "Our new VP of Sales starts in March, and the last one lasted nine months" is a need. "We'd like to offer coaching" is not yet one.
2. **A sponsor with a budget.** Ask who owns the problem and whose budget pays. Coaching can come from an L&D budget, a department budget or the CEO's office, and each has a different approver.
3. **A shortlist and chemistry calls.** Expect the sponsor to present two or three coaches and let the coachee choose. Treat the chemistry call as the interview it is: ask about the goal, play back what you heard, and say how you would work.
4. **A proposal.** One or two pages, covered in the next section.
5. **Vendor setup.** Expect a supplier form, your [Form W-9](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-9) so the company can report what it pays you, a master agreement, and often a certificate of insurance and data-protection terms. Ask early how long accounts payable takes to add a new supplier, and when invoices get paid.
6. **The three-way agreement.** Before coaching starts, agree with the coachee and the sponsor on goals, roles and what gets shared (more on this below).
7. **Midpoint and end reviews.** A short three-way check at the midpoint and a closing review with the sponsor. The closing review is where the next cohort gets sold.

## What a corporate coaching proposal needs

A proposal for a corporate engagement has to let the sponsor defend the decision to their own boss. Keep it to one or two pages, and cover:

- **The problem in the sponsor's words**, and the business result they want.
- **Who is coached**, and each leader's focus, in as much detail as the sponsor can share.
- **The structure.** Number and length of sessions, the engagement's length, any stakeholder interviews or 360 at the start.
- **What the sponsor sees.** Attendance, the goals agreed in the three-way meeting, a midpoint and a closing summary that the coachee has read first.
- **What stays private.** The content of the sessions.
- **The measures** and when they are taken.
- **Price per leader for the whole engagement**, payment terms and a start date. Package pricing reads better to a sponsor than an hourly rate, because it answers "what will this cost per leader?" in one line.

## Corporate coaching contracts: the clauses that matter

Expect a larger company to send its own master agreement. Read it against the coaching relationship, which has three parties, not two.

- **Confidentiality.** The [ICF Code of Ethics](https://coachingfederation.org/credentialing/coaching-ethics/icf-code-of-ethics/), in force since April 1, 2025, asks coaches to agree with the client and the sponsor, before coaching begins, on roles, responsibilities, confidentiality and financial arrangements (standard 1.1), and to have a clear agreement on what information is exchanged and how (standard 2.2). Write that agreement into the statement of work so nobody improvises it later.
- **Legal limits on confidentiality.** Name the cases where you would have to disclose something, as standard 2.3 of the same code expects.
- **Termination.** Any party may end the relationship, subject to the agreement (standard 1.2). Set the notice period and what happens to unused sessions.
- **Cancellation.** A late-cancellation rule for sessions, written once and applied the same way to every leader.
- **Records and data.** How you store notes and assessments, and when you delete them.
- **Associates.** If other coaches deliver part of the work, a subcontract with confidentiality and non-solicit terms for each one.

If a large company pays well after the invoice date, ask for part of the fee at the start of each cohort.

## Run a pilot that can turn into a program

A pilot lets a sponsor test you without betting the whole budget. Design it to produce a decision, not a nice feeling.

1. **Three to five leaders** with a visible goal and a manager who supports the work.
2. **Three to four months**, long enough for behavior to change and for others to notice.
3. **Two or three measures**, agreed with the sponsor before the first session.
4. **A decision rule in writing**: "If the stakeholder ratings improve for most of the pilot group, we extend to the next cohort at the full price."
5. **A paid price.** A pilot discount is fine. A free pilot isn't: the sponsor has no stake in the result, and the coachees' calendars feel it first.

Pick the pilot group with the sponsor. Leaders who want the coaching make a fairer test than leaders who were sent.

## Measure coaching so the sponsor can defend the budget

The [Kirkpatrick Model](https://www.kirkpatrickpartners.com/the-kirkpatrick-model/) gives sponsors a language they already know. It has four levels, and Kirkpatrick Partners advise starting the design with the last one: the results the organization wants.

| Level | What it measures | In a coaching engagement |
|---|---|---|
| 4. Results | Whether the targeted organizational outcomes occur | One result the sponsor already tracks: retention of the coached leaders, a new executive's time to productivity, team engagement scores |
| 3. Behavior | Whether people apply the critical behaviors on the job | Manager and peer ratings of two or three agreed behaviors, at the start and the end |
| 2. Learning | Whether people gain the intended knowledge, skills and confidence | The coachee's own rating of progress on each goal |
| 1. Reaction | Whether the experience feels favorable and relevant | A two-question rating after a few sessions |

Keep it light. A three-question survey to four or five stakeholders at the start, midpoint and end gives the sponsor a before-and-after picture without a research project. If the sponsor asks for an ROI figure, build it from their own numbers, such as what it cost the last time a senior hire left in the first year.

## Reaching People leaders before the brief goes out

By the time a formal request for coaches goes out, the sponsor may already have names in mind. Meet sponsors when the problem first appears, at moments like these:

- **A wave of manager hires.** A company posting several manager roles at once will soon have leaders who have never managed before. [Searching companies by the roles they are hiring for](/plays/companies-hiring) shows the wave.
- **A senior hire.** A VP or C-level opening means a leader who will have to land quickly.
- **A fresh round.** [Recently funded startups](/plays/recently-funded-startups) have new targets and, soon, new executives to bring in.
- **A new People leader.** A new head of People or L&D has a first-year plan to write. Watch announcements and press releases by hand.
- **A People leader posting about the problem**, such as manager development or leadership pipelines.

If your offer includes workshops for groups of managers, [how to get corporate training clients](/blog/how-to-get-corporate-training-clients) covers that sale.
