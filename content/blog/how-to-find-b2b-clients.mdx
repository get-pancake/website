**TL;DR:** Work the methods in order of how much trust they start with. First, people who have already paid you or worked beside you. Second, partners who sell to the same buyers. Third, companies showing a public reason to buy now: a job post, a funding round, a post about the problem. Directories, referral groups, events and content pay off over months, and lists bought on fit alone come last.

That order is how to find B2B clients for most small firms selling to other businesses, from agencies and consultancies to IT providers and software startups. Below: three questions that can change the order for you, the ten methods in one table, what to do this week for each, how to reach the person who signs, and a client-finding guide for your industry.

---

## Three questions that set the order for your business

The ranking holds for most small B2B businesses, but three facts about yours can move a method up or down the list.

1. **Who signs the contract?** An owner who decides alone, at a 12-person freight broker, buys through trust and proximity: a referral, a local group, the accountant's recommendation. A team with a budget, at a 400-person software company, buys through evaluation: proof, comparisons, and a champion who argues for you inside.
2. **How big is one deal?** It sets how many hours you can spend winning it. A $3,000 project can't pay for three meetings and a custom proposal. A $60,000 retainer can.
3. **Do your buyers leave public traces?** Companies that hire, raise money and post about their work can be found at the moment they need you. A dental practice or a roofing contractor rarely does any of that, so for firms selling to them, visibility and referrals beat outreach.

## Where do the first B2B clients come from?

From people who have already seen your work (method 1), and from firms that meet your buyer before you do (method 2). Both start with trust you don't have to earn.

**1. Past clients and former colleagues.** A client who was happy two years ago may now have a new budget, a new project or a new employer. Former colleagues who moved to companies that fit already know how you work, even if they've never bought from you.

This week: list every client from the last three years, and every former colleague now at a company that fits. Write to ten of them with one question about what has changed since you last worked together. The [warm outreach templates](/blog/warm-outreach) include openers for both.

**2. Referral partners.** Pick firms that touch your buyer right before or right after you do. The accountant hears about a messy month-end close before a fractional CFO does. A branding studio's clients need a new website. A software vendor's customers need someone to set the product up. Offer each partner something they value: referrals back, a referral fee, or a fast answer whenever they send someone.

This week: name five firms whose clients look like yours, and ask each one for a 20-minute call about the kinds of clients you both serve.

## How do you get B2B clients with no network?

Replace the introduction with a reason. Write to companies that show a public sign of needing you now (method 3), and answer people who ask for help in public (method 4).

**3. Signal-based outreach.** A signal is a public event that suggests a company needs what you sell this quarter: a job post for the work you do or support, a funding round, a post about the problem, or comments under a competitor's posts. Each one gives your first message a reason that isn't about you. A bookkeeping firm might write: "Saw you're hiring your first controller. Is month-end close running late, or is an audit coming?"

The [list of B2B buying signals](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) shows where to see each one, [job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) maps hiring to purchases, and [outbound sales strategy](/blog/outbound-sales-strategy) covers the cadence and the numbers to track. This week: pick one signal that fits your offer, find 20 companies showing it, and write to the person who owns the problem at each.

**4. Public requests.** Some buyers announce that they're looking: a founder asking peers to recommend an IT provider for a 40-person office, a marketing lead asking who built a site they admire, a company publishing a request for proposals. Answer in the thread with something useful first, then follow up directly.

This week: search the communities and feeds your buyers read for "recommend" plus your service, and answer the requests posted in the last few days.

## Where do B2B buyers look for a provider?

In the partner lists of software they already use (method 5), on review sites and directories (method 6), and in rooms where business owners trade referrals (method 7).

**5. Platform partner programs.** If you build on HubSpot, Shopify, Microsoft, Webflow, Zapier or Snowflake, the platform's partner directory puts you in front of buyers who have already chosen the tool. Expect a bar to clear first, such as certifications, client projects or a paid tier. The guides for [marketing agencies](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-marketing-agency), [Shopify agencies](/blog/how-to-get-shopify-clients), [MSPs](/blog/how-to-get-msp-clients), [AI automation agencies](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-ai-automation-agency) and [data consultancies](/blog/how-to-get-data-consulting-clients) walk through those programs.

**6. Review sites and directories.** A buyer who shortlists on a directory reads your reviews before your website. [A basic Clutch profile is free](https://help.clutch.co/en/knowledge/free-features-clutch-offers), and Clutch verifies each client review before publishing it, as of November 2026. For local B2B services such as IT support, office cleaning or commercial insurance, the map results matter more: [a Google Business Profile costs nothing](https://business.google.com/us/business-profile/) and puts you on Google Search and Maps. This week: ask your three happiest clients for a review on the one directory your buyers use.

**7. Referral groups and associations.** [BNI](https://www.bni.com/about/) chapters meet weekly and seat one member per profession, so the IT provider in the room gets the room's IT referrals; BNI counts about 355,000 members in 11,800 chapters, as of November 2026. Chambers of commerce are looser: mixers and a member directory rather than a weekly meeting with one seat per trade. If your buyers belong to a trade association, ask whether it offers a supplier or associate membership.

## Do events, content and bought lists still work?

They do, but slowly or at low odds, which is why they rank last for a small team that needs clients this quarter.

**8. Events and trade shows.** Go where your buyers go, not where your competitors exhibit. Many shows publish their exhibitor list, which doubles as a list of companies in the industry. Write to ten of them before the show with a message that names it, and aim for three booked meetings rather than a stack of business cards.

**9. Content and search.** Writing about the problems your buyers research pays off over years, not weeks. Start with what sales calls have already taught you: the five questions every prospect asks, each answered in full on its own page, with a client story behind each answer.

**10. Bought lists and mass email.** A list of 5,000 contacts with the right job title says who could buy and nothing about when. Sent in bulk, the same message to all of them gets ignored and puts your sending domain at risk. Use fit data as a map instead: [lookalikes of your best client](/blog/how-to-find-similar-companies) and a [prospect list built from your filters](/blog/how-to-build-a-prospect-list) tell you where to look, and a signal tells you when to write.

## Reach the person who signs, not the company

A company never replies; a person does. Find the person who owns the problem, who feels it and answers, then the person who owns the budget, who signs. Write to the first one.

The titles shift with company size. At 20 people, the founder often owns both. At 200, a head of the function owns the problem and a VP or the CEO signs. At 2,000, a director owns it, with a procurement team behind the signature. To find names, read the company's team page, the "reports to" line in its job posts, its press releases, and the posts its people write. [How to find decision makers](/blog/how-to-find-decision-makers) goes step by step.

Then keep the first message small: one reason, one question, no attachment and no calendar link. A first message is a request for one reply, not a proposal.
