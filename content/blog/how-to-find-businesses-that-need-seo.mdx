**TL;DR:** A business needs SEO help when search starts costing it money and someone there is paid to fix it. Four kinds of public evidence show that: hiring (an SEO manager or content marketer job post), change (a migration, a redesign, a new market), pain (a marketer posting about lost traffic after a Google update) and money (a funding round, steady spending on search ads). The table below lists ten signals, where each one shows, and when to write.

This is the prospecting chapter of [our guide to getting SEO clients](/blog/how-to-get-seo-clients). It covers how to find businesses that need SEO, how to read each signal, and how to check one in five minutes before you write.

## Need and budget are different signals

Almost every website could use SEO work, which makes need a weak filter. You want a company where search is on someone's plan this quarter, with a budget and an owner.

Compare two sites. One has missing title tags and a blog that stopped publishing in 2024. The other belongs to a company with an SEO manager job post open and a platform move announced for spring. The first needs SEO. The second is buying it. Start from [buying signals](/blog/buying-signals-b2b), and use site flaws to qualify a lead, not to open the conversation.

Local businesses are the exception, with their own method below.

## Hiring signals: someone owns search and has a budget

A job post is the strongest public sign of an SEO budget. Read the whole post, not only the title.

- **SEO manager or SEO specialist.** Search is funded. Even after the hire, one person can't cover the migration, the technical backlog and link earning. Pitch around the hire.
- **Content marketer, head of growth or a first marketing hire.** A new owner reviews every channel in their first months. Write to the hiring manager while the post is open, or to the new hire once they've started.
- **Job posts that name Semrush, Ahrefs or Google Search Console.** The team already pays for search tools, so the talk is about specialist work, not about whether SEO matters. [Finding companies by the tools in their job posts](/plays/tech-stack) shows how that search works.

Inside the post, look for lines such as "own our SEO roadmap," "lead the site migration," "launch our German site" or "recover organic traffic." Each names the project, not only the role. [Companies hiring for a role](/plays/companies-hiring) shows the hiring signal as a search.

## Change signals: the site is about to move

A site move is when SEO turns from a nice-to-have into a risk. Google's guide to [site moves](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) tells owners to expect temporary ranking fluctuation during a move, and says a medium-sized site can take a few weeks or more before Google shows the new URLs instead of the old ones. Someone has to build the URL mapping and test the redirects. That someone can be you.

The signal comes in three versions:

- **A redesign or a new platform.** Marketing teams post about it, studios announce launches, and job posts mention "migrating to Webflow" or "replatforming to Shopify." Before launch, offer the redirect plan. After launch, offer the check.
- **A new domain or a rebrand.** Every old URL needs a permanent redirect. Load a few old addresses: if they return errors instead of landing on the matching new page, the move has already cost them.
- **A new country or language.** Localized pages need hreflang, local keyword research and a URL structure decision. Watch for country pages going up and job posts for a localization manager or a country lead.

## Pain signals: they can already see the drop

Google ships core updates several times a year and lists each one, with its start date and duration, on the [Search Status Dashboard](https://status.search.google.com/products/rGHU1u87FJnkP6W2GwMi/history). The May 2026 core update, for example, started on May 21 and took 11 days and 21 hours to roll out. In the weeks after each one, marketers post about their traffic in social posts and in forums such as r/SEO and Google's Search Central Help Community.

Two facts from Google's page on [core updates and your website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-updates) shape what you say. Core updates don't target specific sites, so a drop is not proof of a penalty. And after a site improves, it can take several months for results to show, sometimes not until the next core update. Don't promise a quick fix.

Google also tells site owners to wait a full week after an update finishes before analyzing their data. Write after that week, once they've seen their numbers.

A softer version of this signal: marketers commenting on posts by well-known SEO voices, or on a rival agency's recap of the update. Search is on their mind, even if nothing has broken yet.

## Money signals: growth is funded or paid for

**A funding round followed by marketing hires.** New money raises targets, and the board asks how pipeline will grow without paying for every click. The round alone says little. A round followed by a head of growth or content job post says the plan includes search. [Recently funded companies](/plays/recently-funded-startups) explains how to find them.

**Steady search ads.** A company buying ads on searches it could earn organically has a cost problem you can put a number on. Google's [Ads Transparency Center](https://adstransparency.google.com/) lets you search by advertiser name or website and see the active ads it runs through Google. Use what you find to open a conversation about acquisition cost, not as proof of how much they spend.

## Local businesses: walk the map

Local businesses rarely post about rankings or hire for SEO, so signal-watching finds few of them. Walk the map instead:

1. Search Google Maps for a category and a city, such as "family dentist Austin."
2. Skip the first few results and open the profiles further down. Note the ones with few reviews, no website link or old photos.
3. Look for unclaimed profiles. Google's help page on [claiming a Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/answer/2911778?hl=en) explains that owners take over an unverified profile through a "Claim this business" option in Maps. A listing that still shows it hasn't been verified by anyone.
4. Check the website for the basics: a page per service, the city in the title tags, a phone number that matches the profile.

Write to the owner with one finding, one question and the search you ran, so they can see it too.

## Check a signal in five minutes before you write

A signal tells you when to write. These five checks tell you whether:

1. **Who owns search?** Find the head of marketing, growth or content. Write to them, not to the recruiter.
2. **Is there an in-house team?** A company with five SEOs buys specialist work, if anything. A company with none buys a program.
3. **Does the timing match?** A traffic-drop post from the week of a core update means something different from one posted three months later.
4. **What does the site show?** Run a site: search, note when the blog last published, and load two old URLs if the domain changed.
5. **Is it a peer?** SEO consultants post about every update. Their posts look like signals and aren't.

Then write one short message about the signal. [SEO cold email templates](/blog/seo-cold-outreach) has an opener for most of the ten.
