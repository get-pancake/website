**TL;DR:** A working ICP fits on one screen: what kind of company it is, its size and location, the situation that makes it buy, who owns the problem and who signs, and the companies you turn away. The nine examples below show that format for B2B sellers, from a web design studio and an MSP to a developer tools startup and a fractional CFO. Copy the template after them, fill it from your own best customers, and keep buyer personas in a separate document.

These ideal customer profile examples are invented to show the format. Swap in your own numbers.

---

## What a useful ICP spells out

Most ICPs fail in two places: they never say what makes a company buy now, and they never say who to turn away. Each example below answers five questions: what the company is, its size and place, what is true when it buys, who owns the problem and who signs, and who you turn away. Yours should add a sixth: the three real customers it describes.

Write the industry as a code when a list tool or a government table asks for one. The Census Bureau's [NAICS search](https://www.census.gov/naics/) finds it: offices of dentists, for example, are 621210.

## Nine ideal customer profile examples for B2B

### 1. A web design studio that builds for software companies

- **Company:** US B2B software companies whose website is their main sales tool.
- **Size and place:** 20 to 200 staff, at Series A or B, with a marketing team of two to eight.
- **Buys when:** a new round, a rebrand, a new head of marketing, or a job post for a web designer.
- **Owner and signer:** the head of marketing owns the project; below about 100 staff, the CEO signs.
- **Turn away:** online stores, companies under 10 staff, and other agencies.

### 2. An MSP for dental groups

- **Company:** dental groups (NAICS 621210) with three to 15 offices. Practices that are HIPAA covered entities must keep administrative, physical and technical safeguards for electronic patient records ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/security/laws-regulations/index.html)).
- **Size and place:** 40 to 300 staff, every office within a 90-minute drive of your technicians.
- **Buys when:** a new office opens, the cyber insurance renewal asks questions nobody can answer, or the person who "does IT" leaves.
- **Owner and signer:** the practice administrator or COO owns it; the owner dentist or the group's CFO signs.
- **Turn away:** single-office practices, and groups with three or more in-house IT staff.

### 3. A recruiting agency for engineering hires

- **Company:** US software companies after a Series A, B or C.
- **Size and place:** 50 to 300 staff, five or more open engineering roles, one in-house recruiter or none.
- **Buys when:** roles stay open past 30 days, a round closes, or a new VP of engineering arrives.
- **Owner and signer:** the VP of engineering owns the hiring; the CFO approves the fee terms.
- **Turn away:** companies with a talent team of five or more, and staffing firms posting for their own clients.

### 4. Scheduling software for HVAC and plumbing contractors

- **Company:** plumbing, heating and air-conditioning contractors (NAICS 238220) that run service calls rather than new construction.
- **Size and place:** 15 to 150 technicians, in the US and Canada.
- **Buys when:** they hire a dispatcher, open a second branch, or outgrow the whiteboard.
- **Owner and signer:** the operations manager runs the evaluation; the owner signs.
- **Turn away:** shops under five technicians, and franchise locations whose software the franchisor picks.

### 5. A security firm selling SOC 2 readiness

- **Company:** B2B SaaS companies that sell to larger buyers and have no SOC 2 report yet.
- **Size and place:** 30 to 300 staff, in the US or the EU.
- **Buys when:** a large prospect sends a security questionnaire, the trust page says "SOC 2 in progress", or the first security engineer role goes up.
- **Owner and signer:** the CTO owns it; the CEO or CFO signs.
- **Turn away:** companies with a security team and a current Type II report.

### 6. A fractional CFO for venture-backed startups

- **Company:** venture-backed startups between seed and Series B.
- **Size and place:** $2 million to $15 million in annual revenue, no full-time finance leader, US time zones.
- **Buys when:** a round closes, the board asks for a real model, or the first controller job post goes up.
- **Owner and signer:** the CEO, who usually holds both roles.
- **Turn away:** pre-revenue companies, and companies that already employ a VP of finance.

### 7. A training company for manufacturers

- **Company:** manufacturers with two or more plants, such as machine shops (NAICS 332710) and other metal fabricators.
- **Size and place:** 250 to 2,000 employees in the Midwest and Southeast US.
- **Buys when:** a new plant opens, many supervisors are promoted or hired at once, or floor turnover climbs.
- **Owner and signer:** the plant HR lead owns it; the VP of operations signs.
- **Turn away:** single-site shops under 100 people, and companies with an in-house training team.

### 8. A developer tools startup for API testing

- **Company:** software companies that ship public or partner APIs.
- **Size and place:** 20 to 200 engineers, in any English-speaking market.
- **Buys when:** job posts name the API stack it supports, the team hires QA or platform engineers, or an incident report blames an API change.
- **Owner and signer:** engineering managers and staff engineers try it; the VP of engineering signs.
- **Turn away:** agencies building client projects, and teams that maintain their own testing framework.

### 9. A data consultancy for mid-market retailers

- **Company:** retailers and e-commerce brands that run a cloud data warehouse.
- **Size and place:** $20 million to $500 million in revenue, a data team of one to four, in North America.
- **Buys when:** they hire a first analytics engineer, move to a new warehouse, or a new CFO asks for reports the team can't build.
- **Owner and signer:** the head of data owns it; the CFO or COO signs.
- **Turn away:** companies with 10 or more data staff, and brands under $5 million in revenue.

## Ideal customer profile template

Answer each question from the customers you already have, not the market you hope for.

| Field | Question to answer |
|---|---|
| Company | What does the company do, and who does it sell to? |
| Industry code | Which NAICS code covers it, if a list tool will ask? |
| Size | Which headcount or revenue band buys, and below what size do you lose money? |
| Place | Where can you sell, deliver and support? |
| Buys when | What is true at the company when a deal starts? |
| Owner | Which role feels the problem every week? |
| Signer | Which role approves the money? |
| Signals you can see | Which public trace shows the moment: a job post, a round, a tool named in job posts, a post? |
| Turn away | Which companies look right but never close, or close and never stay? |
| Evidence | Which three current customers does this describe? |

The signals row ties the ICP to your outreach: [buying signals in B2B](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) shows where to see each kind.

## ICP vs buyer persona

An ICP picks the companies. A persona picks the person, and the words you use with them.

| | Ideal customer profile | Buyer persona |
|---|---|---|
| Describes | A kind of company | A person inside it |
| Built from | Firmographics, situation, disqualifiers | Role, goals, weekly problems, objections |
| Used to | Choose accounts and drop the rest | Choose who to write to and what to say |
| From example 2 | Dental groups with three to 15 offices | The practice administrator who handles IT between payroll and scheduling |

Most ICPs carry two or three personas: the owner, the signer and sometimes a daily user. [How to find decision makers in a company](/blog/how-to-find-decision-makers) maps those roles by purchase.

## Write yours from your best customers

Build it from evidence, in six steps:

1. List every customer from the last two years with what they paid, how long they took to close and whether they stayed.
2. Mark the best five by outcome: they closed without a fight, paid on time, stayed, used what they bought, or referred someone.
3. Write what those five share, field by field. A trait only one of them has is a nice-to-have, not a rule.
4. Mark the worst five. What they share becomes your turn-away line.
5. Count the companies that match before you build anything. [How to build a prospect list](/blog/how-to-build-a-prospect-list) shows how.
6. Reread the ICP every quarter, or after every ten new deals.

Three mistakes undo most ICPs. "SMBs in North America" fits more companies than you could ever contact. A wish list of famous logos describes who you want, not who buys. And an ICP without a turn-away line can't say no. Once yours holds, [lookalikes of your best customers](/blog/how-to-find-similar-companies) turn it into named accounts.
