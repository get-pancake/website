**TL;DR:** Branding projects go to the studio a company already knows when its name or identity stops fitting: after a funding round, a merger, a new product line or a new marketing leader. So the channels that win put you in the room before a brief exists: past clients and the investors around them, partner agencies that see the change coming, and outreach to companies showing a trigger. Portfolios, directories and awards help once you're on the shortlist.

Below is how to get branding clients through ten channels, ranked for studios and independent brand designers, with a table to compare them, the case against spec work, and how to turn one identity project into years of work. The triggers themselves are listed in [companies that need rebranding](/blog/how-to-find-companies-that-need-a-rebrand).

---

## Who hires a branding agency, and what sets it off

Three buyers commission branding work, and they buy in different ways.

**Startup founders.** At 10 to 100 people, the CEO usually owns the brand. They hire after a round, before a launch, or when the name stops fitting the product. They post about it, ask their investors for recommendations, and decide fast.

**Marketing leaders at growth companies.** At 100 to 1,000 people, a head of marketing or brand owns it. They hire after a merger, a new product line or a move upmarket, run a short pitch with a written brief, and want strategy as much as design.

**Local businesses.** A restaurant, a clinic or a builder wants a logo, signage and a website, often on a small budget. They buy through referrals and local designers, and leave little public trace.

In all three, a trigger decides when money moves. Any one company rebrands rarely, so the work is to meet each company at the moment it decides, then stay on as its brand keeps changing.

## Channels 1 to 3: be in the conversation before the brief

### 1. Past clients and the people around them

Every launched brand is a referral source, and not only through the client. Investors and board members sit on other companies that will raise and rebrand. The marketing lead who ran your project will move to another company one day and need a brand there too. Keep track of all three.

**This week:** list every brand you launched in the last three years. Next to each, note the investors named on the company's site and the person who ran the project. Write to the founder of the most recent one and ask which companies in their investors' portfolios are about to raise or launch.

### 2. Partner agencies that see the change coming

Four kinds of firms hear about a rebrand before you do:

- **Web design studios.** A new site often exposes a dated identity, and the studio needs a brand to build with. The [web design client playbook](/blog/how-to-get-web-design-clients) ranks partner agencies near the top for the same reason, so referrals can run both ways.
- **PR firms.** A merger, a rename or a big launch needs an announcement, and the PR firm learns the date early.
- **Naming consultants and trademark lawyers.** A new name needs an identity, and a forced rename needs it fast.
- **Marketing agencies.** They run the campaigns a tired brand drags down, and some don't do identity work. Their own playbook, [how to get clients for a marketing agency](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-marketing-agency), ranks partners second, so the door is open.

Offer each a clean handoff: you take the identity, they keep the site, the launch or the campaign, and nobody poaches the client. Venture firms' platform teams can play the same role for their portfolios. Ask the ones whose companies you've worked with whether they keep a list of design partners.

### 3. Outreach to companies showing a trigger

Outreach works for branding when it starts from a trigger you can see:

- a founder posting about a rebrand, a new name or a brand refresh;
- a startup announcing a funding round ([recently funded startups](/plays/recently-funded-startups));
- a job post for a brand designer, a brand manager or a creative director ([companies hiring for a role](/plays/companies-hiring));
- a founder commenting on a rival studio's case study;
- a merger, a new product line or a new head of marketing, which you'll mostly spot by hand.

[Companies that need rebranding](/blog/how-to-find-companies-that-need-a-rebrand) lists ten triggers with where to see each. Read the post before you write. "Excited to unveil our new brand" is a reveal, and the identity work is done. "Our name no longer fits what we do" is a plan.

The first message asks about the trigger and leaves your portfolio out: "Saw your post about the name not fitting anymore. Is it the product that outgrew it, or the market you're moving into?" This is the channel [Pancake for branding agencies](/for/branding-agencies) is built around.

## Channels 4 to 7: the proof that wins the shortlist

### 4. Case studies that tell the business story

A buyer who has shortlisted you reads your case studies to answer one question: will this studio understand our business? A grid of logos doesn't answer it. Write each case study as the business change that started the project, the strategy you proposed, the identity, and what happened after. A round closed with the new deck. A sales team stopped apologizing for the old name. A careers page finally matched the company.

Pick the three that match the clients you want next, and lead with those. A fintech founder looks for fintech.

### 5. Dribbble and Behance

Both are portfolio sites where buyers can hire you on the platform, and both take less on bigger projects. As of November 2026, [Dribbble's designer platform fee](https://help.dribbble.com/en/articles/11062173-dribbble-project-fees) is graduated: 10% on the first $500 of a project, 7% on the next $500 and 4% above $1,000, so a $2,500 project costs the designer $145. Annual Pro Standard and Pro Plus members pay no designer fee. [Behance's freelancer fee](https://help.behance.net/hc/en-us/articles/10770324288923-FAQ-What-are-the-fees) runs from 5% on the first $500 to 0% above $2,500, and Behance Pro waives it.

Treat both as proof and as a source of smaller identity projects, not as your main pipeline. Post full case studies rather than single logos, with your studio name and a contact line on each.

### 6. Directories and partner listings

Review directories such as Clutch help once you have client reviews to show, because buyers use them to build a shortlist. If you work alongside a website platform, check its partner directory too: [Webflow's Certified Partner directory](https://webflow.com/certified-partners/browse) lets buyers filter for "Branding & Strategy." Collect reviews from your last three launches before you pay for any placement.

### 7. Awards and design press

Awards and design press rarely bring a client directly, but they change who calls. A rebrand featured in a design publication or shortlisted for an award reassures the larger buyer who has to defend the choice inside the company. Enter selectively: entry fees add up, and one win in your target industry beats five in categories your buyers never read.

## Channels 8 to 10: slower routes, and one to avoid

### 8. Teardowns and guides

Founders read about other companies' rebrands. A short teardown of a public rebrand in your niche, covering what changed and why it worked or didn't, shows how you think without a pitch. So does a guide to renaming a company or a checklist for briefing a branding agency. Publish them where founders read, not only on design sites.

### 9. Workshops for accelerators and founder groups

Accelerators and founder communities run sessions for their cohorts and need speakers. A one-hour positioning workshop puts you in front of a dozen founders at once, at the stage when many are about to raise and outgrow a placeholder brand. Ask the organizer to introduce you to any founder who wants to keep talking, and follow up within two days.

### 10. Marketplaces and design contests

Upwork and Contra can land a first paid logo or identity job. Design contest sites ask you to compete with unpaid entries, which is spec work under another name. Use marketplaces to earn your first reviews, and skip the contests as you grow.

## Pitch without spec work

Some buyers ask for free concepts before they choose a studio. [AIGA's position on spec work](https://www.aiga.org/resources/aiga-position-on-spec-work) sets out the risks: clients get compromised quality, because spec work skips the research and testing good design needs; designers risk being taken advantage of; and both sides carry legal risk over intellectual property and trademarks. AIGA also publishes a sample letter you can adapt to decline.

Offer a paid first step instead: a half-day positioning workshop, a brand audit or a naming sprint, priced on its own and credited to the full project if they go ahead. The buyer sees how you work without you giving the work away.

## Turn one identity project into years of work

A rebrand ends, and the next one at that company may be years away. Plan the follow-on before launch:

1. **Sell the rollout.** Website, sales deck, packaging, event booths, social templates. List them in the proposal as phase two.
2. **Offer brand care.** A small monthly retainer covers new templates, reviews of other agencies' work, and updates to the guidelines.
3. **Ask for referrals at launch.** The week the new brand goes live is when the founder is proudest of it. Ask which founders they know who are raising or launching next.
4. **Come back at the next trigger.** A new round, a new product line or a new head of marketing at an old client is a reason to call.
