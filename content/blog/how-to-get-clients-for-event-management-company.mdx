**TL;DR:** Corporate event clients go with the agency the event owner already trusts, so the job is to get there before the venue search starts. The owner is rarely titled "event manager": it is a head of People planning an offsite, a sales leader planning a kickoff, a field marketer planning a roadshow. Watch for the traces that come first, such as an event manager job post, a post about next year's offsite or a fresh funding round. Ask the owner one question about the event, then turn every event you run into the next booking.

Everything below is about how to get clients for an event management company that sells to businesses: offsites, conferences, sales kickoffs and team building. Weddings and private parties run on different channels.

## Who owns each corporate event

Find the owner before you pitch. At companies without an events team, an event belongs to whoever feels the problem it solves.

| Event | Usual owner | Also involved | Signal to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company offsite or retreat | Head of People, chief of staff | The CEO, the workplace or office manager | Posts about an offsite or retreat; a remote-first careers page |
| Leadership retreat | Chief of staff, the CEO's executive assistant | The CEO | Posts by chiefs of staff; a fresh funding round |
| Sales kickoff | VP of Sales, sales enablement, sales operations | Marketing, for content and staging | A sales hiring push; an enablement job post |
| User conference or customer summit | Head of marketing, the events team | Product, customer success | Event manager job posts; Cvent or Bizzabo in job posts |
| Field events, roadshows, dinners | Field marketing manager | Regional sales leaders | Field marketing job posts |
| Trade show presence and hospitality | Events or marketing lead | Sales | The show's exhibitor list |
| Holiday party, all-hands, team building | People operations, workplace experience | Finance, for the budget | Headcount growth; recaps of last year's event |

When a company has its own events team, that team is your buyer. You sell what it can't staff: production, logistics in a city it doesn't know, or a format it hasn't run before.

## What planners expect for 2027

Planners are heading into a busy year. In [Amex GBT's 2027 Global Meetings and Events Forecast](https://www.amexglobalbusinesstravel.com/press-releases/2027-global-meetings-events-forecast/), 91 percent of the 500 meetings professionals surveyed in August 2026 expect their meetings and events spend to rise in 2027. More of them expect to hold more meetings than fewer, in every meeting type.

The same survey says what attendees ask for: more personalized experiences (35 percent), more social activities and networking (31 percent), and more hands-on sessions and workshops (31 percent). Geopolitical instability is reshaping plans as well: 39 percent of respondents report a change of destination or city, 37 percent are holding more virtual meetings, and 30 percent report smaller meetings or a change of venue. An agency that can produce a hybrid session or move a program to a closer city has something to say about that.

For an agency, that points to a pitch built on what attendees will feel and do, with the cost trade-offs shown plainly, since planners also name rising costs as a pressure. A list of the logistics you handle won't stand out.

## Signals that show up before the venue search

Many events leave public traces before anyone writes an RFP. These six come first, and they are the signals [Pancake for event agencies](/for/event-agencies) is built around.

1. **Event roles in job posts.** An event manager, events coordinator, field marketing manager or workplace experience manager post means a company is putting money into events. These roles open often: the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/business-and-financial/meeting-convention-and-event-planners.htm) counts 172,100 meeting, convention and event planner jobs in 2025 and projects about 16,800 openings a year through 2035. A new planner arrives with a calendar to fill and no vendor list yet. [Companies hiring for a role](/plays/companies-hiring) shows that search, and [job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) maps other roles to what they buy.
2. **Event platforms in job posts.** Cvent, Bizzabo or Splash in a job post tells you events are part of the job, and roughly at what scale. [Searching by the tools named in job posts](/plays/tech-stack) turns those companies into a list.
3. **Posts about an offsite, a kickoff or a summit.** Planning posts ("looking for ideas for our team offsite") and recaps both count. A team that just ran one has a reason to plan the next, and a list of what it would change.
4. **A funding round.** A round brings a hiring plan and, often, a reason to put the whole team in one room.
5. **Remote-first and fast-growing teams.** A remote company has to plan every in-person moment on purpose, and some say so on their careers page: "remote-first, with two team gatherings a year." Headcount growth means the last venue no longer fits.
6. **By hand: exhibitor lists and company news.** Trade shows publish exhibitor directories well ahead of the show, and [the CES 2027 directory](https://exhibitors.ces.tech/) is already searchable by category, state and country. Each exhibitor may need booth staff, a client dinner or a hospitality suite. Office openings, acquisitions with two teams to merge, and company anniversaries are worth a news alert.

[The full list of B2B buying signals](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) covers where to see each of these, and the ones you can only watch by hand.

## Three openers for three signals

A first message to an event owner asks about their event, not about your agency. Three made-up cases for a small agency that plans offsites and kickoffs:

- **A workplace experience manager role at a 240-person software company.** "Saw you're hiring a workplace experience manager. Will the new hire own the offsites too, or only the office?"
- **A head of People writing that the team hasn't met in person since spring.** "You wrote that the team hasn't been together since spring. Is a full-company gathering on the table for the new year, or smaller team trips?"
- **A fintech listed in the CES 2027 exhibitor directory.** "Saw you're exhibiting at CES. Are you hosting anything for clients during the show, or keeping it to the booth?"

None of them pitches. Each is easy to answer, and the answer tells you whether there is a project and who owns it.

## How to get clients for your event management company: eight channels, ranked

This order is a judgment call from how each channel works for a small agency, not a survey result. The first two cost almost nothing; the last two take the longest.

1. **Repeat clients and the annual calendar.** Many corporate events recur: the kickoff, the offsite, the holiday party, the summit. Book next year's date in the debrief, while the feedback is fresh, and send the photo recap with the proposal.
2. **People who change companies.** Every attendee, sponsor and executive assistant who loved your event may run one at their next company. Keep a list and check it monthly for moves.
3. **Outreach to the owner on a signal.** Write to the person who owns the event, not to a shared events inbox, with one question about it. The signals above tell you who and when.
4. **Venue and hotel sales teams.** Venues hear about many events first, and some of the companies asking need more help than a venue provides. Give the venue's sales managers a reason to pass you those inquiries, and book their space when it fits your clients.
5. **Vendors next to you.** AV and production companies, photographers and video crews, caterers, swag suppliers and the facilitators who run sessions at offsites all see events early. Corporate trainers count event agencies among their partners for the same reason, as [how corporate training firms find clients](/blog/how-to-get-corporate-training-clients) describes.
6. **Associations and their chapters.** MPI, PCMA and ILEA chapters put you in rooms with in-house planners and suppliers. Join a committee rather than only attending: the people who run a chapter meet everyone.
7. **Proof that travels.** A two-minute recap video, a one-page case with the goal, headcount, format and outcome, and a template buyers can reuse, such as an offsite agenda or a kickoff run-of-show. These get forwarded to the person who signs.
8. **RFPs and preferred-vendor lists.** Large companies source big events formally. Answer the RFPs that fit, and ask your current clients' procurement teams how to get on the approved list.

## Winning the first event with a new client

A first event is a trial for both sides. Five habits turn it into the first of several:

1. **Discovery that goes past logistics.** Ask what the event is for (alignment, celebration, training, pipeline), how many people will come, which dates can move, the budget range, who signs, and what went wrong last time.
2. **Two or three concepts, priced.** Show what each option trades away, so the buyer chooses between them instead of negotiating one down.
3. **No free venue research.** A full sourcing shortlist is the work itself. Share one sample itinerary to show your thinking, and start sourcing once a planning fee or deposit is paid.
4. **Fees a buyer can compare.** Say plainly whether you charge a flat planning fee, a percentage of the total budget or a day rate, and what each includes. Unclear fees make a new agency look riskier than the one the buyer already knows.
5. **A debrief that books the next one.** Within a week of the event, send attendance, a short survey result, photos and a proposed date for next year.
