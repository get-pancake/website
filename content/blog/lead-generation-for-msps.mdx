**TL;DR:** Lead generation for MSPs that lasts runs on two feeds you can repeat: partners who hear about IT trouble before you do, and public signals from companies big enough to leave them. The partners: accountants, property managers, brokers and the vendors of the software your clients run. The signals are IT job posts, Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace named in job ads, and posts about an outage or a move. Skip bought lists: nobody on them asked to hear from you, and Gmail's guidelines tell senders not to buy addresses.

This page is the system behind the leads. Every source ranked by speed and cost, including Microsoft's partner directory and cold calling, is in [how to get MSP clients](/blog/how-to-get-msp-clients).

## What makes an MSP lead worth a call

An MSP lead is a company in your size band and service area with a dated reason to change how it handles IT, plus a person who can sign. Without the reason, it's a name on a list.

The reasons that move offices, from most to least urgent:

- **The IT person is leaving.** A resignation, a retirement, or the office manager who "does the computers" handing them back.
- **Something broke.** An outage, a phishing incident, a backup that wouldn't restore.
- **Something is changing.** A move, a second location, an acquisition, a hiring push.
- **Someone outside is asking.** A cyber insurance application, a client's security review, an auditor's letter.
- **A contract is ending.** The current MSP agreement is up for renewal, usually with a notice period.

Ask two questions before you spend an hour on a lead. Does it fit the seat count you serve well? Which of these reasons applies this quarter? If the second answer is "none," file the company and wait for a signal.

## The signals behind lead generation for MSPs

Most of those reasons stay private. A few leave public traces, and those are the signals to check every week. The table lists each one, where it shows up and who to write to.

| Signal | What it suggests | Where to see it | Who to contact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Help desk or IT support job post | The current setup can't keep up | Job boards, the company's careers page | COO or office manager |
| First IT manager or sysadmin opening | IT is becoming a function | Job boards | The hiring executive, often the COO or CFO |
| Microsoft 365, Intune or Google Workspace in a job ad | The stack you would manage | The post's requirements list | Whoever owns IT |
| A post about an outage, phishing or a cyber insurance renewal | A problem or a deadline right now | Public posts in the feed | The person who posted |
| A new office or a second location | Circuits, cabling, Wi-Fi, phones | Posts, local business journals, property managers | Owner or operations lead |
| A funding round or an acquisition | Hires, devices, systems to merge | Press releases, funding databases | COO or CFO |
| Comments on a rival MSP's posts | Shopping around, or unhappy | That MSP's public posts | The commenter |
| A compliance letter or questionnaire | An outside party wants proof | Rarely public: partners hear first | Practice administrator or managing partner |

Read job posts in full. A help desk post at a 40-person company often lists the exact tools the technician will support, and the "nice to have" line shows what the team lacks. A post still open after six weeks says the hire is hard, which makes a co-managed bridge easy to offer. The [guide to job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) covers which roles point to which purchase.

Most rows can run as searches: [companies hiring for a role](/plays/companies-hiring), [companies by the tools in their job posts](/plays/tech-stack), posts by topic, funding rounds, comments on a rival's posts. That is the part [Pancake automates for MSPs](/for/msps). Compliance letters and many office moves reach you only through people, which is why partners matter.

## Partners who send MSP leads

Partners turn private triggers into introductions. Each one below meets your buyer for another reason, and each needs a reason to send that buyer to you.

1. **Accountants and bookkeepers.** They hear "our server crashed during month-end close" before anyone else. Tax preparers carry [federal data-security duties](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-security-summit-remind-tax-pros-they-need-a-written-information-security-plan-to-protect-client-data) of their own, so many become a client first and a referrer second.
2. **Property managers and commercial brokers.** They know who is moving, expanding or leaving, often months ahead. Offer a move checklist and a quick site survey, and ask to join the vendor list they give new tenants.
3. **Software vendors and their implementers.** Sellers of dental, legal and construction software need a local IT partner for installs and for network problems blamed on their product.
4. **Cyber insurance brokers.** They see the application questions an owner can't answer, such as whether MFA covers every account or when backups were last tested. A broker who can name an IT firm to close those gaps keeps the renewal moving.
5. **Copier, phone and camera dealers.** They work inside the same offices, on the same network, and take the blame when it fails.
6. **Referral groups.** [BNI says each chapter allows one professional per specialty](https://www.bni.com/the-latest/blog-news/why-bni-allows-only-one-profession-per-chapter/), so once you hold the IT seat, no other IT provider sits in that room.

Put each partnership on one page: who you fit (seat count, industries, area), what happens after an introduction (a call within a day, a short update to the partner) and how you say thanks. Then send each partner something useful once a quarter, such as a note on a scam hitting their clients' inboxes.

## MSP marketing ideas that start conversations

Pick ideas that put you in a room or an inbox with a buyer. Six worth testing:

1. **A lunch-and-learn for an accounting firm's clients.** Twenty minutes on what insurers and auditors ask about MFA and backups, then questions.
2. **A move-in note through a property manager.** One page for new tenants: who to call about internet, Wi-Fi and phones during move-in week.
3. **A phishing drill at a chamber of commerce.** Show a convincing fake email, ask the room to spot five tells, hand out the list.
4. **A quarterly letter to your own clients.** One fix per letter, written for the office manager, with a line asking who else should receive it.
5. **A cyber insurance application review.** Go through the form with an owner before renewal and mark the answers that need work.
6. **A review request tied to a good ticket.** When a client thanks you for a fast fix, reply with your Google review link while the thanks is fresh.

If you sell security services too, the [playbook for getting cyber security clients](/blog/how-to-get-cybersecurity-clients) covers selling them without fear tactics.

## The trouble with buying MSP lead lists

Bought MSP leads disappoint for one reason: the people on the list showed no sign of wanting a new IT provider, and none of them asked to hear from you. You pay for names, not timing.

Mailing a list also risks your domain. [Gmail's email sender guidelines](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/81126) tell senders not to purchase email addresses from other companies or message people who didn't sign up, and warn that those recipients may mark the messages as spam, so later messages to them land in spam too. For a firm that clients judge on email security, a flagged domain is a poor advertisement.

The law applies as well. The [FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) says the act makes no exception for business-to-business email. Each commercial message needs your valid physical postal address and a working opt-out, and you must honor opt-outs within 10 business days.

If you still want to test a vendor, run it as an experiment: one month, one area, a budget you can lose, and one number to judge it by, the cost of a signed agreement. Then compare that number with the cost of lunch with an accountant who already sends you work.

## From MSP lead to signed agreement

MSP lead generation pays off only at the signature, through a meeting and an assessment, so make both easy to accept.

- **Answer the same day.** An office with a dead server keeps calling other MSPs while it waits for you.
- **Offer a defined assessment.** One visit, a written list of the five things you would fix first, and an onboarding price. Some MSPs charge for it and credit the fee against the first month, which filters out offices collecting free advice.
- **Quote per user, on one page.** Owners compare your number with their current bill, so show what's included next to it.
- **Log the source.** Note where each lead came from and why each lost deal was lost. After a quarter, you will know which partner deserves another lunch.
