**TL;DR:** Start with the 50 people who already know your work, not with strangers: they reply soonest and need the least convincing. That's how to get web design clients fast. Week 1 goes to past clients and contacts with one fixed-price offer, week 2 to 50 companies showing a need right now, week 3 to partners and follow-ups, and week 4 to proposals sent within 24 hours of each call. No discounts, no free mockups.

No plan can promise a signature by day 30. This one is built so that by week 4 you have calls to turn into proposals, and a pipeline left for month two. If you need web design clients this month, follow the weeks in order. For the slower channels worth starting alongside, see the ranked list in [how to get web design clients](/blog/how-to-get-web-design-clients).

## Before day 1: pick one offer you can sell this month

A fast sale needs a small, fixed offer: one outcome, one price, one start date, delivered in two to four weeks. Custom scoping takes rounds of email. A fixed offer can be accepted in one reply.

Three examples, with placeholder prices (use your own rates):

- "A five-page Webflow site for a consultancy, live in three weeks, $6,000."
- "A landing page for your product launch, live in ten days, $2,500."
- "A one-week speed and conversion sprint on your current WordPress site, $1,800."

Set two more numbers before you send anything: your floor price and your deposit. Deciding them now keeps you from discounting in week 4, when a buyer hesitates. Then check your own site. It needs two or three projects close to the offer, each with one line on what changed for the client.

## Week 1: past clients and your network

Week 1 goes to people who already trust you, because they reply fastest. The target is 10 personal messages per weekday, 50 by Friday.

Work down this list, in order:

1. Past clients from the last three years.
2. Former employers and colleagues.
3. Freelancers and agencies you've worked with.
4. Friends and family who run businesses.
5. Anyone who once praised your work or asked what you charge.

Write each message by hand. Name the offer, give the start date, and ask for a name:

> "Hi [name], I'm opening two slots this month for [offer in one line], starting [date]. Do you know anyone who needs this? If it's you, reply and I'll send the details."

Add one public post on the platforms your buyers use, with the offer, the start date and how to reach you. Answer every reply the same day, and book a call with anyone who says "maybe."

## Week 2: build an outreach list from signals

Week 2 builds a list of 50 companies that show a need right now, and sends each one a first message tied to that reason. Where you look depends on who you sell to.

**Local businesses.** Search Google Maps for one trade in one town. Open each listing and note the businesses with no website, a broken one, or a booking form that fails on a phone. Write the specific problem next to each name; it becomes your first line.

**B2B companies.** Look for job posts for a web designer, a website manager or a Webflow developer. Look for marketing heads posting about a redesign, companies announcing a funding round, and new heads of marketing in their first months. [How to find businesses that need a website](/blog/how-to-find-businesses-that-need-a-website) lists the signals and where each one shows up.

The daily target is 10 companies researched and 10 first messages sent. Each message asks one question about the signal. No pitch, no mockup, no audit attached:

> "Hi [name], I tried to book from your site on my phone and the form wouldn't send. Did you know about that?"

> "Hi [name], saw the post about your new role. The first months usually come with a list. Is the website on yours?"

Only describe a problem you checked yourself. [Web design cold outreach templates](/blog/web-design-cold-outreach) has more first messages, one per signal, and the follow-ups to go with them.

## Week 3: partners and follow-ups

Week 3 adds people who can send you work, and follows up on every open conversation from weeks 1 and 2.

Message 10 potential partners: SEO, branding and PPC agencies, copywriters, and for local work, accountants and IT providers. Ask for a 20-minute call to compare notes on the clients you each serve. Partners rarely send work in their first week, but the ones you meet now can feed months two and three.

Follow up on everything still open. Send at most two follow-ups per conversation, three or four days apart, and make each one add something: a quick observation about their site, a relevant example, an answer to a question they asked. Then stop. The daily target is every follow-up that's due, plus two partner messages.

## Week 4: proposals and closing

Week 4 turns calls into signed work. Send each proposal within 24 hours of the call, with options, a start date and a deposit.

- **Book fast.** Offer a call slot within 48 hours of any "yes" or "maybe."
- **Keep the call to 30 minutes.** Ask about the goal, the deadline, the budget range and who decides.
- **Use three options.** Your fixed offer goes in the middle, with a smaller and a larger version around it.
- **Let the calendar do the closing.** "The start date holds until Friday" is honest pressure when your schedule is real.
- **Follow up on day 2 and day 5** after the proposal, each time with something useful.
- **Start only on a paid deposit.** No deposit, no kickoff.

[How to sell web design services](/blog/how-to-sell-web-design-services) has the discovery questions and the proposal structure in detail.

## How to get web design clients fast: the 30-day scorecard

Track the numbers below on one sheet. If weeks 1 and 2 bring no replies at all, the offer is the problem, not the volume: rewrite it around one outcome and send it again.

| Week | Focus | Daily target (weekdays) | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before day 1 | One offer, floor price, deposit | None | The offer fits in one sentence |
| 1 | Past clients and network | 10 personal messages | 50 sent, every reply answered the same day |
| 2 | Signal-based list | 10 companies researched, 10 first messages | 50 companies listed, 50 messages out |
| 3 | Partners and follow-ups | Follow-ups due, plus 2 partner messages | 10 partner conversations started |
| 4 | Proposals and close | A proposal within 24 hours of each call | Every proposal out, with a deposit request |

## What not to do when you're in a hurry

Speed tempts you into shortcuts that cost more later.

- **Discounting.** A lower price becomes the going rate for everyone that client refers. Cut scope or split the payments instead.
- **Free spec work.** [AIGA's position on spec work](https://www.aiga.org/resources/aiga-position-on-spec-work) lays out the risks: clients get designs made without research, designers get taken advantage of, and both sides face intellectual property problems. AIGA also offers a sample letter for explaining this to a client. Offer a short paid discovery instead.
- **Taking a bad-fit client.** No budget range, nobody who can decide on the call, a deadline with no reason behind it. A bad project in month one blocks the good one in month two.
- **Mass messages.** The same template sent to 500 strangers gets ignored, and some recipients will flag it as spam.
- **Leaning on a marketplace alone.** Upwork can bring a first project quickly, but [each proposal costs Connects, at $0.15 each](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/211062898-Understanding-and-using-Connects), the [freelancer service fee runs from 0% to 15% per contract](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/211062538-Learn-about-the-Freelancer-Service-Fee) as of November 2026, and you compete on price in plain view.
- **Waiting on slow channels.** Directories, content and local search take months. Start them now, but don't count on them for this month's revenue.
