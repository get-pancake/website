**TL;DR:** Founder-led sales means the founder runs every deal until the pattern repeats: finding the people, the first conversation, discovery, the proposal, the close. Block five to ten hours a week, keep a five-stage pipeline in a sheet or a free CRM, ask about past behavior on discovery calls, and end every call with a dated next step. Hire your first rep once you can write down how your last five deals closed and the steps match.

This founder-led sales playbook is for B2B founders selling without a sales team, solo or with a cofounder, from the first deal to the first sales hire. It assumes you already have people to talk to. If you don't, the [first-customers playbook for B2B SaaS](/blog/how-to-get-first-customers-b2b-saas) covers where they come from.

## What founder-led sales is, and what it isn't

Founder-led sales is a learning loop with revenue attached. The founder owns the pipeline, runs the calls, writes the proposals and signs the deals, and every deal changes something: the pitch, the price, the product.

Three reasons the founder has to do it at first:

- **You can change the offer on the call.** A rep has to ask permission to adjust scope or price. You don't.
- **Early buyers buy the team.** A company that signs with a ten-person startup is betting on the people building it, and wants to hear from one of them.
- **Objections are product feedback.** "We'd need it to work with our ERP" is a roadmap item. Heard secondhand from a rep, it arrives with less detail.

What it isn't: an excuse to skip process. Founders who sell without a pipeline, written next steps and notes end up with warm conversations everywhere and no idea which one is about to close.

## The founder-led sales process, week by week

The rhythm matters more than the hours. Block the same slots each week and hold them like a customer meeting. A starting point for a founder who also builds:

| Block | When | Length | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pipeline review | Monday, first thing | 30 minutes | Every open deal has a next step and a date |
| Finding and first messages | Monday | 90 minutes | 25 to 30 people with a reason to talk have a first message |
| Replies | Three short slots across the week | 20 minutes each | No reply waits more than a working day |
| Calls | Tuesday and Wednesday afternoons | 2 to 3 hours in all | Discovery and demos held, notes written the same hour |
| Proposals and follow-ups | Thursday | 60 minutes | Every call from the week has its recap or proposal sent |
| Loss review | Friday | 30 minutes | Each lost deal has a reason in the buyer's words |

That comes to about seven hours. With a cofounder, give one person the pipeline and let the other join calls where their knowledge closes the deal. A shared pipeline with no owner leaks follow-ups.

## A five-stage pipeline you can run in a sheet

Five stages are enough before a sales team exists. Each one has a rule for leaving it, so a deal can't sit in "interested" for two months.

1. **Signal.** You have a reason to write: a referral, a post about the problem, a job opening, a funding round. Leaves when the first message goes out.
2. **Conversation.** They replied. Leaves when a call is booked.
3. **Discovery.** The call happened, and you confirmed the problem, the owner and the timing. Leaves when they agree to see a proposal or start a pilot.
4. **Proposal or pilot.** Written terms are out. Leaves when they sign or say no.
5. **Closed.** Won, or lost with a reason.

A spreadsheet works with ten columns: company, contact, signal, stage, next step, next-step date, deal value, last contact, owner and loss reason. When the sheet gets crowded, a free CRM holds the same fields. [HubSpot's free CRM](https://www.hubspot.com/products/crm) covers up to two users and 1,000 contacts with no expiration date, as of November 2026, which fits two cofounders.

One rule beats any tool: no open deal without a dated next step. Review the numbers in the Monday slot. Count new conversations, calls held, proposals out and days spent in each stage, and split win rates by the signal that started each deal. The split tells you where to look for the next ten.

## Discovery calls: ask about the last time it happened

Discovery decides the deal, and it's the call where talking too much costs the most. Rob Fitzpatrick's short book [The Mom Test](https://www.momtestbook.com/) is built on one idea: people tell you polite lies about the future, so ask about their past. A 30-minute call can run like this: two minutes on the agenda, fifteen on their situation, eight showing the one workflow that matches it, five on the next step.

Questions that get facts instead of compliments:

1. "Walk me through the last time this happened."
2. "What did it cost you: hours, money, a customer?"
3. "What have you tried already, and why did you stop?"
4. "Who else would have to agree before you change how this works?"
5. "What happens if it stays as it is for another quarter?"

Write down their words, not your summary. "We lose a day every month-end" belongs in the proposal and on your website. Leave the call with a date on the calendar, not a promise to circle back.

Watch for three warning signs: compliments with no detail ("this is so cool"), no named owner for the problem, and no event that makes it urgent. Any one of them means you're still in discovery, whatever the buyer says.

## Demos and proposals that close

Show only the workflow that came up in discovery, and use their data or a close copy of it. A full product tour tends to end with "send me something" as the next step.

The proposal fits on one page:

- the problem, in the buyer's own words from discovery;
- the scope, including what you won't do;
- the price, with at most two options;
- the start date and the measure you'll both check at 60 days;
- what you need from them: data, an admin, a kickoff slot.

For deals with more than one approver, add a short mutual plan: the dates for the security questionnaire, the contract review, the signature and the kickoff, each with a name next to it. A buyer who won't put dates on that plan is telling you the deal isn't real yet.

Decide your price floor before the call. On the call you can trade, but only for something you need, such as annual terms, a reference or a case study.

## Closing, and losing well

Ask for the decision in plain words: "Is there anything that would stop you from signing by the 15th?" The answer is either a date or the real objection, and both move the deal.

After two missed dates, ask whether to close the file. It's a fair question, and it gives the buyer an easy way to tell you the truth. When a deal is lost, ask why in one short message, log the reason in the loss column and read the column on Friday. Three losses with the same reason are a product decision.

New conversations keep the pipeline alive while you close. The best openings come from a reason to write now: [job posts that signal a purchase](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals), a recent round from the [list of public funding sources](/blog/how-to-find-recently-funded-startups), or a post about the problem. [Pancake for SaaS startups](/for/saas-startups) shows those searches as example Plays. The first message asks one question tied to that reason and pitches nothing.

## When to hire your first sales rep

Hire when three things are true at once:

1. **The process repeats.** Your last five deals followed the same steps, and you could write them on two pages.
2. **Your calendar is the bottleneck.** Qualified buyers wait more than a week for a call, or you turn conversations away.
3. **The deal size can pay a salary.** Our [comparison of Pancake and a first BDR](/blog/pancake-vs-hiring-a-bdr) puts a fully loaded first BDR at about $108,600 in year one. Check that your average contract can carry a number like that.

Then pick the role for the gap. If you can't close all the meetings you get, hire an account executive to run the playbook. If you close well but run short of conversations, that's a prospecting gap: compare a first SDR with software before you add a salary.

Jason Lemkin's advice at SaaStr is to [hire two sales reps instead of one](https://www.saastr.com/when-you-hire-your-first-sales-rep-just-make-sure-you-hire-two/), so you can tell a weak rep from a weak process by comparing them. It costs more upfront, and a single hire gives you nothing to compare against. Whichever you choose, stay on the calls for the first months: the new rep shadows you, then you shadow them, and the largest deals stay yours until the playbook holds.

Founders who'd rather delay the first hire can push the founder-led stage further. [Building a GTM motion without a sales team](/blog/autonomous-gtm-without-sales-team) lays out that route.
