**TL;DR:** Pick three to five customers you would gladly sign again, write down the traits they share, and search places that group companies by those traits: your customer's rivals, review-site categories, SEC industry codes, investor portfolios, vendors' customer pages, job posts that name the same tools, and lookalike databases such as Crunchbase's Similar Companies. Vet every match by hand against your must-haves. A lookalike shows who could buy, not when, so wait for a timing signal before you write.

Here is how to find companies similar to the customers who pay you best. The method is the same for an agency, a consultancy or a B2B startup; only the traits change.

---

## Choose the customers worth copying

Copy outcomes, not logos. The famous name on your homepage may have taken nine months to close and a discount to keep. Pick three to five accounts that:

- signed without a long fight;
- paid on time, at your normal price;
- stayed past the first renewal or the first project;
- used what they bought;
- sent you a referral, or would.

If those five split into two groups, such as software companies and staffing firms, you have two profiles. Run two searches, never one blended search.

For each of them, write down what was happening when they bought: a new hire, a funding round, a migration, a new leader. That situation is the timing you will look for later.

---

## The traits that make two companies alike

"Similar" should mean similar on the traits that predicted the sale. Write them down, then mark three or four as must-haves. The rest break ties.

| Trait | Example from a best customer | Where to check it on a lookalike |
|---|---|---|
| What they sell | Scheduling software for veterinary clinics | Homepage headline, product pages |
| Who they sell to | Clinic groups with five or more locations | Customers page, case studies |
| How they sell | Annual contracts sold by a sales team | Pricing page: a price list, or "talk to sales" |
| Size | 60 to 150 staff | Company page, careers page |
| Stage | Raised a Series A in the past 18 months | Press page, funding databases |
| Location | US headquarters, remote team | Contact page, job post locations |
| Tools the team runs | HubSpot and Snowflake | Tools named in its job posts |
| Situation when they bought | Had hired its first RevOps lead weeks before | Job posts, team page |

The two traits most lists skip are who they sell to and how they sell. Two companies in one industry can buy for opposite reasons: a payroll software company and a payroll services bureau share a keyword and little else.

## Seven places to find companies similar to your customers

### 1. Your customer's own rivals

Your best customer knows who it competes with, so ask on your next call. Then search for "[customer] competitors", "[customer] alternatives" and "[customer] vs". Comparison pages name the companies that fight for the same deals. Google's help page on [search operators](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/2466433?hl=en) lists site:, quotes, the minus sign, before:, after: and filetype:, and no operator for similar sites, so search with words.

### 2. Review-site categories

For a software customer, find its category on G2 or Capterra. The category page lists the products buyers weigh against it, and the companies behind them often sell to the same market. Skip the giants at the top of the list and look at the companies closest in size to your customer.

### 3. Industry codes

Companies that file with the SEC carry a Standard Industrial Classification code in their EDGAR filings, which indicates their type of business; 7372, for example, is prepackaged software ([SEC SIC code list](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/standard-industrial-classification-sic-code-list)). EDGAR's company search lists every filer under one code, with its state. The list keeps long-gone filers, so check each company for recent filings.

### 4. Investor portfolios and accelerator batches

The fund that backed your customer often backs companies at the same stage, and its portfolio page lists them. [Y Combinator's startup directory](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies) filters its companies by batch, industry, region, company size and whether they are hiring.

### 5. Vendors' customer pages

Companies that sell to the same buyers as you publish logo walls and case studies. A vertical software vendor's case studies are a list of companies in that vertical, picked by the vendor's own idea of fit. Trade association member directories and trade show exhibitor lists do the same job in older industries.

### 6. Job posts that name the same tools or roles

If your best customer ran HubSpot and was hiring a RevOps manager when it signed, search job posts for the same tool and the same role. [Hiring signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) explains what each role says about a team, and [companies by the tools in their job posts](/plays/tech-stack) shows the same search as a standing one.

### 7. Lookalike databases

Crunchbase company profiles include a Similar Companies section. A machine learning model builds it from industry tags, company descriptions, firmographics and financial data, and Crunchbase notes that full access to its predictions is for Business customers ([Crunchbase](https://support.crunchbase.com/hc/en-us/articles/360052260893-Navigating-a-company-profile-on-Crunchbase)). Treat every suggestion as a candidate to vet, not a verdict.

Ad platforms use the same word for something else. Meta describes a lookalike audience as a way for your ads to reach new people who share characteristics with your existing customers ([Meta](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/164749007013531)). It is a setting for ad delivery, not a list of companies you can research.

## Vet each lookalike before it goes on the list

A model or a category page proposes; you decide. Run every candidate through five checks:

- **Must-haves, pass or fail.** One failed must-have takes the company off the list, however good the rest looks.
- **False twins.** An agency that "does SaaS marketing" is not a SaaS company. Read the homepage and the pricing page before you trust a category tag.
- **The situation.** Check whether the lookalike is where your customer was when it bought. A company that hired its RevOps lead last year is past that moment.
- **A spot check.** Vet the first 20 by hand. If fewer than half pass, your traits are too loose: tighten them before you pull more.
- **Exclusions.** Remove current customers, open deals and partners. Agencies should also check their conflict rules, since some clients expect you not to work for their direct rivals.

---

## Add a reason to write before you reach out

A lookalike tells you a company could buy. It says nothing about this month. Before you write, look for one of these at the account:

- a job post for the team you support;
- a recent funding round;
- a tool you work with, named in its job posts;
- a post by someone there about the problem you solve.

[Buying signals in B2B](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) lists more, with where to see each one. Accounts with no signal go on a watch list that you recheck once a month.

Never open with the resemblance. "You remind us of one of our clients" reads as surveillance, and naming that client without permission breaks a confidence. Ask about the situation instead.

---

## Find the people you sell to at each lookalike

Look for the title that bought at your best customer. If a head of operations signed there, find the head of operations here, and keep the signer for later. [How to find decision makers in a company](/blog/how-to-find-decision-makers) covers the sources and the checks. Then put each account, its signal and its people into your [prospect list](/blog/how-to-build-a-prospect-list).
