**TL;DR:** Companies hire an outside development team when their roadmap outgrows the people they have: right after a funding round, while engineering roles stay open, or when an old system needs a rebuild nobody in-house can take on. Reach them at that moment through past clients and the people who leave them, partners who hear about projects first, and outreach tied to the signal. Clutch and marketplaces mostly help buyers check you, and AWS's partner program pays off once you already have clients on AWS.

This is how to get clients for a software development company, a product studio or an outsourcing firm in 2026: who buys, the moments that start a project, eight channels ranked, and how to work the two strongest signals.

---

## Who hires an outside development team

Four kinds of buyers sign most outside development work, and each decides on different evidence.

- **Startup founders**, usually under 50 people. They need a first product or a second version shipped before the next round. The founder or a lone CTO decides, fast, on trust and judgment more than process.
- **Scale-ups and mid-market software companies.** They need capacity in a stack they already run, often as a team that joins their sprints. A VP of Engineering decides and checks references in that stack.
- **Companies outside software**, in logistics, insurance, healthcare or manufacturing. They need internal tools, integrations or a customer portal. A COO or CIO decides and wants proof from their own industry.
- **Enterprises.** They buy through vendor lists, security reviews and procurement. Cloud partner status and audited processes weigh more than your portfolio.

A ten-person shop can win the first three. Enterprise work tends to arrive later, through a partner program or a larger firm that subcontracts. Outsourcing and nearshore firms sell to the same four buyers, with one extra question to answer, covered near the end: what working across borders will feel like week to week.

## The moments that start a software project

A project starts at a moment, and each moment leaves a public trace you can find before the buyer asks anyone for quotes.

| Moment | What the company needs | Who decides | Where it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| A funding round | A product shipped before the next raise | Founder or CTO | Funding announcements, SEC Form D filings |
| Engineering roles open for weeks | Capacity now, while recruiting runs | VP of Engineering or CTO | Job posts, reposted roles, hiring threads |
| A rebuild or a rescue | A new system, or a project taken over from a vendor that failed | CTO or COO | Posts about tech debt, a rebuild or a missed launch |
| A new product line or AI feature | Skills the team doesn't have yet | Head of product | Job posts naming a new framework or model provider |

The funding and hiring rows are the easiest to watch, and two sections below show how. The rebuild row is rarer and worth the hunt: a team that lost a quarter to a failed release wants a steady pair of hands, not the cheapest bid.

## Software development agency clients: 8 channels, ranked

The order weighs how fast each channel turns into a signed project for a shop of 5 to 50 people, and how large that project tends to be. It is our judgment, not survey data.

### 1. Past clients, and the people who leave them

Your best leads already know how you work. Past clients need a second phase, maintenance, or the product they shelved last year. And the people who signed off on your work change jobs: the product manager who hired you becomes a CTO somewhere else, with a budget and no team yet.

Keep a list of everyone who approved a project with you, where they work now and what their new company builds. A short note when one of them starts a new role is the cheapest lead you will ever get.

**Monday task:** export every client contact from the last three years, check where each person works today, and write to the five who moved.

### 2. Partners who hear about projects first

Several kinds of firms see development work before anyone writes a spec:

- **Design and UX studios** that don't build. Their clients ask "who can build this?" at the end of the engagement.
- **Fractional CTOs and technical advisors.** They scope the work and need a team they trust to do it.
- **Accelerators and VC platform teams.** Some keep a list of service providers they recommend to portfolio founders. Ask to be on it.
- **Web and no-code studios** whose clients outgrew Webflow, Bubble or a WordPress plugin. If you sell websites too, the [web design client playbook](/blog/how-to-get-web-design-clients) covers that side.

Offer a referral fee, or build white label under the studio's name, and keep the first shared project small: a technical audit or a two-week prototype.

### 3. Outreach tied to a signal

Outreach works for a dev shop when it starts from one of the moments above: a raise, a role that stays open, a post about a rebuild. A message that names the moment reads as relevant. One that lists your tech stack reads as spam.

The first message asks one question about the signal and pitches nothing: "Saw the senior backend role has been open since September. Is the roadmap waiting on that hire?" This channel suits shops that sell to startups and B2B software companies, because those buyers leave traces in public. It does little for a shop whose clients are local businesses that never post a job. It is also the channel [Pancake for software development agencies](/for/software-development-agencies) runs, and the two sections after this list show the manual version.

### 4. Cloud partner programs

If your clients build on AWS, its partner program puts you where mid-market and enterprise buyers look. As of November 2026, the Select tier of [AWS's Services path](https://aws.amazon.com/partners/services-tiers/) costs $2,500 a year. It asks for four accredited people (two technical, two business), two people with a foundational AWS certification and two with a technical one, and three launched opportunities with a combined monthly recurring revenue of at least $1,500. Select brings a listing in AWS Partner Solutions Finder and access to the APN Customer Engagements (ACE) program for partner opportunities.

The certifications take weeks and the launched opportunities have to come from clients you won elsewhere, so start once you have them. The other clouds run their own partner programs; pick the one your clients already pay for.

### 5. Clutch and review directories

Many buyers check a dev shop on Clutch before a call, and it is crowded: the custom software development category lists [58,095 companies as of November 2026](https://clutch.co/developers). Buyers narrow it by budget, location, tech stack and team size, then read verified reviews.

A bare profile does little. Choose the filters you can win (one stack, one or two industries, an honest minimum project size), collect reviews from three to five clients who match them, and link the profile in every proposal. Skip paid placement until a buyer filtering for your stack would pick you on reviews alone.

### 6. Hiring threads and job boards, read as leads

A job post is a public note that a team lacks people. Hacker News runs a [monthly "Who is hiring?" thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/submitted?id=whoishiring) where startups describe their roles, stack and location, and startup job boards carry the same signal. Look for senior roles reposted for a month or more, founding engineer roles at seed-stage startups, and stacks that match yours.

Write to the founder or engineering lead directly, not in the thread, and respect posts that say no agencies. Offer what a hire can't: a team that starts next week and carries the release while recruiting runs.

### 7. Proof that shows how you think

Buyers of custom software are buying judgment, so show yours in public. Write a case study around one hard decision: why you chose a queue over a scheduled job, or what you cut to hit a launch date. Publish the postmortem of a rescue project, maintain an open-source library, give a talk at a local meetup. These pieces bring few inquiries on their own, but prospects read them before they reply, and they help every other channel close.

### 8. Marketplaces

Upwork and similar marketplaces can bring a new shop its first paid projects and reviews. They are a hard place to sell a six-figure build: buyers compare bids side by side, and many jobs are small and fixed-price. Use them for references in a new stack or market, then move your selling time to channels 1 to 3.

## Finding startups that just raised

A round gives a startup money and a deadline, because the next raise depends on what ships. The founders want to hire, hiring takes months, and the gap between the announcement and a full team is where an outside team fits.

You can see rounds in three places:

1. **Announcements** on the startup's blog and social posts, and in funding newsletters that cover your region.
2. **SEC Form D filings.** A US company raising under Regulation D [must file a Form D within 15 days after the first sale of securities](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/exempt-offerings/filing-form-d-notice), and you can read the filings on EDGAR. Many filers are funds and property vehicles, so expect to skim.
3. **A burst of new engineering roles.** A company that opens five at once has money to spend and a plan that needs people.

Write within a few weeks of the news, before the hiring plan hardens, and ask about the plan rather than offering yourself: "Congrats on the round. What has to ship before the next one?" The [guide to finding recently funded startups](/blog/how-to-find-recently-funded-startups) goes deeper on sources, and Pancake can [search startups by round, date and size](/plays/recently-funded-startups).

## When a company can't hire engineers fast enough

A role that stays open tells you more than a new one. The company has budget, a roadmap waiting and a recruiting process that isn't working. Read the post for three things:

- **The stack.** A post naming Flutter, Django or React Native tells you whether your team fits before you write.
- **The seniority.** A founding engineer role at a small startup means nobody in-house owns that area yet.
- **The age.** A role reposted for a month or more means the search is struggling.

Offer to take a defined piece of the roadmap while they recruit, then hand it to the new hire with documentation. Don't pitch yourself as a replacement for the hire: the hiring manager wants the person, and needs the roadmap to move in the meantime. Write to the CTO or VP of Engineering, not the recruiter. ## Selling from another country

Nearshore and offshore firms sell the same projects with one extra objection: will this feel like working with a team down the hall? Answer it with specifics a buyer can check:

- the hours of overlap with the client's working day, written into the proposal;
- one named lead who joins their standups and speaks for your team;
- references from two or three clients in the buyer's country;
- a contract that assigns the IP to the client and names the governing law;
- a paid two-week discovery sprint before a long commitment.

For these buyers, predictability beats price. A company that has been let down by a remote vendor before is paying for a lower chance of a repeat. Where your team sits doesn't change who you can reach: the signals above work the same from Lisbon, Bogotá or Kraków.
