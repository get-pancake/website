**TL;DR:** A job post is a budget someone already approved, a problem written in the company's own words, and a dated record of when the work became urgent. Hiring signals pay off when you match the role to what that team buys next, write to the manager the role reports to, and reach out while the post is open. SHRM's 2025 benchmark puts the median time to fill a non-executive role at 44 days, and the new hire's first months bring a second window.

This post reads one signal closely, for founders and anyone else who sells to businesses. The [guide to buying signals](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) covers the others.

## Why a job post is a buying signal

Four things make a job post more useful to a seller than most company news.

- **The budget exists.** A salary is a multi-year commitment, not a trial subscription. Someone senior signed off, so the work has money and an owner.
- **The problem is written down.** The responsibilities list the work that isn't getting done today. The requirements list the tools and methods the team already uses.
- **It's dated.** You can tell when the need surfaced, which no press release about "growth plans" tells you.
- **It names the team.** The title, the department and often a "reports to" line point at the person who buys.

A post means a decision about this work is under way. It doesn't mean the decision goes your way, so treat it as a reason to ask a question, not a reason to pitch.

### Three purchases follow a hire

What sellers win from a job post tends to take one of three shapes:

1. **Tools for the new hire.** Seats, data, training, and the software the person needs in their first week.
2. **Cover while they hire.** An agency, a contractor or a fractional lead who carries the work until the seat is filled.
3. **Software that does part of the job.** A product that handles the repetitive share of the role, so the team needs fewer hires.

Know which of the three you sell before you read a single post. A studio pitching cover writes differently from a software company pitching seats.

## Five details that sharpen the signal

The job title gets you to the post. These details decide whether you write.

1. **The first posting date.** A board's "new" label can mean a repost. [How to find companies that are hiring](/blog/how-to-find-companies-that-are-hiring) shows where the original date hides. A post that has stayed up for months is hard to fill or always open, and both make weaker signals.
2. **The pay range.** In California, an employer with 15 or more employees must include the pay scale in any job posting, according to the [Labor Commissioner's pay transparency FAQ](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/california_equal_pay_act.htm). Set the range against the title: it shows how senior the role is and what the company will spend on the problem.
3. **The "reports to" line.** When a post has one, it names your buyer.
4. **The count.** One opening may be a backfill. Five openings for the same role is a team being built, and a new team needs tools.
5. **The verbs.** "Build from scratch," "migrate" and "clean up" describe a project with a start and an end. "Maintain" and "support" describe steady work. Projects tend to buy sooner. The tools in the requirements give you the stack, a signal of its own: [companies by the tools in their job posts](/plays/tech-stack).

Check who posted it, too. A staffing agency's listing hides the employer, so it tells you about the agency's client list, not about a company you can reach.

## How soon to reach out after a job post

There are two windows, and the second is easy to miss.

**While the role is open.** SHRM's [2025 Recruiting Benchmarking report](https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/research/2025-recruiting-benchmarking-report.pdf) found a median of 44 days from opening a requisition to an accepted offer for non-executive roles, and 45 days for executive roles. Extra-large organizations ran slower, at 61 days for non-executive roles. Write in the first two or three weeks, while the manager is still deciding what the new person will own and what gets bought around them.

**After the hire starts.** A new hire arrives with a mandate and fresh eyes, and early on they have the most room to change what they inherited. That makes the 90 days after the start date a second opening, especially for the roles marked "first 90 days" in the table.

Freshness beats volume. Treat a post older than about a month as weak unless it was reposted with changes. When a post comes down, the role was filled, paused or dropped, and your question changes with it.

Hiring often follows money. A company that posts five roles a few weeks after announcing a round is spending the plan, which makes the pair stronger than either signal alone. [How to find startups that raised money](/blog/how-to-find-recently-funded-startups) covers the funding half.

## Hiring intent data or job boards by hand

"Hiring intent data" is the vendor name for job posts sold as sales data: postings collected from job boards and career pages, matched to companies, and made filterable by title, date, location and company size. Some providers also extract the tools each post names.

Buying data gets you coverage and dates. Reading boards by hand gets you context and costs only time. Whichever you choose, run three filters before anything reaches your list:

- **Fresh:** posted inside the window you chose, judged by the posting date rather than the date a board scraped it.
- **First-party:** posted by the employer, not by a staffing agency on a client's behalf.
- **Mapped:** a role that matches a row in your version of the table above.

A founder with twenty target accounts can check their career pages by hand. A seller covering a whole market needs the data, or a search that runs on its own.

## Messages that use the post without sounding like a recruiter

Write to the person who owns the problem: the hiring manager, or the leader the role reports to. The recruiter's job is filling the seat, and the budget for tools and outside help rarely sits with them.

Then ask about the work, not the hiring. Three openers:

- **Selling to sales teams:** "Saw five SDR openings on your careers page. Will the new reps start on inbound leads, or go outbound from week one?"
- **Selling data services:** "The data engineer post mentions moving reporting off spreadsheets. Is the warehouse chosen yet, or is that the new hire's first project?"
- **Selling to finance teams:** "The controller post mentions a first audit. Is the month-end close still run in spreadsheets?"

Each one quotes the post, asks a question only that company can answer, and leaves the pitch for the reply. Don't mention candidates, don't offer to "help with the hire" unless you recruit, and never pretend to be an applicant.
