**TL;DR:** Retained mandates go to firms that a CEO, an investor or a board member already trusts, so executive search business development is mostly relationship work: past clients, the executives you placed, the finalists you didn't, and the investors who recommend firms to their companies. Add outreach to companies that show a leadership hire coming, such as a VP role still posted after six weeks, a fresh round or a first VP hire. Then win the pitch with a sharper read of the seat than the firms beside you. Confidentiality limits what you can say at every step.

This guide covers executive search firm business development for partners and principals at boutique retained firms. If you run a contingent desk, [how to get clients for a recruitment agency](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-recruitment-agency) fits your model better.

## Where retained search clients come from

Retained work tends to come from people who have watched a search run well, from either side of the table. Seven sources feed a retained firm, and they move at different speeds.

| Source | What starts the conversation | Time to a pitch | Who signs | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Past clients | The next leadership hire, or a CEO who moved | Weeks | CEO or CHRO | Each new client can shrink your candidate pool |
| Executives you placed | The team they rebuild | 3 to 12 months after they start | The executive you placed | Let them settle in first |
| Finalists you didn't place | A seat at their next company | Months to years | The finalist, once they hire | Stay useful without pitching |
| Investors and board members | A leadership gap in a company they back | Weeks to months | CEO, with board input | Some keep a preferred-firm list |
| Coaches, compensation consultants, deal lawyers | A leadership change they advise on | Months | CEO or board | Send referrals back |
| Companies showing a signal | A posted VP role, a round, a first VP hire | 3 to 10 weeks | CEO or founder | Most C-level searches are never posted |
| A point of view in public | A CEO who read your market notes | 6 to 12 months | CEO | Slow, but it lifts every other source |

The times are our estimates for a boutique with a track record in one function, not survey data. Executive coaches work with the same CEOs, and search firms are one of their referral sources: [how executive coaches get clients](/blog/how-to-get-executive-coaching-clients) shows where your paths cross, and where a referral can go both ways.

## Placed executives and finalists: the clients you already made

The executive you place this year may be hiring their own VPs next year. New leaders inherit teams they didn't choose, and the firm that found them is a natural first call, if it stayed in touch.

Keep in touch on a calendar, not when you need work:

- **At 90 days,** call to ask how the role compares with the brief. It's good service, and it surfaces the first gaps in their team.
- **At one year,** ask which seat they would fill next if the budget appeared.
- **When they change companies,** which you'll have to spot by hand, write within their first month. A new company means a new leadership team to shape.

Finalists who lost a search matter as much. The runner-up remembers who ran a fair, fast process and who called them before the news went out. Tell every finalist the outcome yourself, then think of them first for the next role that fits.

Track both groups in one sheet: name, company, start date, next check-in. Give placed executives the care you give paying clients, because for business development they are worth as much.

## Signals that a leadership search is coming

Some searches show up in public before anyone calls a firm. Four are worth watching.

### A VP or C-level role still posted after six weeks

Many companies try their own recruiters or a contingent agency first. A VP post still live after six or eight weeks suggests that route hasn't produced a shortlist. Read the date the post first went up, not its latest repost; [reading job posts as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) covers the other details that sharpen one. Then ask the CEO one question: "What has made the search harder than you expected?"

### A funding round

A round pays for a hiring plan, and the plan often names leaders: a first VP of Sales after a Series A, a CFO who can run the next raise. Watch [recently funded companies](/plays/recently-funded-startups) in your sector and write once the announcement settles: "Congrats on the round. Which leadership seat does the plan fill first?"

### A company hiring its first VP

Say a 70-person company posts its first VP of Engineering. Its CEO may never have hired at that level, and needs help defining the role and the pay as much as finding people. Check the leadership page: no VP in that function means a first. Offer a scoping conversation before you offer a search: "Is this the first VP you've hired? What has to be true a year after they start?" [Pancake's page for executive search firms](/for/executive-search-firms) shows these hiring signals set up as searches.

### Public-company departures, watched by hand

When a public company's CEO, president, CFO, principal accounting officer, COO or another named executive officer retires, resigns or is terminated, the company must disclose it in a Form 8-K under Item 5.02, generally within four business days ([SEC Form 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/files/form8-k.pdf)). The successor search may already belong to a firm. Pitch the layer below instead, since a new CEO or CFO may reshape the team under them.

## How to win retained search when you pitch

A retained mandate goes to the firm that shows the CEO it already understands the seat. When firms pitch side by side with similar fees and processes, that read decides it. Six moves:

1. **Restate the role as a problem.** Not "VP of Sales," but "someone who moves sales from founder-led deals to a team that closes without the CEO, within four quarters."
2. **Show the market before you're hired.** A map of where plausible candidates work today, what they earn and why they would move. No names.
3. **Name the trade-off.** If the profile the CEO describes rarely exists at the pay on offer, say so in the room.
4. **Commit to dates.** A calibration slate within the first weeks, a shortlist date and a weekly update.
5. **Bring references for the same seat.** Two CEOs who hired a similar role through you, and a finalist who didn't get it.
6. **Put the terms on one page.** Fee stages, what counts as a completed search, your off-limits scope, and what happens if the hire leaves early.

Write the pitch for the CEO, then send it to the board member or investor who will ask the CEO about it. They often decide which firms get asked to pitch at all.

## Confidentiality: what executive search business development can't say

Retained search runs on information clients and candidates trust you to keep, and a firm that leaks it in a sales conversation loses the client and the next pitch. [AESC's standards](https://www.aesc.org/standards/) commit member firms to protecting the confidential information clients and candidates entrust to them, data privacy and security included.

Five rules keep business development on the right side of that line:

- **Never name a client or an open search** in outreach, or in a pitch to another company.
- **Keep a placement out of case studies** until the hire is public, and ask the client before you use their name.
- **Describe the pattern, not the company:** "three CFO searches for founder-owned manufacturers this year" says enough.
- **Respect replacement searches.** The incumbent may not know. Never write to a company's executives about "the search for your CFO" unless the client has gone public.
- **Set your off-limits rules before you pitch:** which clients' people you won't approach, and for how long. A new client can close part of your candidate pool, so weigh it before you sign.

Credentials help a boutique here. As of November 2026, [AESC says its member firms](https://www.aesc.org/aesc-for-clients/) pass a vetting that includes an audit of firm practices, client references and member endorsements, and recommit to its standards annually.
