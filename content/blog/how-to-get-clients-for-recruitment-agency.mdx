**TL;DR:** Your quickest fees sit in your own placement history: hiring managers who signed your terms before, and candidates you placed who now run teams. Next come companies whose job posts show a search going badly, such as a role still open after six weeks or three posts for one team. Warm accounts first, stalled searches second: that is how to get clients for a recruitment agency without waiting months. Split-fee networks, supplier lists and content come later and pay less per hour of work.

This page starts with the three kinds of buyer, ranks ten channels by how soon each one tends to produce a first fee, and ends with a weekly routine for a small desk. It covers permanent recruitment. Temp and contract work is a different sale with different buyers, covered in [how to get clients for a staffing agency](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-staffing-agency).

## Founders, hiring managers and talent teams buy differently

The person who signs your terms decides which channel can reach them. Most agencies sell to three kinds of buyer.

- **Founders of companies under about 50 people.** Nobody on staff recruits full time, so the founder runs every search and feels each week a seat stays empty. They answer a message tied to a real reason, agree terms on one call, and rarely keep a supplier list. Funding rounds and "we're hiring" posts point to them.
- **Hiring managers at companies with a talent team.** The head of sales owns the pain. The talent acquisition team owns the agency budget and the terms. Win the manager with something useful about the candidate market, then expect the talent team to review your terms before a role arrives.
- **Talent and procurement teams at large employers.** They buy through preferred supplier lists, rate cards and sometimes tenders. Approval takes months and fees get negotiated, but one approval can feed a desk for years.

Executive search sells to CEOs and boards, and many senior searches are never advertised: [Pancake's page for executive search firms](/for/executive-search-firms) covers that sale.

Sort your last ten fees by buyer type before you pick channels. The type that paid most is where the next client is likely to come from.

## 1. Past clients and the people you placed

Your next client has probably paid you before, or been placed by you. Every placement leaves two future buyers behind: the manager who signed off, and the candidate who will run a team two jobs from now.

Most agencies under-work this list because nobody owns it. Give it an owner and an hour a week.

- **Hiring managers who changed companies.** A manager in a new job inherits open seats and often has no agency yet. Your track record moved with them.
- **Candidates you placed who now manage people.** They know how you work from the other side of the table. Ask how the team is growing, not whether they need an agency.
- **Clients who went quiet.** A client with no roles for a year may have built a talent team, frozen headcount or found a cheaper agency. One question tells you which.

If you left an agency to start your own, read the restrictive covenants in your old contract before you contact its clients. Breaching one can cost more than the fee.

**This week:** list your placements from the last five years and note where each hiring manager and candidate works today. Write to the five who moved most recently, with one question about their new team.

## 2. Roles that have been open too long

A job post still live after six weeks is the clearest sign that a company needs outside help. [SHRM's 2026 benchmarking](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/recruiting-benchmarking) puts the median time to fill a nonexecutive role at 39 calendar days, so a post that has been up for six weeks has already outlasted the typical search.

Three patterns are worth a message:

- **The same post, six weeks or older.** Read the date it first went up, not the date of its latest repost.
- **A repost with a new title or a higher pay range.** The first version didn't work, and the company knows it.
- **One role posted on several boards at once.** The talent team is widening the net, which often means the first channel came up short.

Skip posts that say "no agencies". Plenty of open roles carry no such line.

Write to the hiring manager, not the recruiter who posted the job. The manager feels the empty seat, and the talent team will still review your terms once the manager wants you. Ask what is making the role hard to fill, and offer nothing until they answer. Hiring is the signal [Pancake for recruiting agencies](/for/recruiting-agencies) starts from, and [how to find companies that are hiring](/blog/how-to-find-companies-that-are-hiring) shows where to see these posts and how to date them by hand.

## 3. Hiring bursts and fresh funding

Several posts for one team in a short window mean a plan, a budget and a deadline. A company that opens four account executive roles in a month is building a sales team, and the person building it rarely has time to screen every applicant.

Funding is the same signal, one step earlier. A round announced this month often turns into job posts next quarter, and in a young company the founder who raised it still runs the hiring. Rounds appear in press releases and funding databases, and you can [search recently funded startups](/plays/recently-funded-startups) by round, date and company size.

Ask about the plan, not the money: "Saw the seed round close in October. Which role do you need filled first?"

---

## 4. Managers posting about their team

When a VP of sales posts that they are "building out the team", they have named the search, the owner and the urgency in one line. These posts are rarer than job posts, but warmer, because the manager wrote them.

Two related signals:

- **Comments on a rival agency's posts.** A head of talent who comments on a competitor's post about slow compliance searches has told you their problem. Leave the rival out of your message and ask about the problem.
- **Founders posting about the first hire in a function.** A first salesperson or a first data engineer is hard to get right, and founders know it.

## 5. Leading with a candidate

Some desks open accounts by presenting one strong candidate to companies that haven't advertised a role: the most placeable candidate (MPC) approach. It works for scarce skills, when the candidate wants a move and the company is plausibly hiring.

It also carries the most risk. A CV sent without the candidate's agreement can land with their current employer or start a fee dispute with another agency. In Great Britain, [the Conduct of Employment Agencies and Employment Businesses Regulations 2003](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/3319/regulation/19) bar an agency from introducing a work-seeker to a hirer without confirming the person is willing to work in the position the hirer wants to fill. Ask the candidate first, company by company. [Recruitment business development messages](/blog/recruitment-business-development-messages) covers the spec CV question in full, with first messages that work better.

## 6. Split-fee networks

A split network turns other recruiters' clients into roles you can work. One recruiter holds the job order, another brings the candidate, and they share the fee on terms agreed in advance.

[Top Echelon's TE Network](https://topechelon.com/product/te-network/) serves recruiters and placement agencies in the US and Canada and counts over 500 member agencies. As of November 2026, membership costs a one-time $350 start-up fee and $150 a month, plus a 6% brokerage fee on placements made through the network. [NPAworldwide](https://npaworldwide.com/) runs a split-placement network of independent firms across Europe, Asia, Australia, Africa and the Americas.

Use a network in both directions: post the roles you can't fill alone, and work other members' roles in your niche. It brings roles more than clients, since the client relationship stays with the member who holds the job order.

## 7. Recruiter marketplaces

Marketplaces flip the model: companies post roles, and independent recruiters choose which ones to work. [Paraform's page for recruiters](https://www.paraform.com/recruiters) advertises access to more than 1,000 vetted clients, and its earnings calculator assumes an 80% success-fee split, as of November 2026.

It suits a solo recruiter with a strong candidate network and gaps in the client list. The trade-offs: other recruiters may work the same role, and the company signed up with the platform, not with you. Check the platform's rules on dealing with a company directly before you count on repeat work.

---

## 8. Preferred supplier lists and tenders

Large employers route agency work through a preferred supplier list (PSL), owned by the talent team or procurement. Getting on one is slow, but a place on it brings roles you never had to find.

Three things move a PSL decision: a hiring manager who asks for you by name, proof that you fill the roles current suppliers miss, and paperwork that is ready before anyone asks for it, such as insurance certificates, data protection terms and references. Ask when the list is next reviewed and work toward that date. Public-sector buyers run the same process as formal tenders, on longer timelines.

---

## 9. Referral partners

Some people hear about hiring plans before any job is posted. Pick partners whose clients look like yours:

- HR consultants and fractional HR leaders, who design hiring processes but don't run searches;
- venture firms' talent partners, who help portfolio companies hire;
- employment lawyers and accountants, if you place into small businesses.

Offer a referral fee or send work back. Either way, ask for one introduction to a named company, not a general promise to keep you in mind.

---

## 10. Salary guides, market maps and events

Content works for an agency when it holds data a hiring manager can't get elsewhere. Build a short salary guide for your niche from the offers you have seen, and send it to the managers on your list with no ask attached. A market map of who is hiring for your roles does the same job.

Events work at a smaller scale. Host a breakfast for eight hiring managers from one function around one question, such as why senior data hires take so long. You will learn more about their searches in an hour than a booth at a job fair teaches in a day.

## A BD week for a two-person desk

The channels only pay if someone works them on a schedule. Here is a starting week for a desk of two, with about six hours of business development each:

1. **Monday:** pull roles in your niche open six weeks or more, plus new funding rounds. Pick 15 hiring managers.
2. **Tuesday:** write to those 15, one question each. Write to five past clients or placed candidates.
3. **Wednesday:** follow up on last week's unanswered messages with something new, such as a salary figure or a fact about the candidate market.
4. **Thursday:** one partner conversation, and one role worked or posted in a split network.
5. **Friday:** count conversations started, calls booked and terms sent. Drop any channel that has produced nothing in six weeks.

## Terms that turn a first fee into an account

How you sign the first role decides whether a second one follows.

- **Trade a shortlist deadline for exclusivity.** On a role open for two months, a shortlist within ten working days is worth more to the client than a discount. Ask for a few weeks without other agencies in return.
- **Offer a retained or engaged option for senior searches.** Part of the fee up front buys commitment on both sides.
- **Agree what happens if the hire leaves early.** A clear rebate or replacement clause settles the question before it becomes a dispute.
- **Ask about the next role at the first placement.** The manager who just hired is already planning the next seat.
