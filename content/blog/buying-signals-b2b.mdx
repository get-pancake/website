**TL;DR:** Buying signals are public traces that a company may need what you sell soon: a job post for the team you support, a fresh funding round, a post about the problem you fix, a comment under a competitor's launch. Fit tells you who could buy. A signal tells you when to write and what to ask. Below are 18 B2B buying signals in five groups, with where to see each one, the 13 you can turn into a standing search, and the 5 worth checking by hand.

With one hour this week, start with hiring signals: they are the easiest to find and to write about.

---

## What buying signals are, and what they are not

A buying signal answers one question about one account: why now? It is evidence of timing, not proof of intent. A company hiring a data engineer may never hire your data consultancy. The job post tells you a decision about data is being made, and roughly who is making it.

Three kinds of evidence get called signals, and lists that blend them send people after the wrong accounts:

- **Fit** says who could buy: the right industry, size, location and role. It never expires, and it never says when.
- **Trigger events** say when: a role posted, a round announced, a tool named in a new job post.
- **Engagement** says who cares, by name: someone posted about the problem, commented under a rival, reacted to an expert.

"Intent signals" usually means a fourth thing: research behavior sold as data, such as which companies browsed a software category on a review site. It points at a company, rarely at a person, so you still have to work out who to talk to.

A signal earns a message when it passes three tests. It is recent. It points to a person or a team you can reach. And it lets you ask a question that would make no sense sent to anyone else.

## Hiring signals: a job post says the budget exists

A job post is the most candid document a company publishes. Someone approved headcount, described the problem in plain words and often listed the tools the team works in. Read three parts of every post before you write:

1. **The role.** A company hiring SDRs has a pipeline target and will need what an outbound team needs: data, training, tooling. A company hiring a head of enablement is about to have an owner for onboarding.
2. **The tools.** "Experience with Snowflake and dbt" tells you what the data team runs. "Workday experience is a plus" can mean a migration is coming.
3. **The gap.** Phrases like "build the function", "clean up our CRM" or "own the roadmap from day one" say what hurts.

Your message goes to whoever owns that gap: the hiring manager or the leader above the role, not the recruiter who posted it. Offer to carry the work while they hire, or to support the new hire once they start.

Two traps. Staffing agencies post jobs for their clients, and those posts tell you about the agency, not the employer. And a role reposted for months may be hard to fill or never meant to be filled, so check before you build a pitch on it.

## Funding signals: a round leaves a paper trail

A funding round means money, a plan, and a board waiting for results. The plan usually means hiring and new tools, which is where most B2B sellers come in.

Many US rounds leave a public record. Under Regulation D, a company has 15 days from the first sale of securities to file a Form D notice, which then becomes public on EDGAR, [as the SEC's guide to Form D explains](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/capital-raising-building-blocks/what-form-d). Press releases and funding databases cover the announced rounds. No source is complete: an announcement can come long after the money arrives, and some rounds are never announced at all.

Write to whoever will spend the part you can help with: the new head of sales, the head of marketing, the CTO. The founder's inbox fills with congratulations in announcement week. A note sent once the job posts for the plan appear can ask about something specific.

As a search, the funding signal looks like [recently funded startups](/plays/recently-funded-startups). [How to find startups that raised money](/blog/how-to-find-recently-funded-startups) walks through the sources and how to read a round.

## Conversation signals: posts, comments and reactions

Company signals point to an account. Conversation signals point to a person, and often to the exact words they use for their problem. Six are worth watching:

- **Posts about the problem.** "We're rebuilding onboarding" or "our SOC 2 audit starts in March". Search the words buyers use, not your category's jargon.
- **Engagement with a competitor.** Comments and reactions under a rival's launch or webinar. The person knows the category. They may be a customer, a prospect or a curious peer, so ask before you assume.
- **Engagement with an expert.** The voices your market follows. A weaker signal than a rival's audience, from a larger pool.
- **Engagement with your own posts.** The warmest of the six: they have already seen your name.
- **Reactions to one post.** When a post about one narrow problem spreads, the people who reacted have sorted themselves for you.
- **A request for help.** "Can anyone recommend a fractional CFO?" is as direct as signals get. Answer in the thread with something useful, then follow up in a direct message (DM). RFP portals are the formal version, and you check those by hand.

Open with the topic, never with the fact that you were watching. "You wrote that onboarding drags past the first month. What does week one look like now?" reads as a reply to a person. A message that opens by saying you saw them react to a competitor reads as surveillance. [Competitor engagement signals](/blog/competitor-engagement-signals) covers that etiquette in detail.

## Fit signals: lookalikes, role match and web evidence

Fit signals tell you who could buy and nothing about when. Use them to draw the map, then add a timing signal before you write.

- **Lookalikes.** Name your best customer and find companies like it: same model, size and buyer. Useful for a first list in a new market.
- **Role match.** Controllers at manufacturers with 100 to 1,000 people in Ohio. It is a filter, not a reason to write.
- **Web evidence.** A fact the company states in public, with the page to prove it: a trust page that lists SOC 2, a published sustainability report, a careers page that says the team is remote-first. Each one names a need or a constraint you can serve.

Fit and timing compound. A lookalike of your best customer that is also hiring the role you support is worth more than a long list of either.

## Five signals to watch by hand

These five are strong, and Pancake doesn't track them. A short pass each week covers them for a list of target accounts.

- **A past champion changes jobs.** The person who bought from you before now has a new team and a new budget. Look through your past clients' contacts once a month and congratulate the movers with one question about the new role.
- **A new executive arrives.** Company newsrooms and press releases announce them. A new leader brings new priorities, and sometimes new partners.
- **A known contact returns to your pricing page.** Some CRMs tie website visits to contacts who have filled in a form or clicked a tracked link. Don't mention the visit. Ask whether anything changed on their side.
- **Research on review sites.** G2's Buyer Intent data reports which companies viewed your product profile, your category, comparison pages or your pricing page on G2, [according to G2's documentation](https://documentation.g2.com/docs/buyer-intent-data-reference). It names companies, not people, and it suits software vendors listed there.
- **Company news.** An acquisition, a new office, a product launch, a layoff. [Google Alerts](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/4815696?hl=en) emails you when new results appear for a company name you choose.

## Sales buying signals inside a conversation

Once you are talking, signals stop being public traces and become questions. These sales buying signals deserve a direct answer and a next step with a date:

- they ask about price, payment terms or contract length;
- they ask how onboarding works, or how soon you could start;
- they ask for a reference in their industry;
- they ask whether a pilot or a smaller first phase is possible;
- they bring a colleague, or ask what security or procurement will need;
- they describe the problem in numbers: hours lost, deals stuck, a deadline.

The opposite signals are as useful. "Send me something" with no next meeting, a champion who can't say who signs, and a deadline that keeps slipping all mean the deal is stalling. Ask what changed, and whether the timing still holds.

## How to act on a buying signal

The method is the same for every row of the table:

1. **Check fit first.** A signal at a company you can't serve is noise, however loud.
2. **Find the owner.** The person whose problem the signal describes: the hiring manager, the new head of People, the founder who wrote the post.
3. **Ask one question tied to the signal.** No pitch, no deck and no calendar link in the first message.
4. **Write while it is fresh.** The job post is still live, the round is still news, the thread still gets comments.
5. **Stack, then keep score.** Two signals at one company beat one. Track which signal types turn into calls, and drop the ones that don't.

First messages, one per signal type:

- Hiring: "Saw the customer success manager opening. Is the team growing because of churn, or because of new accounts?"
- Funding: "Congrats on the round. Which team grows first?"
- A post: "You wrote that new reps take three months to ramp. Where does most of that time go?"
- Web evidence: "Your trust page says SOC 2 Type II is in progress. Who owns the evidence collection?"
- A job change: "Congrats on the new role. What's the first thing you want to fix there?"
