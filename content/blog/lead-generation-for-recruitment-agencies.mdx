**TL;DR:** Lead generation for recruitment agencies works as a weekly routine, not a burst: a list of hiring signals in your niche, five questions that throw out roles you can't fill or won't be paid for, and one lead sheet that follows each lead from first message to signed terms. A job post is raw material. It becomes a lead once you know who owns the hire, what the fee is worth and why they would talk to you this month.

This guide builds that routine. For the channels themselves, from past clients to split-fee networks, start with [how to get clients for a recruitment agency](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-recruitment-agency).

## A job post is not a lead yet

A lead is a named person who owns a hire you can fill, at a fee worth the work, with a dated reason to talk now. Many agencies stop at the job post and skip the rest, which is why their outreach sounds like everyone else's.

Four parts, all required:

1. **The role.** Inside your niche and region, at a level you place.
2. **The owner.** The hiring manager by name, not the careers inbox.
3. **The reason.** A signal with a date: a post that went up this week, a post still open after six weeks, a round announced last month.
4. **The fee.** A pay range that makes the search worth your time.

Pay ranges make the fourth part easier than it used to be. In California, [an employer with 15 or more employees must include the pay scale in any job posting](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/california_equal_pay_act.htm), and a third party that posts the job for them, such as an agency, must include it too. With the range in the post, you can estimate your fee before you write a word.

## Lead generation for recruitment agencies: the signals worth logging

Signals differ in how warm they are and how fast they go stale, so log the date you saw each one. The last column is our rule of thumb, not survey data.

| Signal | What it tells you | Where to see it | Useful for about |
|---|---|---|---|
| A role in your niche posted this week | A search is starting, before other agencies pile in | Job boards, career pages | 2 weeks |
| The same role open six weeks or more | The in-house search is stalling | The post's original date | Until it closes |
| Three or more roles for one team in a month | A team build with a budget behind it | The company's career page | A quarter |
| The first role in a new function | A hard search, often run by the founder | Job posts, founder posts | 1 to 2 months |
| A funding round | A hiring plan is coming | Press releases, funding databases | 3 to 6 months |
| A manager posting about building a team | The owner, in their own words | Social posts | 2 to 4 weeks |
| Comments on a rival agency's posts | Someone who buys agency help | The rival's posts | 2 to 4 weeks |
| Job posts in a new city | A new office, hiring locally | Job posts, press releases | Several months |
| A past client hiring again | A warm account with open roles | Their career page | Until filled |

Large employers deserve a second look this year. [SHRM's 2026 benchmarking](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/recruiting-benchmarking) found a 67% increase in median requisitions per recruiter at extra-large organizations. A talent team stretched that far has less time per role, and its hiring managers notice first.

[Buying signals in B2B](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) explains how each kind of signal works outside recruitment. Where to look for hiring companies, and how to date a post, is covered in [how to find companies that are hiring](/blog/how-to-find-companies-that-are-hiring).

## Score a recruitment lead before anyone writes to it

Score each lead before anyone writes to it. A lead that fails any of the first three questions leaves the sheet.

1. **Can you fill it within a month?** Check the candidates you can reach today, not the pool you hope to build.
2. **Who owns the hire, and can you name them?** A head of engineering, a founder, a plant director. If all you have is the talent team, the lead is weaker.
3. **Is the door open?** Skip posts that say "no agencies", and companies that told you their supplier list is closed.
4. **Is the fee worth the work?** Multiply the midpoint of the pay range by your fee percentage. Below your floor, pass, or offer a smaller service such as a fixed-fee shortlist.
5. **How old is the signal?** A week-old post and a two-month-old post need different first messages: one is a race, the other a rescue.

An example scoring rule: one point for each yes on questions 1 to 4, plus one if the signal is under two weeks old or over six. Write to the fives and fours first, and park the rest until a new signal appears.

## The trouble with bought "companies hiring" lists

Lists of "companies hiring now" are easy to buy and hard to use. The same list goes to every agency that paid for it, so the managers on it hear from several recruiters in the same week. Unless the vendor filters them out, other agencies' postings sit in the list too, and you end up pitching a competitor's listing. Few lists name the hiring manager, show how long a role has been open, or carry the pay range.

For a recruiter, agency listings are pure noise, which is why [Pancake for recruiting agencies](/for/recruiting-agencies) leaves them out of its hiring search. Whatever your source, run each row through the five questions, find the owner and date the signal before it enters your sheet.

## Inbound, partner and outbound leads in one sheet

Outbound signals are the stream you control. Two others belong in the same sheet, so you can compare them.

- **Inbound.** Enquiries from your website, replies to a salary guide you sent, hiring managers who noticed your job ads. Give each niche its own page that names the roles you fill and the kinds of companies you place into.
- **Partners.** Referrals from HR consultants and venture firms' talent partners, and roles shared through a split-fee network.
- **Outbound.** Everything in the signals table above.

Keep the source in its own column. After a quarter, that column tells you which stream to feed.

## Build a lead sheet you will keep using

A spreadsheet works for the first few hundred leads, and a CRM after that. The columns matter more than the tool:

- **Company and role:** "120-person SaaS company, senior data engineer".
- **Signal and its date:** "post first seen 9 September, still open".
- **Owner:** name and title of the hiring manager.
- **Fee estimate:** midpoint of the range times your fee.
- **Source:** inbound, partner or outbound, plus the signal type.
- **Stage:** new, contacted, conversation, terms sent, terms signed, role briefed, placed.
- **Next step and date:** "follow up 18 November with a salary data point".

On Friday, count four numbers: new qualified leads, first messages sent, conversations started and terms signed. Then work backward from your goal. Say your desk wants three new clients a month. If one in eight first messages becomes a conversation and one in three conversations ends in signed terms, you need about 72 first messages a month, or 18 a week. Those ratios are assumptions; replace them with your own after four weeks.

Close out any lead that has sat in "contacted" for three weeks with no reply. It goes back to "new" only when a fresh signal appears, such as a repost or a second role.

## Keep the prospect data clean

A lead sheet holds personal data about hiring managers, and the rules depend on where they are. Under the UK GDPR, when you collect someone's details from a source other than them, [Article 14](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/14) says you must give them privacy information within one month at the latest, or at your first message to them if that comes sooner. The same article asks you to tell them where their data came from, and the EU GDPR carries the same rule.

In practice: link a short privacy notice from your email signature, fill in the source column for every lead, and remove anyone who asks the same week. None of this is legal advice.
