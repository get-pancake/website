## Most companies use one email pattern

Most companies give every employee an address in the same format, so one real address points to the rest. For a person named Dana Halvorsen at example.com, the common formats look like this:

| Format | Address |
|---|---|
| first.last | `dana.halvorsen@example.com` |
| first | `dana@example.com` |
| first initial + last | `dhalvorsen@example.com` |
| first + last initial | `danah@example.com` |
| firstlast | `danahalvorsen@example.com` |
| first_last | `dana_halvorsen@example.com` |
| first initial . last | `d.halvorsen@example.com` |

Edge cases break the pattern. A second Dana Halvorsen gets a middle initial or a number. A nickname can replace a legal name, so Robert becomes Bob. Accents and hyphens get dropped or kept. Regional offices may use a country domain such as example.co.uk, and a company that renamed itself may still run mail on the old domain.

## How to find someone's work email for free

Two real addresses at the same domain settle the pattern. One is only a hint. Places that publish them:

1. **Press releases.** The media contact line often carries a named address.
2. **The company's own pages.** Team pages, careers pages that name a recruiter, case studies, author pages and PDFs. Search `site:example.com "@example.com"` to surface them.
3. **The person's own words.** Personal sites, newsletter footers, talk slides and podcast show notes. Search the name and the domain together: `"dana halvorsen" "@example.com"`.
4. **Your own records.** An old thread, a meeting on your calendar or a signed contract from anyone at that company shows the format.
5. **A free tool allowance.** Each finder and verifier in the table below gives some lookups away every month.
6. **The front door.** A role address such as partnerships@ or the main phone line can route a short note to the right person. It is slower, and it skips the guessing.

Never test guesses by mailing every variant to see which ones bounce. Verify first, then send once.

## How to verify a work email before you send

A verifier answers one question: will this mailbox accept mail? It checks the format, looks up the domain's mail servers, then asks the receiving server whether it accepts that mailbox, without delivering a message. Each result calls for a different move:

- **Valid.** The server accepted the mailbox. Send.
- **Invalid.** The mailbox doesn't exist, or the domain takes no mail. Delete it and try the next pattern.
- **Catch-all, or accept-all.** The server accepts mail for every address at the domain, so no check can tell a real mailbox from a made-up one without sending a real message. Send only when two real addresses confirm the pattern, and send in small batches.
- **Unknown.** The server timed out or refused the check. Try again later before you decide.
- **Role or disposable.** A shared inbox such as sales@, or a throwaway domain. Fine for routing a note, not for reaching a named person.

Every finder and verifier publishes its own accuracy figures. Test before you pay: run 20 addresses you already know, such as colleagues, clients and past partners, and count the misses.

## Is it legal to email someone at work?

In the US, the CAN-SPAM Act sets the rules for commercial email, and the FTC's compliance guide says the law makes no exception for business-to-business email ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)). Every commercial message must:

- use accurate "From", "To", "Reply-To" and routing information;
- carry a subject line that matches the content;
- make clear that it is an advertisement, in words you choose;
- include your valid physical postal address;
- explain how to opt out, and honor each opt-out within 10 business days.

As of November 2026, the FTC puts the penalty at up to $53,088 for each email in violation. Its guide also lists harvesting addresses, or generating them through a dictionary attack, among the aggravated violations that can bring criminal penalties.

In the EU, a work address with a person's name in it is personal data. The European Commission gives `name.surname@company.com` as an example of personal data and `info@company.com` as an example of data that isn't ([European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/application-gdpr_en)). The Commission adds that the GDPR's rules apply to business email addresses like these. If you write to people in the EU or the UK, check the rules for each country first. This section is general information, not legal advice.
