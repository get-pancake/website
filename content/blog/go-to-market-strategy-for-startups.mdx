**TL;DR:** A go-to-market strategy for startups fits on one page: the buyer and the moment they need you, one sales motion your price can pay for, two channels, a first price, and a few numbers you check each week. Before the tenth customer, the founder sells, whatever the product. Those first deals decide what comes next: product-led signups, partners or outbound.

This plan is for B2B founders between a working product and a first sales hire. It goes line by line through the one-page plan with a worked example, compares the four GTM motions in one table, and splits the first 90 days into three stages, each with a test that says whether to move on.

## What a startup GTM strategy has to decide

A GTM strategy is six decisions, and each one should fit on a line. If a line needs a paragraph, the decision isn't made yet.

1. **Buyer.** The company, and the person inside it who feels the problem.
2. **Trigger.** The event that makes the problem urgent this quarter.
3. **Promise.** The result you deliver, in words the buyer would use.
4. **Motion.** How the first deals happen: founder-led, product-led, partner-led or outbound.
5. **Channels.** The two places the first conversations come from.
6. **Price and proof.** What you charge, and the number that shows the plan works.

The brand, the launch date and the website all follow from these six, so they come later.

### A GTM strategy example, filled in

Say you're building inventory forecasting software for brands that sell on Shopify. After a month of calls, the page reads like this:

- **Buyer:** the head of operations at a US direct-to-consumer brand on Shopify with $5M to $50M in yearly sales and 20 to 150 staff.
- **Trigger:** the brand adds a second warehouse or a wholesale account, and the stock spreadsheet stops matching demand.
- **Promise:** fewer stockouts on the products that bring in most of the revenue, without hiring a planner.
- **Motion:** founder-led for the first ten customers, then a Shopify App Store listing.
- **Channels:** messages to brands hiring a demand planner, and introductions from two Shopify agencies.
- **Price and proof:** $900 a month on annual terms; ten paying brands and two agency partners within 90 days.

The page earns its keep by what it rules out: brands below $5M that can't pay $900 a month, sellers who only use Amazon, enterprise retailers with procurement teams, and paid ads until a message has earned replies.

## Write the ICP as a buyer and a moment

An ideal customer profile (ICP) for a startup names four things: the company, the person, the moment, and the companies you will turn away. The first two say who could buy. The last two save you the most time.

- **The company.** Industry, size, business model, region and the tools it runs. Use filters you can check in public data, so the profile turns into a list.
- **The person.** A title and what that person owns. If two titles own the problem at different company sizes, pick one for now.
- **The moment.** The trigger that makes the problem urgent. It becomes the list of signals you watch and the first line of your messages.
- **The exclusions.** A short "not for us" list. Every company on it is a sales call you won't waste.

To pick the first segment, apply three tests. You can name 100 companies in it. Its buyers feel the problem this quarter, not someday. And one person there can say yes without a committee. If a segment also matches your own background, because you worked in it or speak its jargon, start there.

The [first-customers playbook for B2B SaaS](/blog/how-to-get-first-customers-b2b-saas) narrows the ICP further, to the profile of your first ten customers.

## Choose a GTM motion: founder-led, product-led, partner-led or outbound

Your price decides which motions you can afford, and your buyer decides which ones work. Christoph Janz of Point Nine Capital laid out the arithmetic in [Five ways to build a $100 million business](https://christophjanz.blogspot.com/2014/10/five-ways-to-build-100-million-business.html): reaching $100 million a year takes about 1,000 customers paying $100k or more each, 10,000 paying $10k or more, or 100,000 paying $1k or more. At around $1k a year, he writes, cold calling usually doesn't work. At $10k, an inside sales team can close the deals, though traditional field sales usually still doesn't pay.

| Motion | How first customers arrive | Fits | Needs before it works | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Founder-led | The founder finds, calls and closes each deal | Every B2B startup's first 10 to 20 deals | Blocked hours and a list of named buyers | The founder stops selling when the product needs attention |
| Product-led | Users sign up, get value alone, then pay | Lower prices; a product one person can adopt | A signup with no call and a useful first session | Signups that never reach a first result |
| Partner-led | Marketplaces, agencies and platforms bring customers | Products that plug into a platform or need setup work | Customers already, since partners want proof | A listing nobody promotes |
| Outbound | You write to buyers who show a reason to talk | Deals worth hours of selling; buyers who leave public traces | A narrow ICP and signals to act on | Volume with no reason in the message |

**Founder-led** comes first for almost every B2B startup. The founder can change the product, the price or the terms on the call, and hears every objection firsthand. [The founder-led sales playbook](/blog/founder-led-sales) covers the weekly routine and the signs it's time to hire.

**Product-led** works when one person gets value without a meeting. Measure activation, the share of signups that reach their first useful result, before you celebrate signups. Expect larger accounts to ask for a call anyway, and decide in advance who takes it.

**Partner-led** rewards traction you already have. [HubSpot's App Marketplace](https://developers.hubspot.com/docs/apps/developer-platform/list-apps/listing-your-app/app-marketplace-listing-requirements) lists an app only once it has at least three active installs from HubSpot accounts unaffiliated with your company, as of November 2026. Once you're in, the economics can be kind: Shopify lets app developers [keep all of their first $1 million in lifetime gross app revenue and 85% above that](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/distribution/revenue-share), minus a 2.9% processing fee, also as of November 2026. Agencies that set up your product for their clients turn into a channel after they've seen it work once.

**Outbound** fits when a deal pays for a few hours of selling and buyers leave public traces: job posts, funding rounds, posts about the problem. [Outbound sales strategy](/blog/outbound-sales-strategy) covers targeting, cadence and the numbers to watch, and [warm outreach vs cold outreach](/blog/warm-outreach) shows how a signal makes a stranger's first message read as relevant.

Most startups run two motions: founder-led plus one other. Choose the second from how your first five deals closed, not from a famous company's story.

## Pick two channels for the first quarter

Two channels worked steadily for a quarter teach you more than six channels tried for two weeks each. The motion narrows the choice:

- **Founder-led and outbound:** your own network and your investors' portfolios, then messages to companies showing a signal. [Buying signals in B2B](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) lists the signals worth watching and the first message each one allows.
- **Product-led:** a launch on a platform your users read, integrations and templates people find inside tools they already use, and help pages that teach the job your product does.
- **Partner-led:** one marketplace listing, and two or three agencies whose clients match your ICP.

Leave three channels for later. Paid ads need a message that has already earned replies. Content and search compound for years but start too slowly to land the first ten customers. Trade shows cost the most per conversation until you know exactly whom to meet. For a wider view, the [ranked guide to finding B2B clients](/blog/how-to-find-b2b-clients) compares ten channels.

An AI agent such as Claude or ChatGPT can draft this one-pager from your website and your call notes in a few minutes. Running the channels takes more than a draft: data to find the people, an account to write from, and a memory of who said what. [What AI agents can run in GTM today](/blog/ai-gtm) covers that split.

## Pricing basics for a first GTM plan

Set a price before the first sales call, tie it to a unit the buyer already counts, and check that it pays for the motion you chose.

1. **Pick the unit.** Seats, locations, orders, sales volume or usage. Choose one that grows as the customer gets more value. The forecasting tool above prices by yearly sales band, which rises as the brand grows.
2. **Make sure the price pays for the motion.** Add up the hours one deal takes, from first call to onboarding, and put a cost on them. If the first year's revenue can't cover that cost with room to spare, change the price or the motion.
3. **Publish or quote, but keep a list price.** Product-led companies publish. Sales-led ones can quote, but a written list price is what gives a discount its meaning.
4. **Raise it on evidence.** Log the stated reason for every lost deal. If price never appears after ten proposals, quote the next five higher.

Later, unit economics take over. David Skok's [SaaS Metrics 2.0](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/saas-metrics-2/) offers two guidelines: a customer's lifetime value above three times what it cost to acquire them, and that cost earned back within about 12 months. He calls them guidelines and notes that many healthy SaaS businesses miss them early on.

## The first 90 days of a startup GTM plan

Run the first 90 days as three 30-day stages. Each one ends with a decision, not a report.

**Days 1 to 30: prove the problem.** Write the one-pager and list 100 named companies that fit it. Start 50 conversations, warm contacts first, then strangers who show a signal, and hold 15 discovery calls. You're done when at least five buyers describe the problem in their own words before seeing a demo, and you can name the trigger they share.

**Days 31 to 60: prove someone pays.** Ask for money: annual contracts at your list price, or paid pilots with a price and an end date. Tag every deal, won or lost, with the channel and the signal that started it. You're done with three paying customers and one channel that produced most of your calls.

**Days 61 to 90: prove it repeats.** Put twice the hours into the channel that worked and drop the weakest. Write down how the last five deals closed: the steps, the objections, the days each one took. Pick your second motion from that record. You're done when next quarter's plan has one weekly number per channel.

When a stage fails, change one line of the one-pager, usually the buyer or the trigger, and run that stage again. Adding channels rarely fixes a buyer problem.

## GTM metrics for startups, stage by stage

Before product-market fit, count conversations and conversions. After it, count costs and retention.

- **Before deals repeat:** conversations started, replies by signal and by channel, discovery calls held, calls that turned into paid deals, days from first message to signature, and loss reasons in the buyer's words.
- **Product-led:** activation, and the share of active accounts that pay.
- **From about ten customers:** customer acquisition cost, the months it takes to earn it back, logo retention, and net revenue retention, meaning what a group of customers pays you a year later with expansion added and churn taken out.

One check before you spend on scale: once a few dozen people use the product weekly, ask them how they would feel if they could no longer use it. Sean Ellis found that companies with strong traction almost always had at least 40% answer "very disappointed," and companies that struggled almost always fell short of it, as Superhuman's founder Rahul Vohra recounts in [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/).
