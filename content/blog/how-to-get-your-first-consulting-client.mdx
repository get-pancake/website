**TL;DR:** Look for your first consulting client among the people who have already watched you work: the employer you are leaving, former bosses at other companies, peers who now own a budget. Offer them one narrow problem, solved in a paid pilot of two to six weeks, at a fixed price built from a floor you calculate before the first call. That is how to get your first consulting client without a website, a brand or a pipeline, and the pilot becomes the case study that brings the second.

The steps below run from the weeks before you give notice to the second engagement. [Our ranked guide to getting consulting clients](/blog/how-to-get-consulting-clients) covers the channels you add once the first project is signed, and [Pancake for consultants](/for/consultants) shows how to find buyers beyond the people you know.

## Before you give notice: read three clauses

Your employment agreement decides who you may approach in year one. Read it before you tell anyone your plans.

1. **Non-compete.** The FTC's nationwide ban never took effect: as of November 2026, its own page says [the Noncompete Rule is not in effect and not enforceable](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/noncompete-rule). State law and your contract decide what applies to you.
2. **Non-solicitation.** Many agreements bar you from pursuing the company's clients, or hiring its staff, for a period after you leave. For a new consultant it can matter more than the non-compete, because it decides who may be on your first list.
3. **Confidentiality and intellectual property.** Templates, client data and frameworks you built on the job may belong to the employer. Rebuild your tools from scratch.

An hour with an employment lawyer costs less than a dispute with your first potential client.

## Step 1: Narrow the offer until someone else can repeat it

A first client hires you for one problem. So write the offer as one sentence with four parts: the buyer, the problem, the result and the time frame.

Say you spent eight years running customer onboarding at software companies. "I help companies improve customer experience" gives no one a reason to think of you. "I cut onboarding time for B2B software companies with 50 to 300 people, in a six-week project" does. A former colleague can repeat it to a friend at dinner, and that is how a referral travels.

Narrow does not mean small. It means a buyer recognizes the problem in the first line and knows whether it is theirs.

---

## Step 2: List the 40 people who have watched you work

The fastest way to start getting consulting clients is a list you already have. Build it in a spreadsheet, with one row per person:

- former bosses, and their bosses;
- peers who moved to other companies;
- people you managed who now run teams;
- vendors and partners you worked with closely;
- clients of your former employer, only if your agreement allows it.

Score each person on two questions: do they own a budget for your problem today, and would they take your call? Start with the people who score yes on both.

Write to each one alone, and ask for their view before you ask for work: "I've gone independent to fix slow customer onboarding at software companies. Is that a problem you see at your company, or at others you know?" People answer a question about their world. They ignore an announcement.

---

## Step 3: Offer a paid pilot instead of a retainer

A pilot lowers the risk of hiring someone new. Make it small enough to approve fast and complete enough to prove the point:

- **one problem**, with a deliverable you can name ("a redesigned onboarding plan and the first three accounts run through it");
- **two to six weeks**, with a decision date at the end;
- **one measure** agreed up front, such as days from signed contract to first use;
- **a fixed fee**, paid partly before you start.

Never run the pilot for free. A free pilot tells the buyer the work is worth nothing, and it gives them no reason to make time for you.

---

## Step 4: Price the first deal from a floor

Work out the lowest day rate you can live on before the first call, so you never invent a price under pressure.

Say you need $160,000 a year in revenue to cover your pay, taxes, insurance and tools, and you expect to bill 80 days in year one. Selling, admin and gaps between projects eat the rest. Your floor is $2,000 a day. A six-week pilot that takes 12 days of work is then at least $24,000, plus a margin for the unknowns.

Quote the pilot as a fixed fee, not as days, and ask for half up front. If the buyer pushes back, cut scope instead of the rate. Your first price becomes the reference for every project after it, including the referrals it sends.

---

## Step 5: Have the paperwork ready before they say yes

Expect a client who says yes on a call to ask for documents the same week. Have them drafted:

- **A one-page proposal**: the problem in their words, the deliverable, the measure, the timeline, the fee and payment terms.
- **A short consulting agreement or statement of work** covering scope, payment, confidentiality and who owns the work product. Have a lawyer review your template once.
- **Form W-9.** Clients use it to collect your taxpayer identification number for [the information returns they file on what they pay you](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-9).
- **An EIN.** The IRS issues one [online, in minutes, for free](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number). As a sole proprietor you may enter it on the W-9 instead of your Social Security number.
- **An invoice template** with your payment terms and how to pay you.

If the client is your former employer, scope a project, not your old job. The IRS looks at who controls how the work is done and whether the relationship looks continuing, per [its guidance on contractors and employees](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee).

---

## Step 6: Make client one produce client two

The first engagement is also your first sales asset. Measure the result you agreed on, and write it up in three parts: the problem, what you changed, what moved.

Ask for two things when the result shows, not at the end of the contract. First, permission to describe the work, named or anonymous. Second, a referral: "Who else do you know who is fighting slow onboarding?" Then offer the next phase before the pilot closes, such as rolling the new plan out to the next segment of customers.

## How to get your first consulting client if the list comes up empty

Forty people may produce only a few conversations, and the people you know may not have the problem this quarter. The next source is strangers who show the problem in public: a company posting a job for the role you would otherwise fill, a leader posting about the problem you fix, a startup that recently raised. [Lead generation for consultants](/blog/lead-generation-for-consultants) turns those signals into a three-hour weekly routine, and [the solopreneur's guide to getting clients](/blog/how-to-get-clients-as-a-solopreneur) covers how to keep selling while you deliver.
