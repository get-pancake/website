**TL;DR:** Six steps take a web design lead from first reply to signed contract: qualify in writing, run a 30-minute discovery call, send a one-page scope recap, price a proposal with three options, follow up on a fixed schedule, and close with a contract and a deposit. Speed matters most right after the call, so send the proposal within two business days.

This guide to how to sell web design services starts where lead generation ends: the first reply. To find the people to talk to, start with the ranked channels in [how to get web design clients](/blog/how-to-get-web-design-clients).

## How to sell web design services, stage by stage

The web design sales process runs through six stages plus a "not now" pile, and every open deal sits in one. The stage tells you the next action. Target times are goals, not industry data.

| Stage | What you do | Move on when | Target time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Reply or inquiry | Answer the same day; ask three qualifying questions | They share the reason, a budget range and who decides | 1 to 3 days |
| 2. Qualified | Book a 30-minute discovery call | The call is on the calendar | Within a week |
| 3. Discovery done | Send a one-page scope recap | They confirm or correct it | 1 business day |
| 4. Proposal sent | Three options, a start date, a deposit | They pick an option or ask questions | 1 to 2 weeks |
| 5. Negotiation | Adjust the scope, not your rate | The contract is ready to sign | A few days |
| 6. Won | Contract signed, deposit cleared, kickoff booked | Work starts | Same week |
| Lost or not now | Log the reason and set a reminder | They come back, or you check in | 3 to 6 months |

## Step 1: Qualify the lead before you book a call

Ask three questions in writing before you offer a call: why now, what budget range, and who decides. The answers sort a lead in one reply.

> "Thanks for getting in touch. So I can come prepared: what's prompting a new site now, what budget range are you working with, and who else will weigh in on the decision?"

Some buyers won't name a budget. Give yours instead. Say your builds run $8,000 to $25,000: write "Our sites usually land between $8,000 and $25,000 depending on scope. Is that in the range you had in mind?" A yes moves them to stage 2. A no saves you both a call.

A reply to your outreach is interest, not intent. Ask the same three questions, more gently, before you book anything.

---

## Step 2: Run the web design discovery call

A discovery call is 30 minutes in which the buyer does most of the talking. You leave knowing the goal, the constraints and how the decision gets made.

Use a fixed agenda: why they reached out, the questions below, a playback of what you heard, and the date your proposal arrives.

**The goal**

1. What has to be true six months after launch for this project to count as a success?
2. Why now? What happens if the site stays as it is for another year?
3. Who are the most important visitors, and what should they do on the site?

**The current site**

4. What works today that we must not break?
5. Which pages do you avoid sending people to?
6. Who updates the site now, and how often?

**The scope**

7. Who writes the copy, and is any of it ready?
8. Which tools must the site connect to, such as your CRM, forms, booking or payments?
9. Are there brand guidelines, or is that part of the work?

**The decision**

10. Who else is involved, and who signs?
11. What budget has been set aside?
12. Is there a hard launch date, and what's behind it?

Question 6 often decides the platform. A marketing team that edits pages weekly needs a CMS it can use without you.

## Red flags to catch on the call

Bad projects often show warning signs in the first conversation. Listen for these:

- A deadline with no reason behind it, or two weeks for a 20-page site.
- No budget range, even after you've shared yours.
- Three decision makers and no single owner.
- Copy that "will be ready soon."
- A request for free designs before they decide.
- Every past designer described as the problem.
- Scope that grows while you talk.

For each one, pick a response: name the risk on the call, price it into the proposal (a copywriting line item, a rush fee), or pass politely.

## Step 3: Send a one-page scope recap

Within one business day, send a one-page recap: the goal in their words, what's in scope, what's out, open questions, and the date the proposal will arrive. End it with one question: "Did I get this right?" Corrections are cheap at this stage. A misunderstanding written into a signed contract is not.

---

## Step 4: Write a proposal with three options

Price the project as three options built around the same goal. The buyer then weighs your options against each other, not you against another studio.

Structure the proposal in this order:

1. The goal and the problem, in their words.
2. The approach, phase by phase, from discovery to launch.
3. Three options, with what each includes.
4. A timeline with a real start date.
5. The price, the payment schedule and the deposit.
6. What you need from them, and by when.
7. Your terms, attached.
8. How to accept: sign and pay the deposit.

A hypothetical example for a 60-person B2B software company, with placeholder prices:

- **Essentials, $14,000:** an eight-page Webflow site built from their brand guidelines, a CMS for the blog, two rounds of revisions.
- **Recommended, $22,000:** Essentials plus a messaging workshop, copy for the five key pages, a case-study template and analytics setup.
- **Full, $31,000:** Recommended plus an illustration set, three landing pages for launches and three months of care plan.

Make the middle option your recommendation, and describe the differences as outcomes, not page counts. [How to get high-paying web design clients](/blog/how-to-get-high-paying-web-design-clients) covers outcome pricing for larger buyers.

---

## Step 5: Follow up without chasing

Agree on a decision date during the call. Then follow up on a fixed schedule, and make every message add something.

- **Day 2:** check that the proposal arrived and offer a 15-minute walkthrough.
- **Day 5:** send the answer to a question they raised on the call, or a relevant example.
- **Day 10:** offer a smaller scope if the budget looked tight.
- **Day 21:** close the file, politely.

> "I haven't heard back, so I'll assume the timing isn't right and close this out on our side. If anything changes, reply here and we'll pick it up."

---

## Step 6: Close with a contract and a deposit

A project is sold when the contract is signed and the deposit has cleared. Book the kickoff only then.

The contract should cover:

- the scope, by reference to the option they accepted, and what counts as a change request;
- revision rounds, and what happens when feedback or content arrives late;
- the payment schedule, the deposit and late fees;
- who owns the work, and when ownership passes to the client, for example on final payment;
- support after launch, termination with a kill fee, and your right to show the work.

You don't have to draft this from nothing. The [AIGA Standard Form of Agreement for Design Services](https://www.aiga.org/resources/aiga-standard-form-of-agreement-for-design-services), updated in 2020, is a set of modular terms to attach to your own proposal: basic terms and intellectual property provisions for every project, plus an Interactive module for web work. Have a lawyer review your version.

Some places write the basics into law. [New York City's Freelance Isn't Free Act](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/workers/workersrights/freelancer-workers.page) requires a written contract for freelance work worth $800 or more, counting every agreement with the same client over 120 days. The contract must state the work, the pay and the payment date. Without a date, the client has to pay within 30 days of the work being completed.

Size the deposit to your risk: enough to cover your costs to the first milestone. On the $22,000 option above, that could be $8,800 up front, $6,600 at design sign-off and $6,600 at launch.

## How to market web design services so the pipeline stays full

The process above only works when enough conversations enter stage 1. A sales funnel for a web design agency has four layers: people who see your work, people who start a conversation, qualified leads, and proposals.

Count how many move from each layer to the next every month, and fix the weakest one first. Plenty of calls but few signed proposals points to steps 2 to 5. Few calls points to the top of the funnel, which [lead generation for web design agencies](/blog/lead-generation-for-web-design-agencies) covers.
