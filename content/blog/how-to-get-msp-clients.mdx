**TL;DR:** Referrals sign first. Your current clients, the accountants who advise the same offices and the software vendors you already support can each hand you a client this quarter. That is how to get MSP clients among small local offices, which rarely post jobs or announce anything. Companies with 20 to 500 staff leave public traces instead: they advertise IT roles and name Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace in job posts, so outreach timed to those posts works there.

Below are the 10 sources MSPs use, ranked, with a table to compare them and a five-step plan for a first client. The weekly system that keeps leads coming, from signals to watch to partners to recruit, is in [lead generation for MSPs](/blog/lead-generation-for-msps).

## An 8-person dental office and a 120-person freight broker buy IT differently

The buyer decides which sources work, so start with yours.

**Small local offices.** A dental practice, law firm or accounting office with 5 to 20 people has no IT staff. When the server dies or the part-time IT person retires, the office manager asks people the practice already trusts: the accountant, the software vendor, the landlord, the dentist next door. Then they search for IT support in town. These offices rarely post IT jobs or write about their network, so there is nothing public to watch. Referrals and local visibility win.

**Companies with 20 to 500 staff.** A freight broker, a manufacturer or a growing software firm has an operations lead or a controller who owns IT, sometimes with one overworked technician. When the load outgrows them, they post a job: help desk analyst, IT manager, systems administrator. The post often names the tools, such as Microsoft 365, Intune or Google Workspace, and that trace lets you reach the right person the week the need appears.

Most MSPs serve both. Rank the sources for the buyer behind most of your monthly recurring revenue.

## 1 and 2: the people who already vouch for you

### 1. Current clients and the peers they know

Your clients grow, open second sites and hire, and every new seat is revenue you don't have to win from scratch. Ask about headcount plans in each quarterly business review, and offer the services they need next: tested backups, security awareness training, a licensing review, phones.

Referrals come from the same meeting. Ask while the client is looking at the page of tickets you closed. Make the question about their world: "Which other practice in your study club keeps complaining about IT?" or "Who else in this building still calls a nephew when the printer breaks?" A specific question gets a name. "Know anyone who needs IT?" gets a shrug.

Thank each referral in a way that fits your market, such as a credit on next month's invoice. Before your next round of reviews, mark the three clients who like you most and put the question on their agendas.

### 2. Accountants and tax preparers

An accounting firm advises dozens of the offices you want, and its clients ask it who handles their IT. It also carries an IT duty of its own. The [FTC Safeguards Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftc-safeguards-rule-what-your-business-needs-know) counts tax preparation firms as financial institutions and requires multi-factor authentication for anyone accessing customer information, plus encryption of that information on the firm's systems and in transit. The rule's Qualified Individual, who runs the security program, can work for a service provider if a senior employee at the firm supervises them.

The IRS keeps reminding the profession. Its [August 2026 news release](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-security-summit-remind-tax-pros-they-need-a-written-information-security-plan-to-protect-client-data) says federal law requires tax and accounting professionals to create and maintain a Written Information Security Plan, and points smaller practices to its Publication 5708 template.

So lead with the firm's own plan: review its written plan and its MFA setup, become its IT provider, and referrals follow from a partner who has watched your work. Stay away during tax season.

## 3 to 5: the sources that hear about IT trouble first

### 3. Software vendors your clients already run

Every niche runs on a few line-of-business apps: practice management for dentists and vets, case management for law firms, estimating and ERP software for contractors. When that software misbehaves, the vendor's support team often traces the cause to the client's network and needs someone local to fix it.

List the five apps your clients use most and find out how each vendor handles IT referrals. Some keep a list of IT providers who know the product, some run training or certification, and resellers who install the software need a local partner for hardware and networks. Become the firm a vendor's support desk trusts, and its other customers nearby start hearing your name.

Copier dealers and business phone vendors work the same way: they install into the same offices and get blamed for network problems that aren't theirs.

### 4. Microsoft's partner directory and referrals

If you resell or manage Microsoft 365, set this up early. As a Microsoft partner, you can create a [business profile in Partner Center](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/customers/create-a-marketing-profile) that appears in the Connect with partners directory on Microsoft Marketplace, and Microsoft's sales and service staff can use it to refer customers to you. You can add up to five locations per country or region, which surface when buyers search by proximity, and opt out of referrals from businesses outside your target size. Microsoft says a fast answer to a referral can improve your placement for future customers.

A designation strengthens the profile. The [Solutions Partner for Modern Work](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/membership/solutions-partner-modern-work) requires at least 70 of 100 points on Microsoft's partner capability score, with points in each of five metrics, as of November 2026. Its small and medium business path counts customer tenants with 11 to 300 paid licenses in a workload, which matches a typical MSP book.

Treat the directory as a credibility layer more than a pipeline: it helps a buyer who found you elsewhere say yes.

### 5. Landlords, brokers and fit-out contractors

An office move is a deadline with IT attached: an internet circuit to order, cabling, Wi-Fi, phones, a network closet to relocate and one weekend to do it in. The property manager, the tenant's broker and the fit-out contractors hear about it first.

Give them something to hand over. A one-page move checklist with your name on it ("order the circuit first, map the closet, test the phones before Monday") makes their job easier and reaches the tenant at the right moment. Property managers also field complaints about building Wi-Fi, and a dependable IT contact makes their building look better.

Watch for moves yourself too: people post about a new office or a second location, and local business journals report signed leases.

## 6: outreach to companies that post their IT needs

For companies with 20 to 500 staff, a public signal tells you who needs help now. Four are worth watching:

- **An IT job post.** Help desk analyst, IT support technician, IT manager, systems administrator: the company has outgrown whoever handles IT today.
- **Tools named in job posts.** Microsoft 365, Intune, Entra ID or Google Workspace in a job ad shows the stack you would manage. [Finding companies by the tools in their job posts](/plays/tech-stack) turns that into a search.
- **Posts about trouble or change.** An operations lead writing about an outage, a phishing scare, a cyber insurance renewal or a new office.
- **A funding round.** New hires, new laptops and onboarding that someone has to run.

Write to the person who owns IT without the title: the COO, the controller, the office manager, the practice administrator. The recruiter who posted the job is the wrong door. [Searching for companies hiring for a role](/plays/companies-hiring) shows how to get from the post to that person.

A company hiring its first IT manager is not a lost deal. That person will need after-hours cover, project help and someone to call while on vacation: co-managed IT. Ask about the post before you offer anything: "Saw the IT manager opening. Will that person run everything, or will after-hours support and projects stay with an outside team?"

One trap: MSPs post help desk jobs too. A rival hiring technicians is not a prospect.

## 7 to 9: local visibility and local rooms

### 7. Google Business Profile and local search

Small offices search for help in their own town, so be findable there. Claim your Google Business Profile, choose the category that matches managed IT, list the towns you serve, and ask each new client for a review once onboarding is done and the first month has gone quietly. Then give each town and each industry you serve its own page on your site, with a real client story from that place.

This source is slow, and it compounds. Reviews you collect this quarter keep working long after you asked for them.

### 8. Other MSPs and solo technicians

The MSP down the road is a competitor and a source. A firm that only does Microsoft 365 needs someone for phones and networks. A solo technician needs cover when they're sick or away. A shop that won't touch healthcare will pass dental practices to someone who will. Agree in writing on what each side takes, and on how a client returns if it came from the other.

Some owners near retirement sell their client agreements to a nearby MSP instead of closing. Tell peers and your distributor you would talk, and judge any deal on the agreements that renew, not on the client count.

### 9. Workshops, chambers and referral groups

A 45-minute session on phishing and multi-factor authentication, run for a chamber of commerce, a trade association or an accounting firm's clients, puts you in front of owners as the person who explained it plainly. Bring one handout with five actions and your logo, and skip the pitch.

A local referral group, where each member brings leads for the others, suits an IT provider: accountants, insurance agents and commercial brokers all meet offices with IT problems. Pick one group and attend for six months before you judge it.

## 10: cold calls and appointment setters

Cold calling still fills calendars for some MSPs, mostly those selling to offices that leave no public trace. It ranks last because each meeting costs the most hours, and some offices take the meeting only to get a lower quote out of their current provider.

If you call yourself, work a narrow list (one industry, one area) with one reason to call, such as the written security plan the IRS expects from every tax preparer. If you hire an appointment-setting agency, listen to recorded calls first, ask what happens when a booked meeting doesn't show, and pay for meetings with decision makers rather than for dials. Before you hire a salesperson for it, compare [what a first BDR costs against a tool](/blog/pancake-vs-hiring-a-bdr).

## How to get your first MSP client

A new MSP has no references, so the first client comes from people who have already watched you work.

1. **List everyone who has seen you fix something.** Former employers, break-fix customers, the office manager at your last job, friends who run businesses. Write to each one personally.
2. **Pick one package for one office size.** For example: help desk, patching, backups and Microsoft 365 security for offices with 10 to 50 users, at one flat price per user. One offer is easier to refer than a menu.
3. **Ask your own advisors.** Your accountant, lawyer and insurance agent each know a dozen owners. Ask each for one introduction to an office that complains about IT.
4. **Open with a paid onboarding project.** Document the network, turn on MFA everywhere, test a restore. It proves your work before the monthly agreement starts and pays for the onboarding hours.
5. **Turn client one into a reference.** Ask for a review and permission to name them, then tell that story on every call that follows.
