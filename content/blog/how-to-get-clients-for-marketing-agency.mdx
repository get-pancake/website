**TL;DR:** Start with the people who already know your work: current clients with a new budget, former client contacts who moved to new companies, and partners who sell the service next to yours. Then go where demand shows in public: companies hiring a marketing manager or a head of growth, startups that just raised, founders asking for agency picks. Partner badges, review profiles and content make buyers trust you once they find you. They rarely start the conversation.

How to get clients for a marketing agency depends on which agency you run, so this playbook starts with the buyer, sets a target with simple retainer math, then ranks 10 channels in one table and gives each a first move. It's written for owners of digital, creative and ad agencies with 5 to 50 people, where the founder still runs new business.

---

## Digital, creative and ad agencies sell to different buyers

The channel that fills a paid search agency's calendar can leave a brand studio idle. Start with who signs your contracts and what they need to see.

- **Digital and performance agencies** (paid search, paid social, SEO, lifecycle email). The buyer is a head of growth, a demand-gen lead or an e-commerce manager. They buy on numbers: cost per lead, pipeline, return on ad spend. Expect a test on one channel before they hand over more.
- **Creative and brand agencies.** The buyer is a CMO, a brand lead or a founder. They buy on taste and proof: work they can see, made for companies like theirs. Projects cluster around a rebrand, a launch or a funding round, so referrals and outreach timed to those moments do the most.
- **Ad and media agencies.** The buyer holds a media budget and wants someone to protect it. Credentials weigh more here than anywhere: partner status, case studies with spend levels, references a buyer can call.
- **Influencer and creator agencies** sell to brand and growth leads and watch signals of their own: see [how to get clients for an influencer marketing agency](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-influencer-marketing-agency).

One more split runs across all four. Agencies that serve local businesses, such as dental groups, roofers or restaurants, grow through referrals, local events and a Google Business Profile, because those owners rarely post jobs or announce plans. Agencies that serve B2B companies can see demand coming: those companies hire, raise, launch and post in public. If your agency builds websites as well, the [web design client playbook](/blog/how-to-get-web-design-clients) covers that side of the business.

## Count the clients you need before you pick channels

Retainers end, so part of your new business only replaces clients you lose. Do this math once and you know how much each channel has to carry.

Say a 14-person agency has 16 retainer clients, and its retainers last 20 months on average (example numbers; use your own). About 10 retainers end in a year (16 × 12 ÷ 20 ≈ 9.6). Growing to 20 clients takes about 14 new ones a year, more than one a month.

Work backward. If you win one pitch in three, 14 clients means 42 pitches. If one first conversation in four becomes a pitch, that's 168 conversations a year, about 14 a month. Then count how many conversations your referrals and partners produced last quarter. The gap is the job of your outbound and inbound channels.

## 1. Current clients and client alumni

The cheapest new client already knows you. Current clients have budget you haven't touched: a second region, a new product line, a channel someone else runs for them. Former client contacts are the other half. When a marketing lead you worked well with moves to a new company, they arrive with budgets to set and partners to choose.

Two habits turn this into a channel:

- **A quarterly growth review with each client.** Show results, then ask about next quarter's targets and what stands in the way. Expansion work starts with that last answer.
- **An alumni list.** Every client contact from the last five years, with where they work now. Check it each quarter, since nobody writes to their old agency to announce a new job.

Ask for referrals by describing one person. "Who else in your investor's portfolio is about to hire their first marketer?" is a question a client can answer on the spot. The scripts and fee options in this [referral program guide](/blog/web-design-referral-program) were written for web studios, and they carry over to any agency.

**This week:** build the alumni list. Start with the 20 contacts you liked working with most, find where each works today, and write to the three who changed companies most recently.

## 2. Partner agencies and consultants

Agencies that sell the service next to yours meet your future clients first. A web studio that relaunches a site leaves a client who now needs traffic. A PR firm that lands coverage leaves a client asking how to turn it into pipeline. Fractional CMOs belong at the top of the list: choosing agencies is part of the job, for every client they take on.

Make each partnership specific:

1. Pick three partners whose clients look like yours and who don't sell what you sell.
2. Write down the swap: "We send you every client who needs a new site. You send us every client who needs paid search."
3. Agree on the reward: a referral fee, a share of the first invoice, or intros both ways with no cash.
4. Review it each quarter. A partner who has sent nothing in two quarters is a contact.

Agencies that sell your service at another price point won't refer much. They see you as a rival, and they're right.

## 3. Outreach timed to a signal

Outbound works when you write for a reason the buyer would recognize. Four reasons point to agency work this quarter:

- **A marketing job post.** A company hiring a marketing manager, a head of growth or a demand-gen lead has budget and a gap. Some want an agency to run the work until the hire starts; others want support for the new hire. [Finding companies by the roles they post](/plays/companies-hiring) shows the search side.
- **A post about a launch, a missed pipeline target or a rebrand.** Each one names a problem with a date attached.
- **Comments on a rival agency's posts.** A marketer who weighs in on a competitor's teardown of paid social costs is thinking about paid social costs.
- **A funding round.** New money arrives with a growth target. [Selling to recently funded startups](/plays/recently-funded-startups) covers when to write.

The first message names the reason, asks one question and pitches nothing: "Saw the head of growth opening. Will that person build paid channels from zero, or take over what an agency runs today?" [Marketing agency cold outreach](/blog/marketing-agency-cold-outreach) has first messages for each signal, by email and by DM, and [lead generation for marketing agencies](/blog/lead-generation-for-marketing-agencies) turns the signals into a repeatable lead flow.

Signals favor agencies that sell to B2B companies. A bakery leaves none of these traces.

## 4. Public requests for agency recommendations

Founders and marketing leads ask for agency picks in public: in social posts, in founder Slack groups, in subreddits such as r/startups and r/SaaS. These requests are the warmest leads that arrive without a referral, and they go stale fast, because other agencies read the same threads.

Three rules for answering one:

- Answer the question asked. If they want "a B2B paid social agency under $10,000 a month," say whether you fit and give one line of proof.
- Reply in the thread or in a short direct message. A pasted credentials deck ends it.
- If you're not a fit, name someone who is. The person asking remembers who helped, and so does the agency you sent their way.

Catch these posts early by searching for phrases such as "looking for an agency" and "agency recommendations" where your buyers post. Pancake can watch for those phrases too: see [how Pancake finds these posts for marketing agencies](/for/marketing-agencies).

## 5. Platform partner programs

If you run paid media or a CRM practice, the platforms you buy from can vouch for you. Two programs are worth knowing in detail, and one changed this year. Terms as of November 2026:

**Google Partners.** The [Google Partners requirements](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/9702452) ask for a 70% optimization score on your registered manager account, $10,000 in ad spend across managed accounts over 90 days, and Google Ads certifications for at least half of your account strategists, plus one certification in each product area where you spend $500 or more in 90 days. Google checks them daily. Premier Partner status goes to the top 3% of participating companies in each country, decided once a year.

**HubSpot Solutions Partner Program.** Since July 15, 2026, entry takes a [partner program membership of $400 a month](https://www.hubspot.com/solutions-partners-tiers-and-benefits-2026), waived when your own HubSpot subscription reaches $400 a month after discounts. Above the entry tier sit Gold, Platinum, Diamond and Elite, earned with points for the customers you source, assist and manage; Gold takes 115 sourced points and 345 in total. Gold partners and above qualify for HubSpot's partner matching program, which pairs HubSpot customers with partners. Partners can also list in the Solutions Directory with their industries, locations, certifications and client reviews.

A badge tells a buyer you can be trusted with their spend or their CRM. It seldom starts a conversation alone, so pair it with reviews and case studies.

## 6 to 10: slower channels that make the others convert

These five rarely start a conversation in the first month. Buyers check them before they reply to anything you send.

**6. Your own content and posts.** Write for buyers, not for other marketers: a teardown of a launch in your clients' industry, the three tests you'd run with a $20,000 budget, what you'd cut first when a target slips. People who engage with your agency's posts are leads in their own right, so keep track of who they are.

**7. Review directories.** On Clutch, buyers compare agencies through client reviews that Clutch's team verifies before publishing. [Listing on Clutch is free](https://help.clutch.co/knowledge/free-features-clutch-offers), and premium upgrades buy more visibility. An empty profile does nothing: ask three happy clients for a review this quarter.

**8. Buyer-side events.** Skip conferences where every booth belongs to an agency. Go where your niche's buyers go: the trade show of the industry you serve, an investor's portfolio day, a founder dinner. Book two meetings before you arrive, not after.

**9. Awards and pitch lists.** For creative and ad agencies, an effectiveness award such as an Effie, or a credit on well-known work, gets you onto pitch lists. Both are slow and costly: entry fees, then pitches that can eat weeks of unpaid work. Set your rule on free pitch work before the first request arrives.

**10. Freelance marketplaces.** Upwork and Contra can land a young agency its first paid projects and reviews. Buyers there compare prices side by side, so use them to earn proof, then move the hours to channels 1 to 3.

## Open with a paid first project, not a free audit

Whatever the channel, the first offer decides how many conversations turn into clients. A free audit hands your best thinking to someone who can take it to their current agency. A small paid project gets you inside the account, with a fee, a deadline and a result to show.

Good first projects have a fixed scope and price, and they end with a decision:

- a four-week paid social sprint on one product line;
- a positioning workshop before a rebrand;
- a tracking cleanup that ends with a dashboard the client keeps.

Price it so the marketing lead can approve it without a committee. Then write the retainer proposal before the project ends, while the results are fresh.
