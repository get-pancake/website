**TL;DR:** Software companies buy UI/UX work at four moments: right after a funding round, while a designer seat sits empty, when onboarding numbers slip, and before a redesign. Land the first clients through people who watched you ship and development shops that need a design partner. Then write to product leaders who show one of those four moments in public. Dribbble, case studies and Figma's partner program make buyers trust you, but they seldom start the conversation.

Below is how to get UI/UX clients when you design products: apps, dashboards, onboarding flows, design systems. If your clients mostly want marketing websites, the [playbook for getting web design clients](/blog/how-to-get-web-design-clients) fits better, because the buyer and the channels change.

---

## The buyer is a product leader, not a marketer

UX work gets bought by whoever owns the product: a founder, a CTO, a VP of Product or a head of design. That decides where you meet them and what proof they want.

- **Seed and Series A startups, 5 to 50 people.** The founder or CTO buys, often with no designer on staff, and decides in one or two calls.
- **Scale-ups with a design team, 50 to 500 people.** A head of design or VP of Product buys what the team can't cover: user research, a design system, one painful flow such as onboarding or billing. They compare you with a contractor and with a new hire.
- **Large companies.** Procurement and security reviews slow everything down. For a small studio, the way in is often a bigger agency that already holds the contract.

The craft splits the same way. UI work, such as a visual refresh before a launch, sells fastest to founders. Research-led UX sells to teams that already measure their product: they can see where users drop, and they pay you to find out why. The [Pancake page for UX design agencies](/for/ux-design-agencies) shows what those teams post and hire for.

## How to get UI/UX clients: 9 channels side by side

Ranked by how soon each channel tends to produce a first call for a studio of one to ten people, then by deal size. The timings are our judgment, not survey data.

| # | Channel | Who you reach | First conversation | Cost | Fits best |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | People who watched you ship | Former PMs, engineers and founders | Days | Free | Anyone with shipped products |
| 2 | Development shops and product agencies | Teams that build but can't design | 2 to 8 weeks | A margin or a referral fee | Studios willing to work under another brand |
| 3 | Product leaders showing a signal | Founders and VPs of Product with a dated need | 1 to 4 weeks | Your time, or a tool | Studios selling to software companies |
| 4 | Case studies with product numbers | Buyers checking you before a call | Months | Your time | Studios with three shipped projects |
| 5 | Investors and accelerators | Founders in a portfolio or a cohort | 1 to 3 months | Workshop time | Studios that sell to startups |
| 6 | Dribbble and Behance | Founders browsing visual work | 2 to 6 weeks | Fees on paid work | UI designers and freelancers |
| 7 | Upwork and Contra | Small, fixed briefs | 1 to 4 weeks | Platform fees | Freelancers building a record |
| 8 | Figma's Service Partner Program | Larger teams rolling out Figma | Slow to qualify | Certification work | Agencies and consultancies |
| 9 | Communities and talks | Designers and product managers | Months | Your time | Specialists with a point of view |

Pick three to work at once: one warm (1 or 2), one outbound (3), and one source of proof (4 or 6).

## 1. People who watched you ship

The fastest UX clients have already seen your work land inside a product. Product managers you designed with, engineers who built from your files and founders you shipped for all move companies, and each move lands them in front of a new backlog.

Keep a short "shipped with" list: every PM, engineer, founder and design lead from your last five years of work, with where each one works now. Check it each quarter and write to anyone in a new role about their new product, not about you.

Past clients carry the next layer of work. The MVP you designed needs a design system. The flow you shipped needs a usability test. Ask about a specific flow: "Which screen do your support tickets point at most?" gets a better answer than "Need any design help?"

## 2. Development shops that need a design partner

Development agencies sell product builds, and some have engineers to spare but no senior product designer. When a client asks for design, they hire, improvise or subcontract. Be the subcontract.

Make it easy to say yes:

1. **Price by phase or by day,** so the agency can mark it up inside its own quote.
2. **Hand off the way engineers like:** annotated Figma files, a component list and acceptance criteria for each screen.
3. **Put a no-poaching promise in writing.** The agency's client stays its client.
4. **Start with one small paid project,** so the team sees how you work before it bets a big account on you.

No-code studios and fractional CTOs hit the same gap. Agencies meet founders before designers do, so the [playbook for software development companies](/blog/how-to-get-clients-for-software-development-company) is worth reading from their side.

## 3. Product leaders showing a signal

Outreach to software companies works when it names something the company did in public this month. Four signals point to design work:

- **A product designer, UX researcher or design lead job post.** The team has more design work than people. Some will hire anyway; offer the weeks before the hire starts, or the research the new designer won't have time for. [Job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals) covers which roles predict which purchases.
- **Job posts that name design and product tools.** Treat Figma as a wide net: a post that names it tells you the team designs there, not that it needs help. Narrower names say more. Maze points to a team that tests with users. Pendo or Amplitude points to a team watching activation. [Reading a company's tools from its job posts](/blog/tech-stack-signals) explains the method and its blind spots.
- **A recent funding round.** A round turns a postponed redesign into a budget line. [Finding startups that raised money](/blog/how-to-find-recently-funded-startups) covers the sources and when to write.
- **Posts about onboarding drop-off, activation or a redesign.** Product leaders write about the flow that leaks users, or ask for examples of onboarding they admire. That post is your first line.

The first message asks one question about the signal and pitches nothing: "Saw the senior product designer opening. Is that seat for the new product, or for fixing onboarding in the current one?" Hold the portfolio until they ask for it.

---

## 4. Case studies with a product number

Product leaders hire on outcomes they track: activation, task completion, time to first value, support tickets. "We redesigned onboarding" says little. "Activation rose from 31% to 38% in the six weeks after release" (a made-up example) tells a VP of Product what you'd do for them.

Product work often sits behind an NDA, so plan for it:

- Add a contract clause before the project starts: you may describe the problem and the result, with screens blurred or rebuilt.
- Tell the story without screens when you must: the question, the research, what changed, the number.
- Offer a reference call when you can't show anything.

Three case studies from one kind of product, say B2B dashboards, persuade more than ten unrelated ones.

---

## 5. Investors and accelerators

A fund's portfolio is a list of companies with the same problems at the same stage. Give the whole group something useful: a 45-minute onboarding teardown for an accelerator cohort, or office hours for a fund's portfolio founders.

Then ask the investors behind your past clients who they send founders to for product design, and what it takes to be on that list.

## 6 to 9: platforms and proof that build slowly

**6. Dribbble and Behance.** Both suit UI work, because visual polish shows in a shot and research doesn't. Dribbble also runs Project Briefs, where clients post work for designers to pitch on, and a [full-time job board](https://dribbble.com/jobs) where companies advertise product designer roles. Read that board as a list of teams short on design.

**7. Upwork and Contra.** Marketplaces can land a first paid project and a few reviews. Buyers compare prices side by side, so use them for proof and move your hours elsewhere once you have it.

**8. Figma's Service Partner Program.** As of November 2026, [the program](https://www.figma.com/partners/services/) has two tiers, Select and Signature. Both include certification training, a badge and a dedicated partner manager; only Signature adds customer referrals from Figma and a partner community listing. Figma's [service partner directory](https://www.figma.com/partners/find-a-service-partner/) listed 10 firms when we checked in November 2026, most of them large consultancies, so a five-person studio gets little from it yet. Borrow its service list as offers instead: design system setup, migrations from other tools, rolling Figma out to a team.

**9. Communities and talks.** Friends of Figma groups and design meetups fill up with designers: good for overflow referrals, rarely for buyers. Product management meetups and founder groups hold the people who sign. Talk about one flow you fixed, with the numbers.

## Sell a first project with an end date

A product leader says yes faster to a small, dated project than to a retainer. Three first projects suit a UX studio:

- **A paid review of one flow.** Two weeks, one flow such as onboarding or billing, and a ranked list of fixes with effort estimates.
- **A usability study.** Recruit real users, test the current flow, and show the clips. Teams that track activation see where users drop; you show them why.
- **Bridge capacity.** A senior designer three days a week while the full-time search runs, with a handover document for the new hire.

Skip spec work and free audits. Each one teaches the buyer that your thinking is free.
