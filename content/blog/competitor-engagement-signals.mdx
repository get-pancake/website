**TL;DR:** A comment on a rival's post earns one message when three things are true: the person fits your customer profile, doesn't work with the rival, and said something about their own work. Write about the topic of the post, never about the rival or the person's reaction to it. A question in the comments tells you far more than a like, and no first message should reveal that you were watching.

The tactic sounds simple: find people engaging with competitors, then talk to them. Done carelessly, it means writing to the rival's own staff, to happy customers who forward your message to their account manager, and to strangers who wonder why you're watching them. Competitor engagement is one of the conversation signals in [the full list of B2B buying signals](/blog/buying-signals-b2b), and one of the ways a [Pancake Play](/plays) finds people. This page covers what each kind of engagement means, who to skip, which posts to watch, five rules for the message, and openers that read as normal.

## What a reaction, a comment or a share tells you

Engagement is effort, and effort is information. When Twitter published its home-timeline ranking code in 2023, [the README listed the weight](https://github.com/twitter/the-algorithm-ml/blob/main/projects/home/recap/README.md) given to each predicted action: 0.5 for a like, 13.5 for a reply, 75 for a reply the author engages with. The weights were first set so each action contributes about equally on average, so they mostly mirror how much rarer the bigger actions are. Rarer actions take more effort, and they say more about the person.

| What they did | What it cost them | What it suggests | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reacted | One tap | They saw it and didn't scroll past | Wait for a second signal |
| Commented with praise | A sentence | A fan, a customer or a friend of the poster | Learn from it; don't pitch |
| Asked a question ("Does it work with NetSuite?") | A sentence, in public | They're weighing the category now | Your best lead |
| Complained ("Still waiting on support") | A sentence, often annoyed | Friction with the rival, or a bad day | Wait a few days, then write about the problem |
| Shared it in their own words | A post of their own | They vouch for it to their audience | Usually a customer or partner; skip for now |
| Tagged a colleague | A name in a comment | A buying group forming | Write to whoever owns the budget |

A reaction from someone who fits your profile is worth noting, not worth a message on its own. Pair it with a company signal, like a job post for the team you sell to, before you write.

## Who you'll find under a rival's post, and who to skip

Sort the names before anyone gets a message. Under a typical launch post:

- **The rival's employees, investors and agencies** engage with everything their company posts. Skip.
- **Partners and resellers** sell the rival. Skip, or treat it as a partnership talk.
- **Job seekers** crowd hiring posts. Skip.
- **Other vendors and consultants** in the space are watching too. Skip.
- **The poster's friends**, and groups that trade reactions to boost reach, are noise.
- **Current customers** chose the rival. Leave them for now, and treat them as long-term contacts: switching tends to happen around a renewal, not after one cold message.
- **People weighing the category** are your leads. They fit your profile, work somewhere you could sell to, and their engagement says something about their work.

Their title, their company and the words of their comment sort most people in under a minute.

**Run this today:** open your top rival's last three launch posts, sort every commenter into these seven groups, and count the people in the last one. That count tells you whether the signal deserves an hour a week.

## How to find people engaging with competitors: the posts to watch

Not every rival post draws buyers. These do:

- **Launches and feature announcements.** People comparing tools ask how it works.
- **Pricing changes.** Unhappy customers say so in the comments.
- **Webinars and events.** People learning the category sign up and ask questions.
- **Outages and apologies.** Friction, sometimes in public.
- **Comparison posts.** Evaluators read them and argue in the replies.

Skip customer stories, where the commenters are mostly the customer's colleagues, and hiring posts. Posts from the experts your buyers follow work the same way, with a bigger crowd and no loyalty to any vendor. For a SaaS founder with no customers yet, rivals' audiences are one of the [channels for a B2B SaaS product's first customers](/blog/how-to-get-first-customers-b2b-saas).

## Five rules for writing to a rival's audience

1. **Write about the topic, not the rival.** If the post was about onboarding, so is your question.
2. **Never mention the reaction.** "Saw you engaged with [Rival]'s post" tells them you're watching and puts the rival in their head before you. [A 2015 study in the Journal of Retailing](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/15747/) found sharp drops in click-through rates when people realized their personal information had been collected without their consent, and in its experiments, personalized ads did better when the data was collected openly. A message that quotes someone's reaction back to them makes the watching visible.
3. **Don't trash the rival.** The person may have chosen it, recommended it, or know the founder. Criticizing the choice criticizes them.
4. **If they ask how you found them, say so.** "You commented on a post about onboarding, and that's the problem we work on." Honest, short, done.
5. **Ask one question, then respect a no.** In the EU, people can object to direct marketing at any time, and the [European Commission's guidance](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/dealing-requests-individuals/what-happens-if-someone-objects-my-company-processing-their-personal-data_en) says that once someone objects, you may no longer process their data for that purpose. Treat "not interested" as final, wherever they live.

## First messages that don't creep people out

The fix is almost always the same: drop the reference to the rival and ask about the person's work.

| Reads as surveillance | Reads as a normal question |
|---|---|
| "Saw you reacted to [Rival]'s launch post. We do that, but better." | "How long does a new customer take to go live on your product today?" |
| "Noticed your comment about [Rival]'s outage. Ready to switch?" | "When a vendor in your checkout goes down, what's the plan for the next hour?" |
| "Since you follow [Rival], you'll love our tool." | "Who builds the weekly numbers at your company, and how long does it take?" |

One exception: a question asked in public. If someone wrote "does this work with NetSuite?" under a rival's post, answering it is fair, because they asked in the open: "Saw your NetSuite question on a post about AP tools. For us, the answer is yes. How are approvals handled today?" When they reply, [founder-led sales](/blog/founder-led-sales) covers the conversation from there.
