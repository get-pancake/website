**TL;DR:** Small companies hire an HR consultant when HR work arrives before an HR team does. Federal obligations change at 15, 20, 50 and 100 employees, founders post about their first HR hire or a handbook, and a funding round brings a hiring plan nobody owns yet. Reach those companies through the people already advising them (employment lawyers, benefits brokers, accountants, payroll providers), plus outreach timed to those moments. That is how to get HR consulting clients in weeks rather than quarters; content and local groups build more slowly.

This guide is for independent HR consultants and fractional HR leaders selling to companies of about 10 to 250 people. The federal rules below come from the agencies' own pages, read in November 2026. For the channels every independent consultant shares, start with [the consulting client playbook](/blog/how-to-get-consulting-clients).

---

## Two buyers: the founder with no HR team, and the HR team of one

An HR consultant sells to one of two people, and they buy differently.

- **The founder, CEO or COO at a company with no HR person.** HR lands on their desk: offer letters, a leave request, a complaint, a termination. They want the problem gone and a system in place, and they sign quickly once they trust you.
- **The lone HR generalist or office manager.** They know what's missing but can't build it alongside the daily work. They buy projects: a handbook rewrite, pay bands, a review process, help with an investigation. The budget often sits with the COO or CFO, so expect a second conversation.

Sort your last five clients by buyer type. The type that signed fastest tells you where to aim first.

## The headcount lines your prospects are about to cross

Federal employment rules change at several headcounts, and four of them create work a small company has rarely done before:

- **15 employees.** The EEOC says a business is covered by the federal laws against discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, disability or genetic information once it has [15 or more employees who worked at least 20 calendar weeks](https://www.eeoc.gov/employers/coverage-businessprivate-employers), this year or last. Work to sell: a complaint process, anti-harassment training, accommodation procedures, consistent job descriptions.
- **20 employees.** Age discrimination coverage starts at 20 employees on the same 20-week test. COBRA generally requires group health plans of [employers with 20 or more employees in the prior year](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/health-plans/cobra) to offer continuation coverage. Work to sell: COBRA notices, set up with the benefits broker, and age-neutral hiring and layoff practices.
- **50 employees.** The FMLA covers [private employers with 50 or more employees in 20 or more workweeks](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/28-fmla) in the current or previous calendar year. Separately, a company averaging at least 50 full-time employees, including full-time equivalents, during the prior year is an [applicable large employer under the ACA](https://www.irs.gov/affordable-care-act/employers/determining-if-an-employer-is-an-applicable-large-employer) for the current year. Work to sell: a leave policy and tracking, manager training, hours tracking for part-time staff, ACA reporting set up with the payroll provider.
- **100 employees.** Private employers with 100 or more employees submit an annual [EEO-1 Component 1 report](https://www.eeoc.gov/data/eeo-data-collections) of workforce data by job category, sex and race or ethnicity. Work to sell: clean job categories and demographic fields before the first filing.

These are federal lines. States set their own, and the EEOC notes that an employer outside its coverage may still be covered by a state or local anti-discrimination law.

You can see a company nearing a line from outside. Count the openings on its careers page against its team page: a 12-person company with five roles open crosses the first line once it fills them. Ask about the work, not the law: "You're at about 12 people with five roles open. Who owns the complaint process once you pass 15?"

## Eight channels, from referrals to job posts

The times are our estimates for a consultant with a few years of HR leadership behind them, not survey data.

| # | Channel | Who brings the client | First client in | Cash cost | Fits best |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Former employers and colleagues | You | 2 to 6 weeks | None | Newly independent HR leaders |
| 2 | Employment lawyers | A lawyer whose client needs the work done, beyond the advice | 1 to 3 months | Referrals you send back | Investigations, policies, terminations |
| 3 | Benefits brokers and PEO advisors | The broker, at renewal | 1 to 3 months | Your time | Leave, benefits and compliance work |
| 4 | Payroll and HR platform partners | The software vendor | 2 to 6 months | Implementation time | Consultants who set up HR systems |
| 5 | Accountants and fractional CFOs | The client's money advisor | 1 to 3 months | Referrals you send back | Companies under 100 people |
| 6 | Companies at a visible moment | An HR job post, a round, a founder's post | 3 to 8 weeks | Your time, or a tool | Fractional HR leads |
| 7 | SHRM chapters and local business groups | Peers and owners | 1 to 3 months | Dues and evenings | Practices that serve one city |
| 8 | Checklists, templates and workshops | Your own work | 3 to 12 months | Your time | Consultants with one specialty |

## How to get HR consulting clients through lawyers, brokers and accountants (channels 2 to 5)

Small employers ask their lawyer, their broker or their accountant before they ask an HR consultant. Each of those advisors needs somewhere to send the work they don't do.

- **Employment lawyers.** A lawyer advises; someone still has to write the policy, train the managers and document the process. Be that someone, and send the lawyer the questions that need counsel.
- **Benefits brokers and PEO advisors.** A broker meets each client at renewal and hears first about leave requests, COBRA notices and turnover. Offer to handle the policy side of what they sell.
- **Payroll and HR platforms.** Some run partner programs for consultants who implement and support them. Check the platforms your clients already use, and read the current terms on the vendor's own site.
- **Accountants and fractional CFOs.** They see payroll climb and the first HR problems show up in the numbers. [Fractional CFOs win clients at the same moments you do](/blog/how-to-get-fractional-cfo-clients), such as a round or a first controller hire, so trade introductions.

Start with three: one employment lawyer, one benefits broker and one accountant whose clients look like yours. Tell each, in two sentences, what you take on and what you send back to them.

## Companies at a visible moment (channel 6)

Outreach to strangers works when it starts from a moment they made public. Four are worth a message:

- **An HR manager, HR generalist or office manager opening.** The company has decided HR is a job. Offer one of three things: cover until the hire starts, the handbook and policies the new person will inherit, or coaching once they arrive. Write to the founder or COO who owns the hire.
- **Founders posting about HR work.** A post about a first HR hire, an employee handbook, performance reviews or a time-off policy names the problem in their own words.
- **A funding round.** A round brings a hiring plan, and a plan to double the team needs offer letters, onboarding, pay bands and a review process before the new people start.
- **A new state on the payroll.** A job post for a remote role in a state where the company has no staff yet means a new set of payroll, leave and notice rules to follow.

A founder's post makes the easiest opener: "Saw your post about writing the first handbook. Which policy is giving you the most trouble?" [Our page for HR consultants](/for/hr-consultants) shows these moments set up as searches.

## HR consultant marketing that founders use

HR consultant marketing works when it hands a founder a tool for this week's problem. Four that do:

- a one-page checklist of what changes at 15, 20, 50 and 100 employees;
- a fixed-price handbook review for the states they employ in;
- a manager training session for a founder group or an accelerator cohort;
- a short post, on the platforms founders read, for each question a client raised that month.

Local groups count too. SHRM's local chapters put you among HR peers who can pass on overflow, and a chamber of commerce puts you among owners. If training is a big part of your offer, [the corporate training playbook](/blog/how-to-get-corporate-training-clients) covers the buyers who sign for it.

HR consulting business development is mostly the follow-up: one question to each person who downloads the checklist, about the line they are closest to.

## Package the first engagement so it becomes a retainer

A fixed-scope first project gets a faster yes than an open retainer. Three that sell:

1. **HR foundations in 30 days:** a handbook, offer letter and termination templates, an onboarding checklist and a leave policy.
2. **Crossing 50:** an FMLA policy and tracking, manager training, and ACA reporting set up with the payroll provider.
3. **The first review cycle:** a simple performance review process, run once alongside the managers.

Give each a fixed fee and an end date. At the readout, propose the monthly retainer: a set number of hours, a named response time and a quarterly compliance check.
