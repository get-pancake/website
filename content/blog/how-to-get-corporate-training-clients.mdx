**TL;DR:** Knowing how to get corporate training clients comes down to timing: reach the person who owns the program before the need turns into an RFP. Watch for the moments that create training work, such as a batch of manager roles, a sales hiring push, a fresh funding round, a new learning platform or a compliance deadline. Then sell one paid pilot with a behavior measure, and stay close to the people who sat in your sessions, because they bring you into their next company.

## Who buys corporate training, and who signs

Every training need has an owner, and the owner is rarely the only one who signs. Match the program you sell to its owner before you write a single message.

| Program you sell | Who owns the need | Who else weighs in | Trigger to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manager and leadership training | Head of L&D or talent development | HR business partners, the VP whose managers they are | A batch of manager or team lead roles posted |
| Sales training and rep onboarding | Head of sales enablement, VP of Sales | The CRO, sales operations | SDR and account executive roles; an enablement hire |
| Compliance training | HR or People operations | Legal, and finance for the budget | Supervisor roles in a state with a mandate; a deadline |
| New-hire onboarding | L&D or People operations | Hiring managers | A hiring push after a funding round |
| Tool and platform training | The leader rolling out the tool | IT, the platform admin | A new LMS or enablement tool named in job posts |

Training teams recommend more often than they decide. In [Training magazine's 2025 Training Industry Report](https://trainingmag.com/2025-training-industry-report/), 68 percent of the training professionals surveyed recommend purchases, while 21 percent make the final purchase decision. So win the L&D leader, then hand them what they need to sell you upward: the business case in their boss's numbers, such as ramp time for new reps or turnover among first-year managers.

## Where corporate training budgets go

US training spending reached $102.8 billion in 2025, and spending on outside products and services, the line that pays training companies, rose 29 percent to $16 billion, according to the same report, which surveys US organizations with 100 or more employees. Mandatory compliance training and management or supervisory training took the biggest budget shares, at 13 percent each. IT and systems training and onboarding came next, at 11 percent each.

The forward view points the same way. Asked which areas will get more money than the year before, 30 percent of organizations named management and supervisory training, the top answer, as in each of the previous 13 years. AI training followed at 25 percent, interpersonal skills at 22 and onboarding at 21.

Two takeaways. Manager training and onboarding are the budget lines with momentum. Compliance is large but mostly digital: 92 percent of organizations deliver at least some of it online and 45 percent deliver all of it online, so a live compliance offer has to earn its price.

## Six moments that open a training budget

Training needs become visible well before anyone writes an RFP. Each of these six moments leaves a public trace, and they are the signals behind [Pancake for corporate training companies](/for/corporate-training-companies).

1. **A batch of manager roles.** Several "team lead", "engineering manager" or "store manager" posts at once mean new managers on the way, hired or promoted. They need training in their first months, and in California the law puts a clock on part of it.
2. **A sales hiring push.** SDR and account executive posts mean a class of reps to onboard. A sales enablement manager post means someone will soon own that program and want help building it.
3. **A funding round.** A round pays for a hiring plan. More hires mean more onboarding, and more teams mean more new managers.
4. **A new learning leader.** A head of L&D or L&D manager post means a budget owner is coming, with a mandate and no vendors yet. Write to the leader doing the hiring now, and to the new hire in their first month.
5. **A new learning platform.** Job posts that ask for Docebo, Cornerstone, Workday Learning, Highspot or Seismic experience, or for an LMS administrator, say a platform is in place or arriving, and someone has to fill it with content.
6. **Leaders posting about the problem.** L&D and People leaders post about onboarding, manager training and upskilling. A post about rebuilding a new-manager program is an open door for one question. So is a comment under a rival training firm's post.

Say you run a four-person firm that trains first-time managers. One week's list might hold a logistics company posting six shift supervisor roles in Ohio, a software company that raised a round in September and now lists three engineering manager openings, and a head of People who wrote about rebuilding onboarding. That is three companies, three owners and three different first questions, and none of them has issued an RFP.

[Companies hiring for a role](/plays/companies-hiring) is the search behind the first two, and [recently funded startups](/plays/recently-funded-startups) the one behind the third. For more roles and what each team tends to buy, see [job postings as buying signals](/blog/job-postings-as-buying-signals); [the full list of B2B buying signals](/blog/buying-signals-b2b) covers the rest.

## Compliance deadlines: the trigger with a date on it

Most training triggers are fuzzy. Compliance mandates come with dates. Two examples, as of November 2026:

- **California.** Employers with five or more employees must give supervisors two hours and other employees one hour of sexual harassment and abusive conduct prevention training every two years. New supervisors must be trained within six months of taking the role, and the next statewide deadline is January 1, 2027, [according to the Civil Rights Department's employer FAQ](https://calcivilrights.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/32/2022/11/Sexual-Harassment-Prevention-Training-For-Employers-FAQ_ENG.pdf).
- **New York.** Every employer must train every employee each year, and the training must be interactive, under [the state's minimum standards for this training](https://www.ny.gov/sites/default/files/atoms/old-files//StandardsSexualHarassmentPreventionTraining.pdf).

The catch: both states publish model trainings employers can use, and California's free online courses satisfy its law. So don't sell the minimum. Sell what a free course can't do: live practice for new supervisors on situations from their own workplace, with a facilitator who answers their questions.

The sales angle follows. A company with California staff that posts supervisor roles has a six-month clock on each new manager. Offer to fold the required hours into a new-manager program they would want anyway. Treat this as a sales trigger, not legal advice, and point buyers to the agency's own page.

## How to get corporate training clients: seven channels, ranked

The order reflects how fast each channel tends to produce a paid engagement for a small firm. It is our judgment, not survey data.

1. **Alumni and past sponsors.** People who went through your program change companies and bring you along. Keep a list of every sponsor and standout participant, check it monthly for new roles, and write in their first weeks with one question about the new team.
2. **Outreach on the six moments.** Write to the owner of the need, not to procurement, with one question about the trigger. It is the channel you control most: you pick the companies and the moment.
3. **Partners who meet your buyer first.** HR consultancies, executive coaches, recruiters who place managers, LMS implementation partners and event agencies that run offsites all talk to L&D and People leaders. Offer a referral fee, or co-deliver one session so they see you teach. Coaches court the same sponsors, as [this guide to winning executive coaching clients](/blog/how-to-get-executive-coaching-clients) shows.
4. **A paid diagnostic or sample session.** A 90-minute workshop for one team, priced low and credited against a full program. The sponsor sees you teach before asking for budget. Never give a full program away to win one.
5. **ATD and SHRM chapters.** Local ATD and SHRM chapters put you in a room with L&D and HR leaders. Offer to speak with one useful framework, not a pitch. National stages, such as ATD's international conference, take longer to earn.
6. **Tools L&D leaders reuse.** A new-manager checklist, a ramp-time calculator, a 30-60-90 onboarding plan. Good tools get forwarded inside companies with your name on them.
7. **RFPs and approved vendor lists.** Large companies and public bodies buy big programs through formal tenders. If the first you hear of a program is its RFP, someone else may have helped shape it. Answer the ones that fit, and work on getting onto the approved vendor list at companies you already serve.

## How to sell corporate training once they reply

Corporate training sales is a business conversation first and a curriculum conversation second. Three steps take a reply to a signed pilot:

1. **Discovery on the business problem.** Ask what would be different in six months if this worked. Ask how long new managers wait for their first training, or how long a new rep takes to close a first deal. The answers become the pilot's measure.
2. **A paid pilot with a measure agreed up front.** One cohort, one start date, one price, and one behavior you will check afterward. The [Kirkpatrick Model](https://www.kirkpatrickpartners.com/the-kirkpatrick-model/) calls this Level 3, Behavior: whether people apply the training back on the job. It recommends starting from Level 4, the business result, and working back. Good measures are concrete: new managers holding weekly one-on-ones, reps running discovery calls in the new format.
3. **Paperwork before procurement asks.** Have a one-page note on data handling, a certificate of insurance, a W-9 and your rate card ready, so a yes doesn't stall in vendor onboarding.

After the pilot, your sponsor presents the results to their boss. Make that easy: a one-page summary with the measure, quotes from participants and the cost of rolling the program out to the next cohorts. Coaches who sell to HR go through the same steps, covered in [how to sell coaching to companies](/blog/how-to-sell-coaching-to-companies).
